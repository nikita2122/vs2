                             Schedule-Aware Fan Control for
                              Brewery Wastewater Cleaning
                              Short paper on innovation 1 of the thesis


                                                  Abstract
      Aeration blowers are the main electrical load when a brewery cleans process water before dis-
      charge. The hard disturbances are not a daily sewage curve. They are clean-in-place recipes
      the production clock already names, including detergent pulses that cut oxygen transfer. This
      paper specifies a schedule-aware fan law: a one-minute inversion of the oxygen balance that
      previews the recipe, stages identical centrifugal blowers off their surge line, and falls back to a
      dissolved-oxygen loop if the optimizer stops. On a representative week the law uses 9656 kWh
      of blower electricity, 11.6 percent less than a proportional-integral loop parked at 2.5 mg/L and
      69 percent less than constant speed sized for the detergent hour, with no hours above a study
      limit of 125 mg/L chemical oxygen demand. A companion paper treats the live transfer factor.
      Here that factor is an input.


1    Introduction
Brewery wastewater is warm, strong, and scheduled. Equalization, anaerobic pretreatment, and
aerobic polishing are the usual train when the water is cleaned and discharged rather than reused
[1, 2, 3]. After anaerobic pretreatment the polishing tank is still an oxygen-transfer problem, and
the fans that solve it are typically more than half of a biological plant’s electricity [4, 5]. Published
predictive aeration controllers act on municipal benchmarks whose input is a smooth airflow or a
transfer coefficient, not a staged blower, and whose disturbance is a diurnal file [6, 7, 8, 9]. They do
not read a batch recipe.
  The contribution is the disturbance and the actuator together. The brewhouse and clean-in-place
calendar is encoded as a preview. The command is header airflow and the number of online machines,
subject to a surge margin. Inner loops and interlocks stay in the programmable controller [10, 11].
The numerical week below uses the deployed one-step law, not a mixed-integer solver. The horizon
problem is the specification that law reduces to while the machines are identical.

A clamped bias b, with |b| ≤ 0.25 and gain 0.55 h−1 on Csp − C, removes constant offset [12]. The
command is clip((1 + b)q ⋆ , 900, 9000). Staging is one machine below 2600 Nm3 /h, two up to 5200,

machines sharing 900 are not, so the optimizer must be allowed to turn machines off.
   At a quiet base point (OUR ≈ 18 mg L−1 h−1 , Csp = 1.8, T = 28◦ C, α = 0.60) the inversion
returns about 1550 Nm3 /h and about 33 kW. One machine. At fermentation conditions (OUR ≈ 30,
Csp = 2.2, T = 33◦ C, α = 0.40) it returns about 5250 Nm3 /h and about 133 kW, on the two-to-three
machine boundary. The same inversion with the quiet-week factor α = 0.55 asks for only about
3570 Nm3 /h. The missing factor is (0.55/0.40)1/0.82 ≈ 1.47. Innovation 1 can apply that factor only
if innovation 2, or a recipe table, supplies α. In this paper α is assumed known through the 0.30 h
filter used in the thesis estimate.
  If the edge computer does not publish, the programmable controller holds a proportional-integral
loop at 2.5 mg/L. Low dissolved oxygen, a drive fault, a shut outlet valve, or header over-pressure
override the set-point. The optimizer proposes. Level 1 disposes.
  The economic problem this law approximates minimizes, over a horizon, blower energy plus hinge
penalties on C below a floor and on chemical oxygen demand above 125 mg/L, plus a penalty on each
machine start. With identical machines the integer part is the rule above. With unequal machines

4    Estimate
The week is 96 h base, 30 h brewhouse, 18 h fermentation clean-in-place, 12 h packaging clean-in-
place, and 12 h weekend. Aerobic influent chemical oxygen demand runs from 160 to 460 mg/L
depending on regime. Four loops share the plant: the schedule-aware law; a predictive inversion
with α locked at 0.55 and no load preview; the 2.5 mg/L proportional-integral fallback; and constant
speed at 6552 Nm3 /h, 1.15 times a detergent design point. Constant speed draws 185 kW for the
whole week because (1) at that flow is the design point of the power map.

   Table 1 is the result. Every loop meets the study chemical-oxygen-demand limit, so the comparison
is not a compliance contest. Against constant speed the schedule-aware law saves 31151 − 9656 =
21495 kWh (69 percent). Against the proportional-integral habit it saves 1264 kWh (11.6 percent).
About half of that 11.6 percent is the 96 h base regime, where the set-point is 1.8 rather than 2.5
(3469 kWh against 4102 kWh). During fermentation clean-in-place the two use almost the same
energy (2441 against 2460 kWh). The fixed-α inversion matches the schedule-aware energy and lets
dissolved oxygen fall to 0.90 mg/L in that window, under the 1.5 mg/L operational floor. Preview
and a live α are what hold the floor at 1.63 mg/L. They are not a second large energy cut once the
set-point table exists.
  The schedule-aware minimum elsewhere is not perfect. Entering the brewhouse peak, dissolved
oxygen touches 1.39 mg/L. Entering the weekend it touches 0.86 mg/L while the weekend mean is
4.46 mg/L, because the mixing floor over-aerates after the step. Allowing σ < 1 when the previewed
load falls removes the second dip. It was not re-run. Exhaust fans on the same calendar add about
1300 kWh and do not change the ranking.


5    What to install first
The percentages are not an instruction to deploy the edge computer on day one. Constant speed
at the detergent-hour valve is the policy that wastes the 21495 kWh. Moving that header onto the
proportional-integral fallback already in the programmable controller takes most of the saving and
does not require a calendar feed. The set-point table in this paper is the next increment, and it can
run with α taken from the recipe table rather than from a sensor. Fermentation clean-in-place then
uses 0.40 instead of a quiet-week 0.55 even before any network is trained. Only after that week is
logged against the fallback should a live α close the inversion. The companion paper is that third
step. Reversing the order puts a model in the loop while the plant still has no tested fallback [11].
  A header that does not match (1) falsifies the kilowatt-hours and usually not the ranking against
constant speed, because constant speed is the same wrong map held at one flow. The ranking
against the proportional-integral loop is only twelve percent and is more fragile. The acceptance
test is therefore three statements on a production week, not a copied percentage: blower energy
below the fallback on the same calendar, hours above the site permit not higher than the fallback,

and the dissolved-oxygen minimum during each detergent recipe above the floor the site has written
down. In this study that floor is 1.5 mg/L. The 11.6 and 69 percent figures are the output of one
integrator. The three statements are the result that travels.


6    Conclusion
A brewery fan controller should read the production clock and the blower map, not only a dissolved-
oxygen error. On the representative polishing week, schedule-aware set-points and a surge-safe
staging rule cut about twelve percent of blower electricity relative to a competent loop at 2.5 mg/L,
and about two thirds relative to constant speed sized for the detergent hour, without adding hours
above 125 mg/L chemical oxygen demand. The same week shows the limit of the claim: energy
does not fall much further once a predictive law already uses a sensible set-point, unless the transfer
factor is wrong, in which case the loss is dissolved-oxygen margin rather than kilowatt-hours. That
margin is the subject of the companion paper. The figures are an integration of a stated model, not
a trial on a named factory.
