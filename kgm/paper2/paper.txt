                  A Physics-Informed Oxygen-Transfer Sensor
                          for Brewery Aeration Fans
                             Short paper on innovation 2 of the thesis


                                                 Abstract
     Detergents from brewery clean-in-place lower the oxygen-transfer factor α that aeration con-
     trollers usually freeze. A dissolved-oxygen probe cannot separate that drop from a change in
     oxygen uptake: both make the reading fall, and a fan loop adds air for both. This paper specifies
     a soft sensor whose outputs are α, the transfer coefficient, and the uptake, trained with the oxy-
     gen balance and with the blower power map, not only with the probe. A health bit returns the
     fan law to a recipe table when the residual is bad. On a representative detergent window, using
     the lagged true factor instead of a quiet-week value of 0.55 raises the dissolved-oxygen minimum
     from 0.90 to 1.63 mg/L. An open-loop step with uptake held fixed falls to about 0.53 mg/L in
     0.8 h if the wrong factor is used. The sensor is justified by that floor, not by a further large cut
     in kilowatt-hours once the set-point is already reasonable.


1    Introduction
Fine-bubble aeration is sensitive to surfactants. The process-water transfer rate over the clean-
water rate, the factor α, falls when detergents occupy the bubble interface, and the fall is sharper
for fine bubbles than for coarse ones [1, 2]. Brewery clean-in-place liquors are a surfactant source by
design [3, 4, 5]. Municipal soft sensors and recent physics-informed dissolved-oxygen models forecast
concentration [6, 7, 8]. They do not emit α into a fan command. Off-gas hoods measure transfer
directly and remain the reference [9, 10]. They are a poor assumption as the only signal at the
moment a recipe arrives.
   This paper defines the sensor as useful only if a fan law can consume it. The companion paper
writes that law. Here the law is the customer: each minute it inverts an oxygen-transfer curve that
is proportional to α. A forecast of dissolved oxygen that hides a wrong α inside a wrong uptake
authorizes the wrong airflow.

What remains is a flat direction when q and the detergent flag never move. The estimator must

  At a detergent point KL a = 6.50 h−1 , α = 0.40, and driving force 4.60 mg/L, the sensitivity

30 mg L−1 h−1 uptake. That is the budget a sensor is asked to return.


3     Sensor
The deployed map reads a thirty-minute window at one-minute samples: airflow, power, header
pressure, temperature, influent flow, dissolved oxygen, and the detergent flag. Thirty minutes covers
several blower lags and does not cover a whole clean-in-place, so the network cannot memorize
a regime instead of using the flag. Two hidden layers of 32 units feed four heads. The α head
is squashed into (0.15, 0.90). The transfer and uptake heads are positive. A fourth head predicts
dissolved oxygen one step ahead and is not given to the controller. Publishing a second concentration
next to a real probe invites an unaudited average.

The derivative Ċ is a smoothed probe slope, not another network. The power residual stops a
claimed airflow from disagreeing with the meter. The curve residual is the term that removes (1)’s
free direction. Training raises λ only after a data-only stage has fitted the probe, because the two
gradients otherwise conflict [13, 7]. The bound term penalizes α moving faster than 0.5 per hour
unless the detergent flag has changed. Diffuser wear is slow. A recipe is not.
   The health bit is cleared if α leaves [0.15, 0.90], if L on the physics terms exceeds a threshold set
from a commissioning week that includes one detergent day, or if the uptake head moves faster than
the kinetic ceiling. A cleared bit does not stop the fans. The controller loads α from the recipe table.
For fermentation clean-in-place that table value is 0.40, already closer to the truth than 0.55. A
stuck probe freezes airflow rather than integrating a bias against a dead reading [14]. A lost calendar
keeps the sensor and drops the preview. Those degradations are the interface.
  Normalization is frozen at commissioning: airflow over 2500 Nm3 /h, power over 80 kW, header
pressure relative to 52 kPa, temperature relative to 30◦ C. An online mean would shift when the
detergent arrives and would hide the event. Inference is a small forward pass. If it is late, the
previous α is kept. A fan command does not wait on a network timeout.
    In the estimate reported below, the “sensor” is not one training seed. It is the true regime α,

filtered with a 0.30 h lag and a two-percent caution. That is an upper bound on what a good sensor
is worth. A network whose α error exceeds that filtered truth will do worse. Commissioning still
needs an off-gas or portable probe on a detergent day [10, 15]. The threshold that never fires, and
the threshold fit only on a quiet week, are both wrong. The first removes the fallback. The second
declares every detergent day a fault and ignores the sensor exactly when it is needed.


4    What the factor changes
Two airflows are frozen after a detergent step, with uptake held at 30 mg L−1 h−1 , T = 33◦ C, and
βC ∗ = 6.80. The short airflow, 3570 Nm3 /h, is the inversion of (3) at α = 0.55. The corrected
airflow, 5250 Nm3 /h, uses α = 0.40. Dissolved oxygen starts at 1.80 mg/L. The semi-implicit step
of the oxygen balance at 0.1 h, with uptake not allowed to fall, gives Table 1.

                    Time (h)   q = 3570 (α believed 0.55)   q = 5250 (true α = 0.40)
                       0.0                1.80                        1.80
                       0.2                1.08                        2.05
                       0.4                0.75                        2.14
                       0.8                0.53                        2.18


   By 0.3 h the wrong-factor column is already under 1 mg/L. The corrected column settles near
the 2.2 mg/L set-point. Feedback in the full week does not reach 0.53 mg/L, because uptake falls
with dissolved oxygen and because an integral bias adds air. The week minimum with α locked at
0.55 is 0.90 mg/L. With the lagged true factor it is 1.63 mg/L. The operational floor used in the
study is 1.5 mg/L. The sensor is the difference between those two minima. Blower energy of the two
predictive laws over the whole week is 9620 kWh against 9656 kWh. Inside the 18 h fermentation
window the live factor costs about 255 kWh relative to the locked factor (2441 against 2186 kWh)
and buys the floor back. A report that ranks the locked factor first on kilowatt-hours in that window
is ranking a loop that crossed the floor.
  A proportional-integral loop at a fixed 2.5 mg/L also stays above the floor in that window (min-
imum 1.87 mg/L) and spends the difference in the quiet hours. The sensor does not replace that
loop. It tells a set-point-aware inverter which α to use so the set-point is reachable when the recipe,
not the probe, is the first sign of detergent [16, 17].


5    Commissioning check
The threshold ϵ and the recipe-table value of α are identified on a day when the proportional-integral
fallback is still in charge and the sensor is only logging. The day has to include a real detergent
recipe. A threshold fit on a quiet week marks the detergent day as a fault, and the fan law then
ignores α at the only hour Table 1 says it matters. A threshold so loose that the health bit never
clears deletes the fallback to the recipe table. The logged residual on that detergent day, compared
with a portable probe or an off-gas hood, is the quantile [10, 9].
  Two other checks stop a good dissolved-oxygen forecast from being mistaken for a good α. First,
the curve residual in (5) is inspected on the same day. If the emitted KL a and the curve at the
emitted α disagree by more than about fifteen percent, the health bit is treated as clear even if the
network set it. Second, the fan inversion is evaluated twice on the logged airflow: once with the
emitted α and once with 0.55. The gap should be of the order of the 3570 against 5250 Nm3 /h in
Table 1 when the recipe is the fermentation clean. If the two inversions agree, the sensor has not

moved α, and the network is not yet doing the job this paper defined. Agreement of the one-step
dissolved-oxygen head with the probe is not a substitute for that gap. Equation (1) can be fitted by
moving uptake alone.
  The sensor is installed last. Constant-speed headers move to a dissolved-oxygen loop first. The
recipe clock and the set-point table come second, with α from the table. The network closes the
inversion only after the detergent-day log exists. That order is forced by the numbers: the large
energy cut does not need the network, and the dissolved-oxygen floor during detergent does not
survive a wrong α.


6    Conclusion
The oxygen-transfer factor during brewery clean-in-place is a measured disturbance, not a calibrated
constant. A small residual network can emit it from fan power, airflow, and a dissolved-oxygen probe
if the clean-water curve is enforced, so that uptake cannot trade off against α. On the representative
window, replacing a quiet-week factor of 0.55 with the lagged true factor of about 0.40 lifts the
dissolved-oxygen minimum from 0.90 to 1.63 mg/L at essentially the same weekly blower energy.
The open-loop column shows why the weekly minimum is the mitigated error, not the raw one. The
result is an integration with a filtered true factor standing in for a commissioned sensor. It is not
a field residual, and it is not a claim that the sensor saves a further large fraction of fan electricity
once the airflow set-point is already right.
