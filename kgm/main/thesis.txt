                                         Abstract


A brewery uses large volumes of water for brewing, fermentation, and packaging, and

then has to clean that water before it is discharged. The cleaning plant is an industrial

automation problem. Screens, equalization, pH correction, anaerobic pretreatment, aerobic

polishing, and clarification must all run while the brewery itself changes batches. The

actuators that dominate the electricity bill are fans: centrifugal blowers that push air into

the aerobic tanks, and smaller exhaust fans that keep the equalization and flotation rooms

from going septic and odorous. In municipal plants the influent drifts with a daily pattern.

In a brewery the dangerous disturbances are scheduled. A clean-in-place cycle dumps hot,

surfactant-rich, high-load water at a time the production program already knows.

  This thesis formulates two technological responses to that fact. The first is a schedule-

aware hybrid economic model predictive controller for the fan cluster. It treats the

brewhouse and clean-in-place calendar as a measured future disturbance, chooses how many

blowers are online, and chooses their speeds under surge, motor, and discharge constraints.

The second is a physics-informed oxygen-transfer soft sensor. Cleaning surfactants lower

the oxygen-transfer factor that municipal controllers usually freeze at a constant. The

soft sensor estimates that factor, together with the oxygen uptake of the sludge, from fan

power, airflow, temperature, and a sparse dissolved-oxygen measurement, and it writes the

estimate into the controller model.

  The two ideas are developed against the published record on aeration control, blower

thermodynamics, soft sensing, and physics-informed learning, and they are then specified

as an automation architecture: a programmable controller for safety and inner loops, an

edge computer for the optimizer and the soft sensor, and a historian fed by the brewery

schedule. A reduced aerobic polishing model, integrated over a representative production

week, is used to estimate performance against constant-speed fans, a dissolved-oxygen

proportional-integral loop, and a tracking predictive law that does not see the schedule
or the surfactant factor. On that week the schedule-aware law uses about twelve percent

less blower electricity than the proportional-integral loop and about sixty-nine percent less

than constant-speed operation, while the weekly hours above the study discharge limit

for chemical oxygen demand stay at zero. The same run shows why the soft sensor is

not redundant: without it, the predictive law lets dissolved oxygen sag further when the

surfactant arrives. The study is a design and simulation argument for a representative

train. It is not a claim about a named factory.

Chapter 1


Previous Work and the Problem Set

A brewery wastewater train is a computer-controlled plant whose product is a legal discharge,

not beer. The beer has already been made. What remains is the water that touched the

product and the equipment: wort losses, yeast, spent filter aid, and, in sharp pulses, the

acid, caustic, and detergent from clean-in-place. The plant that receives this water must

remove organic load and solids and then release the water. Fans sit in the middle of that

duty. Blowers supply the oxygen without which the aerobic biomass stops, and exhaust

fans keep equalization and flotation from turning anaerobic and odorous. This chapter

reviews what is already known about those two technical problems, fan control under a

predictive law and oxygen-transfer sensing under a changing wastewater matrix, and then

writes the problem this thesis actually solves. The review is selective. It keeps the results

that constrain a brewery design and drops municipal detail that does not survive contact

with a batch production calendar.



1.1      Research on predictive control of aeration fans

1.1.1     Where the electricity goes

Aeration is the largest electrical load in a conventional biological treatment plant. Long-

term plant audits put the share of aeration in the range of roughly half to three quarters


                                             10
of the site electricity, with the exact fraction depending on whether sludge handling and

pumping are large [1, 2, 3]. The mechanism is not subtle. Oxygen is only sparingly

soluble, the saturation concentration in warm water is near 8 mg/L, and the biomass can

consume oxygen faster than diffusion across a quiescent surface can replace it. Machines

are therefore used to create interfacial area. In a diffused-air tank the machines are fans in

the engineering sense: centrifugal or turbo blowers that raise air from atmospheric pressure

to the header pressure set by submergence, diffuser loss, and pipe friction [4, 5, 6].

  Brewery wastewater makes the same machines work harder for three reasons that the

municipal literature treats as side cases. The water is warm, often above 30 °C, so saturation

is lower and the driving force C ∗ − C shrinks [7, 8]. The organic load per cubic metre is

high, so the oxygen uptake per cubic metre is high even after anaerobic pretreatment [9,

10, 11]. And the clean-in-place chemicals are surfactants. Surfactants are the classical

reason the field transfer rate falls below the clean-water test [12, 4]. A controller that was

tuned on a municipal diurnal curve, with a constant transfer factor, is therefore solving a

different disturbance problem from the one a brewery automation system actually has.

  The standard description of the transfer rate is the two-film expression used in design

manuals and in the ASCE clean-water test [13, 14, 15]. In the mixed liquor,

  A second, easily missed, fan duty sits upstream of the aerobic tank. Equalization of

optimizer is written. This thesis keeps them in the architecture and in the energy account,

and it does not pretend they are the main optimization variable.

  Staging is the hybrid part of the problem. Three blowers on one header, which is a

common small-plant arrangement, give a discrete decision n ∈ {1, 2, 3} and a continuous

speed for the machines that are on. Running three machines at low speed can cross the

surge line of each machine even when the header flow is moderate. Running one machine at

high speed can meet the same header flow at a better surge margin and a better efficiency,

until that machine hits its motor limit and a second machine must start. The start itself

has a cost: inrush, a pressure bump in the header, and wear. Hybrid model predictive

control was built for exactly this mixture of logic and continuous dynamics [20]. Cristea

and co-workers used a hybrid nonlinear predictive law on an aeration problem and showed

that the on–off structure of the air supply can be carried inside the optimizer rather than

left to a separate sequencer [21]. What that work does not carry is a brewery calendar,

and what it does not protect explicitly is the surge margin of a centrifugal map. Both

omissions matter once the fans are real machines rather than a smooth airflow input.



1.1.3    From on–off dissolved-oxygen control to predictive laws

The control literature on aeration is large and, in its later part, repetitive. The review

by Åmand, Olsson, and Carlsson is the clean map of the older half: manual valves, time

clocks, on–off bands around a dissolved-oxygen sensor, then proportional-integral control

of airflow or of blower pressure, then cascade structures in which an outer dissolved-oxygen

loop writes an airflow set-point to an inner flow loop [3, 22, 23]. Cascade is the structure



                                            13
still shipped with most blower panels. It is the right inner loop. It is a poor outer loop

when the load jumps faster than the oxygen sensor and the blower can settle, and when

the set-point itself is a number an operator picked once and then left alone.

  A standard discrete proportional-integral law for the outer loop is

with anti-windup on the sum [24, 25, 26]. The bias qbias is where the fan sits when the error

is zero. If the bias is wrong, the integral removes the offset, slowly. During a clean-in-place

slug the integral is still carrying the bias from the previous hour. The fan is late on the

way up and late on the way down. That pattern, not the gain formula, is the operational

defect.

  Ammonia-based aeration control moves the outer set-point. Instead of holding a fixed

dissolved-oxygen number, a slower loop raises or lowers Csp so that effluent ammonia stays

near a low target, which avoids aerating for nitrification the plant does not need [27, 28,

29]. The idea is right for municipal nitrogen permits. A brewery polishing tank is often

carbon-limited, and the permit that bites is chemical oxygen demand, biochemical oxygen

demand, and solids. Ammonia feedback is still useful as a floor: if dissolved oxygen is

allowed to fall so far that nitrification stops, ammonia walks out of the tank even when

the carbon looks acceptable. Rieger and co-workers also make a point this thesis uses.

Feedforward from influent ammonia helps when the load swing is unusual. When the swing

is ordinary, feedback is enough [27]. A brewery clean-in-place dump is the unusual swing,

and it is unusual on a clock the factory already owns.

  Model predictive control enters the aeration literature as a way to use a dynamic model

instead of a fixed set-point. Holenda and co-workers applied a predictive law to dissolved

oxygen on an activated-sludge model and showed tighter control than a linear regulator

on the same plant model [30]. Shen, Chen, and Corriou put predictive control on the

BSM1 benchmark and compared it with the default proportional-integral strategy of that



                                              14
benchmark [31, 32, 33]. Ostace, Cristea, and Agachi used a modified ASM1 inside a

predictive controller aimed at operating cost [34, 35, 36]. Vega and co-workers separated

set-point optimization from the tracking predictive layer, which is the classical two-layer

pattern of process control [37, 38, 39]. Zeng and Liu formulated an economic predictive

controller directly on BSM1, so that effluent quality and operating cost enter the stage

cost instead of entering only through a set-point chosen elsewhere [40, 41]. Revollar and

co-workers compared economic predictive strategies on the same family of plants [42].

Santín, Pedret, and Vilanova showed that a variable dissolved-oxygen set-point, computed

at a higher layer, removes effluent violations that a fixed set-point leaves behind, and they

compared fuzzy and predictive configurations for that job [43, 44].

the right language for a comparison. They are the wrong disturbance. BSM1 influent is a

municipal file with a daily and weekly shape. It does not contain a surfactant pulse tied to

a clean-in-place recipe, and its aeration input is an oxygen-transfer coefficient, not a fan

with a surge line [49].

  Hierarchical predictive control, as in the work of Brdys and of Piotrowski, already splits

the problem in the way a real plant must be split: a slow optimizer writes set-points, and

fast loops move valves and machines [50, 51]. O’Brien and co-workers report a plant trial

of predictive aeration control and record the practical issues, among them model error

and the need to keep the existing safety interlocks [52]. Iratni and Chang survey the later

                                             15
control literature and list the same open issues: sensors, model mismatch, and the gap

between a benchmark file and a plant that an operator will leave in automatic [53]. Stare

and co-workers compared nitrogen-removal strategies on operating cost and showed that

the ranking of controllers depends on the price of electricity and on the effluent weights,

which is a warning against quoting a single percentage as if it were a property of the

algorithm [54].

  The predictive-control textbook results that this thesis relies on are standard and

should be named as such. Offset-free tracking needs a disturbance model or an integrator,

otherwise a constant model error becomes a constant dissolved-oxygen error [55, 56, 57].

Recursive feasibility and stability of a constrained predictive law need a terminal cost or

a terminal set, or a shorter argument that the problem solved online is a descent on a

control Lyapunov function [58, 59, 60, 61]. Real-time solution of the resulting nonlinear

program is a software problem that is now routine at the time scales of aeration, which are

minutes, not milliseconds [62, 63, 64, 65, 66]. None of that machinery, by itself, knows that

a brewery will dump a fermenter in forty minutes.

1.1.4     What the fan-control literature does not give a brewery

The gap can be stated without a new slogan. Published predictive aeration controllers,

1.2.1     Why a dissolved-oxygen probe is not an oxygen-transfer

          measurement

A dissolved-oxygen probe reports C in (1.7). It does not report KL a, α, or OUR. Those

three quantities appear only as a product and a difference. Rearrangement of a steady

balance, ignoring the small advective term, gives


                                 KL a (C∞
                                        ∗
                                          − C) − OUR ≈ 0.                                   (1.8)


Any pair (KL a, OUR) on that line agrees with the same C. A single probe, at a single

airflow, cannot separate a surfactant event, which lowers KL a, from a load event, which

raises OUR. Both make C fall, and a proportional-integral fan loop responds to both by

adding air. If the cause was surfactant, extra air is partly right. If the probe itself has

fouled and reads low, extra air is waste. Rieger, Alex, and co-workers documented, early,

that sensor properties and not only control laws decide whether automatic aeration is

trustworthy [70, 22].

  The direct experimental answer is off-gas testing. The oxygen mole fraction in the gas

leaving the tank, compared with the air in, gives the oxygen actually transferred, and

hence a field KL a and a field efficiency, without assuming the biological rate [71, 72, 13].



                                               17
Leu, Rosso, Larson, and Stenstrom showed that real-time efficiency monitoring of this kind

can be used to cut aeration energy, because the operator stops paying for transfer that the

diffuser grid is no longer delivering [72, 5]. Off-gas hoods are the reference measurement.

They are also equipment: hoods, gas sensors, and a maintenance task. A brewery polishing

tank can justify them as a calibration tool. It is a weak assumption that every tank, every

week, will have a clean hood signal at the moment a clean-in-place slug arrives. A soft

sensor that runs on the fan instruments the plant already owns is the practical companion,

checked against off-gas when the hood is in service.



1.2.2     The surfactant factor

The α factor is the ratio of process-water to clean-water mass transfer under otherwise

matched conditions. Design values near 0.6 are common for municipal fine-bubble tanks.

Rosso and Stenstrom showed that surfactants depress α sharply, and that the depression is

worse for fine bubbles than for coarse bubbles because the surfactant occupies the interface

that fine bubbles were built to maximize [12]. Viscosity and high mixed-liquor solids

depress transfer again, which is why high-solids tanks need their own correction [73, 4].

Brewery clean-in-place liquors are a surfactant source by design. The detergents that

remove beer stone and biofilm are the same class of molecules that sit on an air bubble [68,

69, 7]. In addition, brewery waste is warm and can carry diatomaceous earth fines from

filtration, which abrade and clog diffuser pores and therefore move k0 in (1.2) as well as α

[8].

   A constant α, identified during a quiet week, is then not a conservative design choice. It

is an optimistic one. The fan controller that uses it will under-predict the airflow required

during the detergent hour and will over-predict it afterward, if the identification is ever

updated from that hour alone. The literature already knows the chemistry. It does not, in

the control papers cited in Section 1.1, close the loop from a live α estimate back into a

fan optimizer on a brewery schedule.

1.2.3     Soft sensors and what they have been asked to estimate

Data-derived soft sensors for treatment plants are reviewed by Haimi, Mulas, Corona,

and Vahala [74]. The typical target is an effluent concentration that is measured in the

laboratory a few times a day: chemical oxygen demand, total nitrogen, or suspended solids.

The typical inputs are the online sensors the plant already has. Dürrenmatt and Gujer

compared data-driven model classes for the same operational purpose and stressed that a

model which fits a historical record can still be useless to an operator if its inputs are not

the decisions the operator can move [75]. Newhart, Holloway, Hering, and Cath review

performance analysis of plants from operating data, and Corominas and co-workers review

the step from data to an operational decision [76, 77]. The shared warning is worth keeping.

A soft sensor that is accurate on yesterday’s file and silent about its own physics will be

believed for a month and then bypassed.

  For dissolved oxygen specifically, the prediction problem is easier than effluent chemical

oxygen demand, because the probe exists and is simply late, noisy, or drifting. Koksal and

Aydin report a hybrid of transfer learning and physics-informed modelling for dissolved-

oxygen prediction on an industrial plant, using a simulator as one source of knowledge and

another plant as a second source [78, 79]. That paper is the closest published neighbour of

the second technology here. It improves a dissolved-oxygen forecast. It does not extract α

as a variable to be handed to a fan optimizer, and it does not use a production schedule as

an input. Those two uses are the difference this thesis claims.

  Classical identification would write a small parameter vector θ = (α, k0 ) and a state

that includes C and a slowly varying oxygen uptake, and would run a recursive estimator

[80, 81, 82]. Moving-horizon estimation is the constrained version of that idea and matches

the constraints already present in predictive control [82, 56]. It is the right tool when the

parameter dimension is small and the model is trusted. It is brittle when the airflow-to-

KL a exponent, the probe bias, and a sudden surfactant step are all wrong together. A

physics-informed network is used here not because neural networks are newer, but because

the residual of (1.7) can be imposed on a function approximator that also accepts the


                                             19
schedule features a low-order parameter vector does not have room for.



1.2.4     Physics-informed models

A physics-informed neural network trains a differentiable function uϕ (x) so that a differential

residual, not only a data error, is small [83, 84]. For a scalar residual r(x, uϕ , ∂uϕ ), the

training objective has the shape


                        L(ϕ) = Ldata (ϕ) + λ Lphys (ϕ) + λb Lbound (ϕ).                    (1.9)


Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis demonstrated the pattern on forward and inverse

partial differential equations [83]. Karniadakis and co-workers review the wider programme

of physics-informed machine learning [84]. The training difficulties are now well described.

Gradient conflict between the data term and the residual term produces models that fit

the probe and ignore the balance, or the reverse [85, 86]. Libraries such as DeepXDE make

the residual form easy to express [87]. None of that work is a wastewater paper. The

transferrable part is the residual. Here the residual is not a partial differential equation on

a spatial mesh. It is the ordinary balance (1.7), evaluated on the samples the historian

already stores, plus algebraic residuals from the fan map (1.5) so that a claimed airflow

has to agree with the measured power.

  Neural differential equations are a second, related form: the network defines a vector

field and an integrator produces the trajectory [88, 89, 90]. They are attractive when the

state must be rolled forward over a long horizon. They are heavier than this problem

needs at inference time. The fan controller runs on an edge computer on a cycle of one to

five minutes. A small residual network that emits α, KL a, and OUR in one shot, trained

with (1.9), meets that cycle. The long short-term memory network remains relevant as a

baseline for the data term, which is why it is cited, and it is not the model this thesis asks

the operator to trust [89].

  Identifiability still has to be argued, because a network can hide the non-uniqueness of


                                              20
(1.8) inside its weights. The argument used in Chapter 2 is structural. Airflow and power

fix a delivered q through (1.5). The clean-water curve (1.2) then fixes KL a up to α. The

oxygen balance fixes OUR once KL a and C are known. The schedule feature, which is

zero in quiet hours and one during a detergent recipe, gives α a reason to move that is not

collinear with a smooth diurnal uptake. When that feature is constant for a long time, the

information matrix thins, and the estimator must hold α rather than chase noise. That is

a standard excitation requirement [80], applied to a plant whose excitation is partly chosen

by the production department.



1.2.5     What the sensing literature does not give the fan controller

Off-gas is accurate and scarce. Black-box soft sensors are available and unconstrained by

(1.1). Physics-informed dissolved-oxygen models now exist for industrial wastewater [78],

and they stop at the forecast of C. The missing instrument is a soft sensor whose outputs

are the parameters of the fan problem: a live α, a live KL a, and a live uptake, with a

residual that tells the controller when the dissolved-oxygen probe should not be believed.

That is the second technology. It is useful only if the first technology has a place to put

the numbers. The two reviews are therefore one design.

1.3.4    Questions the rest of the thesis has to answer

The design questions are the following.


1. Can the production calendar be encoded as a disturbance trajectory that a fan optimizer

   accepts without a human redrawing set-points at the start of each clean-in-place?

2. Can the blower staging, the surge margin, and the header power model sit inside that

   optimizer rather than in a separate sequencer that fights it?

3. Can α during a detergent window be estimated from instruments the fan already has,

   with the oxygen balance enforced, well enough that the optimizer stops using the

   quiet-week value?

4. On the representative week of Table 1.2, how much blower energy does the combined law

   use relative to constant speed, to a competent dissolved-oxygen proportional-integral

   loop at a fixed set-point of 2.5 mg/L, and to a predictive inversion that keeps α at 0.55

   and does not read the calendar?

5. Which of those savings survive the mixing floor, the blower lag, and a soft-sensor lag of

   a few tenths of an hour?


  The questions are system questions. They are answered by a specification in Chapter 2

and by an integration of (1.13)–(1.16) in Chapter 3. They are not answered by a claim of

a field trial. A field trial would need a brewery, a permit, and an off-gas campaign this

document does not contain.



1.3.5    Assumptions that bound the problem

The assumptions are stated so they can be attacked. Solids inventory X is constant over the

week. The clarifier does not limit the organic result. Anaerobic pretreatment has already

happened, and its upsets appear only as the influent columns of Table 1.2. The production

calendar is known forty-five minutes ahead with the correct recipe identity. A wrong recipe

                                            27
is a different experiment and is named as such in the estimation. The dissolved-oxygen

probe is biased only within the lag model, except in the fault discussion of the soft sensor,

where a stuck probe is considered separately. Electricity price is constant inside the week,

so economic optimization coincides with energy minimization plus constraint penalties.

Ambient air density is fixed at the inlet temperature used in (1.5). Exhaust fans follow

a speed schedule tied to the same regimes and are metered, but they are not decision

variables of P1. Their energy is reported so the fan account is complete.

  Under those assumptions the plant is controllable in the limited sense that there exist

airflow trajectories, inside the header capacity, that keep the study chemical-oxygen-demand

limit on every regime of Table 1.2 if dissolved oxygen is held near the set-points of Chapter 2.

The constant-speed policy that meets the worst regime also meets the limit, at a power

that the quiet hours do not need. The engineering problem is the difference between those

two facts.

driving force widens or the controller adds air. If a detergent moves α from 0.60 to 0.40

and nothing else changes, the transfer rate falls by the same ratio, to 16.4 × 0.40/0.60 =

10.9 mg L−1 h−1 . The missing 5.5 mg L−1 h−1 is the entire local effect of the surfactant. No

later controller creates oxygen that this product does not allow. Controllers only choose

the airflow that sets KL a20 , and they only choose it well if they know which α to use.

  The same point fixes a bound that is easy to violate in a soft sensor that is trained

only on dissolved oxygen. Suppose the network reports α = 0.60 during the detergent and

lowers the uptake by 5.5 mg L−1 h−1 to keep the balance. The dissolved-oxygen forecast can

still be accurate. The fan command, which needs α, will be wrong by the factor computed

in Section 2.4. Accuracy on C is not accuracy on the actuator. That is why problem P2

asks for α, KL a, and uptake as separate outputs, and why the curve residual in the sensor



                                               30
loss is not an optional regularizer. It is the constraint that stops a good forecast from

authorizing a bad fan command.

  The example also bounds the exhaust fans, so they are not forgotten in a chapter

about blowers. Two exhaust machines at 7.5 kW each, running at a speed fraction of 0.75,

draw 15 × 0.753 = 6.3 kW. Over the ninety-six hours of base production that is about

610 kW h. The aerobic blower at the same hours, once it is allowed to leave constant

speed, is several thousand kilowatt-hours. The exhaust fans matter for odor and for the

automation interlock. They do not change which blower law is worth writing. The problem

set is therefore intentionally asymmetric: both fan duties are in the plant, and only the

blowers are in P1.

2.1.8     What is proved and what is only specified

A full Lyapunov argument for the hybrid economic law on (1.13)–(1.16) is not claimed.

The ingredients that make the standard argument go through are present and should be

named so a later proof has a target. The stage cost is positive definite in the fan power

and positive semi-definite in the hinges. The hinges are zero in a set that the inversion

can reach, by the feasibility check of Section 1.3. The bias (2.10) is a stable linear filter

because Kb ∆t < 1. The blower lag is a stable linear filter. The integer rule is constant

between regime boundaries, so inside a regime the closed loop is a continuous inversion

plus two stable filters, which is the class covered by offset-free predictive control with a


                                             40
short horizon [58, 55, 56]. At a regime boundary the state does not jump. The command

jumps by a bounded amount because of the clip in (2.11) and the clip on q. Bounded

jumps of a command into a stable lag do not destabilize the lag. What is missing for a

theorem is a terminal cost for the full integer horizon problem and a Lipschitz argument

for the Monod vector field under the surge constraint. Both are standard and neither is

numerically required for the estimation, which integrates the deployed law rather than

appealing to a stability certificate.

  The properties the deployed law is required to have, and that the estimation checks,

are weaker and operational. Inside a regime, dissolved oxygen must sit near Csp once the

lags have settled, except where the mixing floor forces more air than the uptake needs.

Across a clean-in-place boundary, the minimum dissolved oxygen must stay above the

floor of 1.5 mg/L in the nominal calendar. Airflow must respect qmin , qmax , and (2.6). If

the soft sensor is marked unhealthy, the law must remain defined, using αtable . Those are

acceptance tests, not a proof.

and Ċ = 0, the first residual in (2.20) is zero. At the detergent point, suppose the network

keeps the old α = 0.60 while the true value is 0.40. The curve then overstates KL a by

0.60/0.40 = 1.5. The predicted transfer rate is one and a half times the true rate. The

residual is about half of the true uptake, which on the clean-in-place window is tens of

milligrams per litre per hour, far above probe noise. The loss therefore has a reason to

move α down, provided the detergent flag or the power measurement stops the network

from instead inventing a matching drop in uptake. The curve residual is what stops that

invention. This arithmetic is the reason the third term in (2.20) is not optional.



2.2.6     Failure modes the sensor must declare

Three failures are specified, and each has a controller response that does not require a new

optimization.

1. The health bit clears because the residual is large. The controller loads α from the

   regime table and continues. Energy optimality is weaker. The plant remains defined.

2. The probe is declared stuck by the uptake bound. The controller freezes q at the last

   healthy value and raises an alarm. Freezing is safer than integrating (2.10) against

   a dead measurement. An operator can force the proportional-integral loop, which

   remains coded in the programmable controller.

3. The calendar feed is lost. σ in (2.11) is set to 1 and Csp stays on the current regime.

   The sensor continues. The controller becomes the fixed-α predictive inversion if the

   sensor also fails, and it becomes a schedule-blind but α-aware inversion if only the

   calendar fails. Those degradations are ordered on purpose. Losing α during detergent

   is more expensive than losing a preview of a load the probe will eventually see.

2.3.4     Sensor cycle and health rule

The soft sensor’s training loss was specified in (2.20). The deployed cycle is a smaller object

  The blocked horizon uses this by letting q0 jump when the calendar flips δCIP , and by

disturbance model is the recipe clock plus α from the residual (2.20). Remove either one

and (2.38) is a competent tracking problem of a kind the literature already contains [30,

2.4.6     What the edge computer must log in that hour

The blocked hour is also an audit object. For each publish the log line has to be recon-

structible from the formulae, otherwise a later disagreement with the meter cannot be

assigned to a cause. The minimum line is: time, regime identifier, detergent flag, α used

and whether it came from the sensor or the table, health bit, dissolved-oxygen measurement,

set-point, load scale σ, inverted airflow before the bias, bias, commanded airflow, staging

n, and a saturation bit if the command met qmin or qmax . That is fourteen fields. None of

them is a weight inside a network. An operator can read σ > 1 together with a detergent

flag and know the calendar, not the probe, asked for air. An operator can read a cleared

health bit and know the table value was substituted. The estimation in Chapter 3 is a

week of those lines, reduced to regime sums. A site that logs only dissolved oxygen and

power can still compute kilowatt-hours. It cannot tell a calendar failure from a sensor

failure from a drive limit, which are the three degradations in Table 2.1. The fourteen

fields are therefore part of the fan controller, in the same sense that the surge inequality is

part of it. A controller that cannot explain its last hour is not finished, however small its

System Build and Estimation

The specification in Chapter 2 is not yet a plant. This chapter places it in an automation

architecture and then estimates its behaviour by integrating the model of Section 1.3

over the week of Table 1.2. The architecture is buildable with equipment that a brewery

treatment room already uses: a programmable controller for interlocks and inner loops,

drives on the fans, a historian, and an edge computer for the minute-scale law and the

soft sensor. The estimate is a simulation of that representative train. Absolute kilowatt-

hours belong to the parameter set written in Chapter 1. The comparisons belong to the

information each controller is allowed to use.

3.1.3     Interlocks that override the optimizer

The programmable controller enforces the following regardless of the set-point it has just

received. A blower may not start if its outlet valve is shut. A blower that reports a

drive fault is removed from the staging count and the edge computer is told the new nmax .

Header pressure above a mechanical trip stops all but one machine and opens a blow-off.

Dissolved oxygen below 0.4 mg/L for more than ten minutes forces n upward if a machine

is available, even if the optimizer is silent. The exhaust fans run if their tank level is above

the low-low cutout. None of these rules is an optimization. They are the reason Level 1


                                                64
exists. The estimation below does not need to hit them on the nominal week. The build is

incomplete if they are only in a manual.

  Motor efficiency class is part of the build and not part of the control law. Machines

on a variable-speed aeration duty should be specified to the current efficiency class for

converter-fed motors, and the fan curve should be the tested curve, not a catalogue cube

law [19, 102, 18]. The power model (1.5) then uses the tested efficiency inside η. In the

estimation, η is the constant product 0.70 × 0.95 × 0.97. A site curve would replace that

product and would be read by the same residual.



3.1.4     Software cycle

The edge cycle is one minute, aligned to the historian. Inside the minute the sequence is

fixed. Read averages. Update the α filter (2.25). Evaluate the sensor residual on the last

window and set the health bit. Run Algorithm 1. Publish q, n, and ν. Log the residual,

the health bit, the load scale, and the active degradation row of Table 2.1. Logging the

degradation row is what makes a later audit possible. A plant that only logs dissolved

oxygen cannot explain a week in which the calendar feed was down.

  The proportional-integral fallback is tuned once, stored in the programmable controller,

and not retuned by the edge computer. Its set-point is 2.5 mg/L, its proportional gain on

airflow is 1400 Nm3 h−1 per mg/L, and its integral gain is 500 Nm3 h−1 per mg/L per hour,

with the integrator clamped at ±8 mg h L−1 in the units of the implemented sum. Those

are the gains of the baseline in Section 3.2, so the fallback the plant will actually run is the

fallback the estimate measures. Gains that exist only in a paper and not in the controller

image are not part of the build.

3.2.5     What the estimate does not show

The integrator does not show a trained network’s residual, a drifting diffuser k0 , a clarifier

failure, an anaerobic upset beyond the influent table, a wrong recipe name, or a price

that changes inside the week. It does not show three machines of unequal size, where the

staging rule (2.13) would be replaced by the integer program. It does not show a permit

on suspended solids. It does not show that every brewery will save eleven percent against

its existing loop. A plant that already runs ammonia-based aeration control at a low

dissolved-oxygen set-point has less left to save against the proportional-integral column

and still has the constant-speed column if any tank is in manual [27, 28, 1].

  Measurement uncertainty on the integrated comparison is the uncertainty of the param-

eters in (1.17) and (1.18), not a Monte Carlo band around a stochastic influent. Those

parameters were chosen so the quiet week and the detergent week are both oxygen-feasible.

A diffuser whose clean-water kref is far below 6.5 h−1 would change the absolute power

and could make the fermentation window infeasible at n = 3. The acceptance test in

commissioning is the hand inversion of Section 2.1 against a measured header, plus one

off-gas traverse on a detergent day [13, 72, 15]. If the hand inversion and the meter disagree,

the table in this chapter is not a forecast for that header.

  Within those limits the estimate supports four sentences. Any of the three modulating

loops cuts blower energy by about two thirds relative to constant speed sized for the

detergent hour. The schedule-aware set-points cut about twelve percent relative to a

competent proportional-integral loop parked at 2.5 mg/L, with no increase in hours above

the study chemical-oxygen-demand limit. A predictive inversion without a live α matches

that energy and gives up dissolved-oxygen floor during the detergent window, down to

0.90 mg/L against 1.63 mg/L. Exhaust fans add about 1300 kW h in the week under a

calendar speed fraction and are not where the saving is. Those four sentences are the

quantitative claim of the thesis.

3.2.6     Implementation order

A plant that accepted the specification would not install both technologies on the same day.

The order implied by the estimate is the order of the tables. First, move constant-speed

tanks onto the Level 1 proportional-integral fallback and confirm the interlocks. That step

takes the large share of the energy and does not require an edge computer. Second, install

the set-point table (2.2) and the calendar subscription, still with α from the regime table

rather than from the network. Table 3.2 says this is where the remaining energy is. Third,

train and commission the soft sensor against off-gas on a detergent day, and only then

close (2.4). The third step is what protects the dissolved-oxygen floor when the detergent

is worse than the table. Building them in the reverse order would put a network in the

loop before the plant has a fallback worth falling back to.

  The software image of the edge computer should be small enough to review: the inversion,

the staging rule, the bias clamp, the health bit, and the log. The full horizon integer

program can replace the staging rule when the machines stop being identical, and it should

be validated against the rule on the identical-machine case before it is allowed to start

a fan the rule would leave off. That validation is a regression test on recorded weeks,

not a new theory. The theory this plant needs was already in the aeration-control and

predictive-control literature cited in Chapter 1. The part that was not in that literature is

the use of the brewery calendar as the disturbance and the use of a transfer residual as the

way α enters the fan command. The week integrated here is the estimate of what those

two uses are worth when the sensor is good and the calendar is right.

3.3.3     Commissioning sequence tied to the tables

Commissioning follows the implementation order already given, with a measurement at

each step so that Table 3.5 can be rejected or kept.

  The first measurement is the header. With one machine online, a technician records

airflow, power, and header pressure at three speeds that the drive will actually use, for

example speed fractions near 0.55, 0.75, and 0.95. Those three points are compared with

Table 2.2. A site whose middle point is far from the table replaces η and the friction

coefficient in (1.18) before any controller is judged. Comparing controllers on a wrong

power map produces a precise ranking of the wrong plant. The fan test standard is the

right experimental frame for this measurement [102, 18, 19]. The thesis table is only the

model those measurements have to beat.

  The second measurement is a clean-water or off-gas transfer test at one airflow near

2500 Nm3 /h, which identifies kref in (1.17) [13, 15, 71]. If the identified kref is not near

6.5 h−1 , the inversion (2.12) is wrong by that ratio to the power 1/0.82, and every airflow

command scales. This is a one-day test. It is not optional, because the rest of the estimate

is a ratio of controllers that share the same kref . The ratio can survive a biased kref . The

absolute kilowatt-hours cannot.

  The third measurement is a detergent day with the proportional-integral fallback still

in charge and the edge computer logging only. The log must show the calendar flag, the

dissolved-oxygen minimum, and a portable check of the probe. That day sets the sensor

threshold ϵ and the regime-table value of α for the recipe. If the portable probe and the

installed probe disagree by more than a few tenths of a milligram per litre, the bias clamp

                                             78
in (2.10) is reviewed before the edge computer is allowed to write a set-point. The literature

on sensor properties is the reason this day exists [70, 22, 74].

  The fourth measurement is the first week in which Algorithm 1 writes commands, with

Level 1 still able to fall back on a single switch. The week is accepted if three statements

hold. Blower energy is below the proportional-integral week on the same production

calendar, not below a week of different batches. Hours above the site permit are not higher

than the fallback week. The dissolved-oxygen minimum during each detergent recipe stays

above the operational floor the site has written down, which in this study is 1.5 mg/L. A

site that copies the numbers 11.6% and 69% without those three statements has copied the

wrong result. The percentages are the output of one integrator on Table 1.2. The three

statements are the result that travels.



3.3.4     What would falsify the estimate

The estimate is falsified, for this train, by any of the following observations, and each one

points at a specific equation rather than at a vague model error.

  If, at the recorded airflows, the power meter disagrees with Table 2.2 by more than

the efficiency uncertainty, then (1.5) or (1.18) is the wrong header and the kilowatt-hours

move. The ranking against constant speed will usually survive, because constant speed is

the same wrong map evaluated at one flow. The ranking against the proportional-integral

loop is more fragile, because that gap is only twelve percent.

  If a detergent day drives α well below the 0.40 of Table 1.2 and the third machine at

qmax cannot hold the dissolved-oxygen floor, then the plant is oxygen-infeasible and no

supervisory law inside this thesis repairs it. The remedy is a diffuser change, a lower load,

or a fourth machine [12, 4, 5]. Reporting a controller saving on an infeasible plant is not a

result.

  If the production clock is routinely wrong by more than the preview, the proposed loop

collapses toward the fixed-α row of Table 3.5 whenever the recipe flag is also wrong, and



                                             79
toward a set-point-tracking inversion when only the time is wrong. The dissolved-oxygen

minimum in the fermentation window is the first place that collapse would show.

  If the clarifier, which this estimate does not model dynamically, loses solids and the

permit that actually binds is suspended solids rather than chemical oxygen demand, then

the indices in Section 1.3 are the wrong indices [97, 6]. The fan law can still be the right

fan law. The claim about discharge compliance cannot be carried across that change of

permit.

  Within the observations the integrator did produce, none of those falsifiers is triggered.

Power at the constant-speed flow matches Table 2.2. The fermentation window is feasible

at a mean near 136 kW, inside a three-machine header whose constant-speed point is

186 kW. The calendar in the integrator is the calendar the controller sees. The permit

in the integrator is chemical oxygen demand, and the hours above 125 mg/L are zero for

every loop. The remaining engineering fact is the one the two technologies were built

for. Constant-speed fans sized for the worst hour of a brewery week waste most of their

electricity on the other hours. A dissolved-oxygen loop removes most of that waste and

leaves a smaller waste that comes from a high, fixed set-point. A schedule-aware inversion

removes that smaller waste. A live oxygen-transfer factor does not stack another large

energy cut on top of a good set-point. It is what keeps the dissolved-oxygen floor intact

when the cleaning chemicals that the brewery must use arrive in the tank the fans are

trying to aerate.



3.3.5     Open-loop step against the closed-loop week

Table 2.4 holds uptake fixed and holds airflow fixed, and it is not a row of Table 3.5. The

comparison is still the right last check on the estimation, because a reader can otherwise

think that a minimum of 0.90 mg/L in the fixed-α loop contradicts an open-loop end point

of 0.53 mg/L. It does not. The open-loop step assumes the biomass keeps consuming

30 mg/(L h) after dissolved oxygen has collapsed. The week model multiplies the rate by

µC = C/(KDO + C). At C = 0.9 and KDO = 0.35 that factor is 0.9/1.25 = 0.72, so uptake

                                            80
has already fallen by more than a quarter, the driving force C ∗ − C has widened, and

the bias has started to add air. All three effects slow the fall. The open-loop table is

the unmitigated error. The week minimum is the error after mitigation by physics the

controller does not deserve credit for, plus mitigation by the bias, which it does.

  What the controller does deserve credit for is the gap between 0.90 mg/L and 1.63 mg/L

in the same window, at nearly the same energy as a predictive law that was handed a

sensible set-point and a wrong factor. The proportional-integral minimum of 1.87 mg/L

shows that feedback with a high set-point can also clear the floor. It pays for that clearance

in the base regime, 4102 against 3469 kWh. The schedule-aware law clears the floor in the

detergent window without paying the quiet-hour price of a permanent 2.5 mg/L set-point.

That sentence is the estimate. The open-loop column at 3570 Nm3 /h is why the sentence

is about α and not only about set-points. A set-point of 2.0 mg/L, computed with the

wrong factor, does not become a tank concentration of 2.0 mg/L until the bias has finished

a correction of the size Table 2.4 measures.

  The constant-speed column does not need this distinction. At 6552 Nm3 /h the header is

above the corrected detergent airflow of about 5250 Nm3 /h for the whole week, which is

why its fermentation minimum is 2.90 mg/L and why its base-regime dissolved oxygen sits

near 5.5 mg/L. Safety by excess air is real. It is also the 21 495 kW h the modulating loops

are measured against. A design review that stops at “the constant-speed plant met the

permit” has stopped before the only question the fans pose, which is how much air the

permit actually required in each hour the calendar had already named.
