                         laminated-timber chassis:

                    Compliance-aware vision metering, and
           singulation-constrained terrain model-predictive coverage.




                    A design thesis in three chapters

Abstract

Wooden seed, in the sense used here, means seed of woody plants—trees and shrubs sown
in agroforestry and restoration—and it also means the material of the machine that carries
that seed. A planter built from laminated timber can be made where steel fabrication is
scarce, it stores less kinetic energy in a light chassis, and its row unit dissipates vibration
more readily than a welded steel parallelogram. The same compliance that damps impact
also moves the boot and the seed tube while a seed is falling. Irregular woody seeds already
singulate poorly on plates and vacuum orifices that were proportioned for maize. The result
is a placement error with two coupled causes: the meter releases the wrong number of seeds,
and the wooden arm releases them from a moving gate at the wrong depth.
    This thesis proposes two technologies and joins them at a single interface, the admissible
ground speed. The first technology, compliance-aware vision metering, watches the seed
plate and the upper seed tube with a detector of the YOLO family, fuses that observation
with boot force, an ultrasonic soil range and an inertial measurement of the arm, and com-
mands plate speed and boot depth. A structural model of the timber flexure tells the filter
when a miss is a true empty cell and when it is a seed whose exit time was shifted by vi-
bration. The second technology, singulation-constrained terrain model-predictive coverage,
chooses the heading on a digital elevation model and then tracks the resulting swaths with
a linearised model-predictive controller. The stage cost penalises grade-dependent traction
work, time, and a miss probability that rises with speed, with acceleration, and with prox-
imity to the resonance of the wooden arm. Speed is therefore not an independent set-point.
It is the variable through which coverage respects the latency and the resonance limits of
the meter.
    The thesis is organised in three chapters. Chapter 1 reviews metering measurement,
vision, wooden and manual woody-seed tools, coverage planning and agricultural model-
predictive tracking, and closes with a formal problem set. Chapter 2 derives both technolo-
gies, including the filter, the feedback law, the energy model and the predictive program.
Chapter 3 specifies a four-row build and reports a numerical estimation on a 1.44 hectare
field at an eight-percent grade. The estimation is not a field trial. It evaluates the Chapter 2
models with coefficients placed inside ranges published by the works reviewed in Chapter 1.
On that model, the joint design at 1.4 m/s on a heading 55° from the uphill axis reaches
a quality-of-feed index of 0.978 and a depth root-mean-square error of 2.15 mm, against

Chapter 1

Research of Previous Work

A wooden seed-planting machine sits at an awkward intersection of three literatures that
rarely cite one another. Precision-planter research measures singulation of maize, soybean
and cotton on steel row units and treats the frame as rigid. Forestry and restoration research
measures whether a hand jab, a hollow planting stick or a broadcaster puts a woody or native
seed into a narrow depth band, and it treats the mechanism as a hand tool. Agricultural-
robotics research plans coverage and tracks a path, and it treats the implement behind the
hitch as a load rather than as a process whose quality collapses when the vehicle accelerates.
This chapter reads those literatures in the order the two technologies of the thesis require.
Section 1.1 reviews metering, sensing and vision, which is the background of compliance-
aware vision metering. Section 1.2 reviews coverage and model-predictive tracking, which
is the background of singulation-constrained terrain coverage. Section 1.3 turns the gaps
into a problem set with indices, constraints and research questions. Throughout, a formula
is quoted only when the later design uses it, and a numerical claim from the literature is
kept attached to the paper that measured it.

   Heege’s survey of precision farming treats seeding as one actuation among several that
become spatial once the machine knows where it is [30]. Ehsani, Upadhyaya and Matt-
son had already shown that a real-time kinematic satellite fix can map the intended seed
coordinate to centimetre level [22]. Mapping the command is not the same as mapping
the seed. Griepentrog, Nørremark, Nielsen and Blackmore separated those two maps for
sugar beet and argued that a seed map is the right input to later mechanical weeding [27].
The distinction is the one this thesis keeps: a coverage planner produces a command map
of where seeds should fall, and a metering controller produces an estimate of where they
did fall. Lowenberg-DeBoer and Erickson, reviewing adoption, note that variable-rate and
section control spread faster than the agronomy that would justify every rate change [45].
For woody seed the justification is harsher than for maize, because the seed itself is scarce
and the replacement cost of a failed hill is a year of growth rather than a later side-dress.


1.1.2     Wooden tools and the depth band of woody seed

The machines that actually put woody and native seed into the ground are, in a large part
of the world, still hand tools, and many of those tools are wood or are used as if they were
a stick. A Missoula Technology and Development Center evaluation of four commercial
single-seed planters—a standard jab planter, a seed stick, a standing tube planter and a
transplanter—found that hollow-tube tools with a bit that opens at the bottom planted
small tree seed at a set depth more reliably than tools that merely poked and hoped,
and that durability of the bit and of the release latch was the practical failure [65]. The
operator supplied the singulation by dropping one seed into the tube. That division of
Chapter 1. Research of Previous Work                                                        5


labour is honest about mechanism: wood and a simple latch can make a hole, and they
cannot see a double.

   A jab planter built and tested by final-year bioresources students for no-till conditions
assembled the same functions—frame and handle, hopper, precision meter, delivery tube,
ground opener—into one manual machine, and compared it with a hoe [33]. The meter
is the part that decides the index in Equation (1.2). The frame is the part that decides
whether the tube is vertical when the seed leaves it. A mechatronic dibbler built for a
radiata-pine nursery attacked a related defect at production scale. Poorly shaped holes
were bending stems; about 22% of seedling rejection at that nursery was attributed to
the hole rather than to the plant. A machine that cut vertical holes of a specified depth
removed the stem bend and raised worker throughput by about 10% [20]. The lesson for
a field planter is not the nursery mechanism. It is the separation between a geometric
specification (vertical, depth within a band) and a human or a servo that tries to meet it.

   Masarei, Guzzomi, Merritt and Erickson measured the emergence of soft spinifex and
found the optimum at a covering of 5 mm, a sharp fall by 10 mm, and almost no emergence
from 30 mm, on the rocky profiles of mine waste [48]. They also record the practitioner
survey result that depth precision is one of the properties restoration seeding lacks, and
that agricultural openers built for crop seed do not hold a band of a few millimetres once
the surface is rough. Bond, Honig and Maze had given the allometric reason: larger seeds
reach the surface from deeper burial, so a depth setting copied from maize is not a con-
servative default for a small woody or native seed [11]. St Jack, Hesterman and Guzzomi,
metering sandalwood seed, showed that a species with an irregular and relatively large
woody seed still needs a meter designed around that shape rather than a crop plate with
the vacuum turned up [63]. This thesis takes a mid-size woody seed as its design case, with
a target depth of 18 mm and an estimation band of ±8 mm. Those two numbers are design
assumptions, chosen to sit between the maize band and the spinifex band. They are not a
measured optimum for a named species.

without an added damper [69]. A row-unit link built as a timber cantilever of length L,

1.1.3     Optoelectronics and high-speed imaging

Before a camera was cheap enough to mount on a row unit, singulation was measured with
a light plane across the seed tube. Kocher, Lan, Chen and Smith built an optoelectronic
sensor that timed seeds as they crossed a beam and converted inter-arrival time into spacing
at a known ground speed [41]. The method is fast, it needs no training set, and it cannot
tell a clod of soil or a chip of husk from a seed, nor can it see two seeds that cross the plane
overlapping. It also sees the seed only after the release decision is over, so it is a monitor
unless a later cell can still be advanced or retarded.

   High-speed imaging removed that ambiguity on the test stand. Mangus and colleagues
mounted a camera at 300 frames per second on a variable-rate electric-drive planter and
combined the frames with the machine’s own speed and seed-tube pulses [47]. Across
constant speeds from 2.4 km/h to 16.1 km/h the mean singulation was 98.45%. Doubles
were rare (0.03%) and triples were absent in that data. The errors that did appear were
organised by the operating state: they nearly doubled when the machine accelerated or
decelerated at 4.8 km/h per second rather than at 2.4 km/h per second, and they rose once
the meter was asked for more than about 1250 seeds per minute. Vision counts and the
commercial seed-tube sensor agreed within 0.8 ± 0.2 percentage points. The paper’s design
recommendation is the one a coverage controller should read as a constraint. There exists
a seed-per-minute region in which the meter is good, and headland behaviour knocks the
machine out of that region even when the steady-state plate calibration is perfect.

   Zhao, Gao, Su, Xu and Huang combined a discrete-element simulation of a high-speed
meter with a high-speed camera, which is the right pairing when the object is the trajectory
inside the housing rather than only the spacing on the belt [73]. Discrete-element work itself
goes back to Cundall and Strack’s contact law for discs and spheres [17]. Lei and colleagues
used it, with a bench test, on a centralised rapeseed meter, where the seeds interact in a
stream rather than in a single cell [44]. For a woody seed the contact parameters—friction
with a timber or polymer wall, restitution after a bounce off a wooden tube—are not the
maize parameters those studies identify. The method transfers. The numbers do not.

   Navid, Ebrahimian, Gassemzadeh and Mousavinia showed that a laboratory camera
and a classical image-processing chain, without a learned detector, already recover spacing
statistics from a side view of falling seeds [50]. Classical processing is attractive on a wooden
machine because the computing budget may be a single board. It fails when the seed is the
Chapter 1. Research of Previous Work                                                         8


same colour as the wooden tube, when dust coats the window, or when two overlapping
silhouettes must be counted as two objects. Those are ordinary conditions in a restoration
planting, and they are why the later papers move to learned detectors.

late. A late correction is not a neutral failure. It inserts a seed into a neighbour’s interval

1.1.6     What this line of work does not close

The metering literature now contains accurate indices, a vacuum model, a discrete-element
method, optoelectronic monitors, high-speed confirmation that acceleration and seed rate
organise the errors, and detectors that name empties and doubles at errors of a few percent.
It also contains one clear negative result: a correct detector plus a slow processor makes
singulation worse above a modest speed [13]. It does not contain a filter whose process
model is the flexure of a timber row unit, and it does not contain a speed command that
refuses the resonance of that flexure and the latency limit of Equation (1.9). Those two
missing pieces are one technology, because the same state vector carries the cell label and
the arm motion. Section 2.1 builds that technology. It cannot, by itself, decide which way
the machine should drive across a slope, or how hard it may accelerate into a headland.
That decision is the subject of the next section.



1.2     Research on coverage planning and model-predictive
        tracking

1.2.1     Coverage as a geometric problem

Coverage path planning asks for a path whose swept width paints a region and whose cost,
usually length or time, is small. Choset’s survey fixed the vocabulary: cellular decom-
position, Morse functions, and the boustrophedon pattern in which the machine runs a
straight swath, turns on a headland, and runs the next swath in the opposite direction [16].
Chapter 1. Research of Previous Work                                                      12


Galceran and Carreras updated the survey across robotics and separated off-line planners,
which assume a known map, from on-line planners, which discover the boundary as they
travel [25]. A seed-planting machine of the kind considered here is an off-line planner at
the scale of the field and an on-line controller at the scale of the swath. The boundary
is known from a survey or from a previous pass. The disturbance that matters is not an
unknown wall. It is a grade, a patch of soft soil, and a meter that misbehaves when the
speed profile is aggressive.

   Oksanen and Visala published coverage algorithms aimed at agricultural field machines
rather than at indoor robots, including the treatment of headlands, obstacles and the
choice of driving direction on real field polygons [52]. LaValle’s planning text supplies the
underlying algorithms—grid search, sampling, feedback on a cell complex—that those field
methods specialise [43]. Dijkstra’s shortest paths and the A* search of Hart, Nilsson and
Raphael remain the workhorses inside a grid once a field has been rasterised [18, 29]. They
are optimal on the grid they are given. They are silent on whether the cost of an edge
should be metres, joules, or expected missed seeds. Putting the wrong scalar on the edge
is not a small modelling choice. It is the difference between a short path and a path the
meter can survive.

   Bochtis and Vougioukas minimised the non-working distance of a machine that covers
a field in a headland pattern, which is the distance spent turning and relocating rather
than sowing [9]. Bochtis, Sørensen and Busato reviewed the wider machinery-management
problem: sequencing of fields, capacity of the machine, and the distinction between a path
that is short and a plan that a fleet can execute [10]. Sørensen and Bochtis had set out
the fleet-management concepts that sit above a single planter [61]. Spekken and de Bruin
optimised in-field routing by counting manoeuvring and servicing time, which is the right
objective when the expensive event is the turn rather than the metre of straight work [62].
Edwards and colleagues evaluated a prototype in-field route planner on neutral-material-
flow operations and showed that a planner’s predicted gain survives contact with a real
sequence of loads only if the model of service time is honest [21]. Fountas and colleagues
place all of these plans in a farm management information system, which is where a seed
map and a coverage file have to live if a later operation is to find them [24]. This thesis
stops at one machine and one field. The interface it leaves upward is a coverage file and a
per-row seed estimate, which is what those information systems already know how to store.
Chapter 1. Research of Previous Work                                                          13


1.2.2     Driving direction, headlands and three-dimensional energy

On flat ground the best driving direction is mostly a statement about how many turns
fit along the short side of the field. Hameed, Bochtis, Sørensen and Nørremark generated
guidance lines for operational planning and made the driving angle a decision variable
rather than a habit of the operator [28]. Jin and Tang solved coverage on two-dimensional
field surfaces with an explicit cost on turns and on overlapped or missed area [34], and then
lifted the same problem onto three-dimensional terrain, where the slope changes the cost
of each heading and can change the decomposition of the field [35]. The three-dimensional
statement is the one a wooden planter on a restoration slope needs. A heading that is only
a few degrees off the contour carries a sustained component of weight down the drawbar.

   Vahdanjoo, Gislum and Sørensen built a three-dimensional coverage tool for a seeding
operation, using a digital elevation model to choose the driving direction against an energy
criterion, and they also produced a variable-rate map and a compaction-risk estimate [66].
On two sample fields their model reduced the required energy by up to 9.5% and 15.6%
relative to a poorer direction. The mechanism is not mysterious. Rolling resistance and
wheel slip depend on the normal and the tangential components of weight,


                              F∥ = mg sin α,      F⊥ = mg cos α,                          (1.13)


and a boustrophedon that runs up and down cancels the conservative part of F∥ only in
the energy ledger of a lossless vehicle. Slip, rolling of a soft tyre in soft soil, and the extra
draft of an opener that is loaded unevenly are not conservative. They leave a remainder
that depends on how the heading sits relative to the gradient. Their paper is the closest
neighbour of the second technology. It optimises energy of seeding coverage on a real eleva-
tion model. It does not constrain the speed so that a vision meter can finish a correction,
and it does not know that a timber arm has a resonant speed.

   Headland geometry interacts with that energy term because the turn is where speed is
low and acceleration is high, which is exactly the state Mangus found doubles the singulation
error [47]. Graf Plessen and Bemporad treat the shaping of the reference that joins one
swath to the next. High-level planners emit corners. A machine cannot track a corner. They
compare circle arcs, generalised elementary paths and bi-elementary paths, and they hand
the smoothed reference to a linear time-varying model-predictive tracker, with constraints
Chapter 1. Research of Previous Work                                                      14


on actuator rate and on the field boundary so that a sprayer does not leave gaps or paint the
crop twice in the turn [55]. The same smoothing is required for a planter, with one added
constraint they did not have: during the acceleration out of the turn the predicted miss
index must stay under a cap. A geometrically beautiful clothoid that demands 1.2 m/s2 for
two seconds is a bad planter trajectory even when it is a good sprayer trajectory.

   Zhang, Qian, Li, Bai and Wang assembled a seeding-robot planner from a lidar-camera
perception front end, an adaptive raster, an A* search fused with ant-colony optimisation,
and a multi-objective scoring of the path [71, 19]. With a raster of 0.15 m, a pheromone
factor of 2.5, a heuristic factor of 3.0 and 100 iterations they report a planning time of
0.45 s, a success rate of 98.7%, an overlap of 3.2%, an efficiency gain of 28.5%, an energy
reduction of 21.3% and a missed-sowing rate of 1.8% in their trials. The architecture is the
right shape for a seeding robot: perception, map, search, score. The score, as published,
does not include a latency inequality from a vision meter or a resonant frequency of a
wooden link. Ant-colony search can carry those terms as soon as they are written into the
edge cost. The contribution is the term, not another metaheuristic.

   Xu, Lai, Guo and Lu name that indifference. Their efficiency-oriented model-predictive

efficiency objective, rather than tracking a frozen path so faithfully that efficiency gets
worse. In simulation they report an 8.56% efficiency gain against a conventional nonlinear
predictive tracker [70]. The device they introduce, a pseudo-point that the machine is not
required to touch, is a way of putting a global objective inside a short horizon. This thesis
uses a different device for a different objective. The global objective that must enter the
short horizon is not only time. It is the probability that the next few seeds are misses,
which is a function of speed, acceleration and the vibration state of the arm. A tracker that
is willing to arrive earlier by crossing the resonant speed of the timber is, on the indices of
Equation (1.2), a worse planter.

sugar-beet field, which remains a useful existence proof that a small vehicle can stay in a row

the action is per row, because four timber flexures do not share one resonance phase.

   Pedersen, Fountas, Have and Blackmore analysed the economics of agricultural robots
and concluded that a robot pays when it replaces a scarce labour peak or when it enables

build in Section 3.1 does not depend on a particular tractor brand. It does depend on a
clock that can stamp a plate command and a camera frame onto the same time base. A
proprietary analogue drive without a time stamp cannot host either technology.

1.3      Problem set

1.3.1     Operating context and scope

The machine considered from here on is a four-row planter. Each row unit carries a cell
plate, a wooden or wood-lined seed tube, a boot, a camera aimed at the plate and the upper
tube, a boot-force sensor, an ultrasonic range to the soil surface and an inertial sensor on
the arm. The chassis and the parallel links are laminated softwood. The design case uses
N = 24 cells, xref = 0.20 m, a drop H = 0.42 m and a target depth of 18 mm. The field
used later for estimation is a rectangle 80 m by 180 m with the long axis up the slope, at a
grade of 8%, so that a contour heading produces short rows and many turns and an uphill
heading produces long rows and a sustained grade load. That geometry is chosen because
it does not let one heading win on every term at once.

   The following assumptions bound the problem. They are restated where a derivation
would otherwise smuggle them in.

Assumption 1.1. Seeds are rigid bodies of one species for the duration of a pass. Mass
and projected area vary from seed to seed but stay inside a known bounded set. The meter
is a cell plate, not a volumetric flute.

Assumption 1.2. Each row-unit flexure is represented by one equivalent mass, stiffness
and damping ratio at the boot. Coupling between the four row units through the chassis is
neglected in the filter and is revisited as a modelling error in the estimation.

Assumption 1.3. The field boundary and a digital elevation model are known before work
starts. Obstacles are static and are already cut out of the coverage polygon. Soil strength
enters only through the force that deflects the boot and through the rolling-resistance coeffi-
cient.

Assumption 1.4. Chapter 3 evaluates models. It does not report a germination trial, a
timber fatigue test, or a detector trained on a new image set. Detector precision and recall
are taken from the published peanut and maize figures cited above and are treated as an
upper bound for a woody seed, not as a measurement on one.
Chapter 1. Research of Previous Work                                                        19


   Assumption 1.4 is a limitation of the thesis, not a detail. A woody seed is harder to
singulate than maize. Using maize and peanut detector scores inside an estimation makes
the vision benefit look as good as those crops allowed. The estimation therefore supports
a design comparison under published sensing performance. It does not forecast a field
percentage for acacia or pine.

period of Equation (1.8). The steering and speed commands sit between them. Splitting
the joint problem along those time scales is a design choice of Chapter 2, and it is justified
by the time-scale separation already used in agricultural predictive control [70, 55]. The
formal risk of the split is sub-optimality: a heading chosen before the day starts does not
see a patch of soil that shifts fn . The build therefore allows the predictive tracker to reduce
speed inside a heading even when it cannot rotate the whole field plan in one sample.

   The failure probability in Equation (1.17) has to be a function the planner can evaluate
without resimulating every seed. The structure used throughout the thesis is

                                                          
                            ρfail = 1 − 1 − MIS − κMUL S(σz ),                           (1.18)


with κ ∈ (0, 1] because a multiple wastes seed and crowds a neighbour but is not always a
total loss, and with S a decreasing function of the depth root-mean-square error. Chapter 3
takes κ = 1/2 and S(σz ) = exp(−σz2 /(2σ02 )) with σ0 = 6.5 mm. Both choices are estimation
devices. They encode the statement, taken from the depth literature [48, 11], that depth
error and count error both destroy a hill, and that they do not destroy it in identical ways.


1.3.3     Requirements

The requirements below are written so that a later design choice can be checked against
them without reinterpretation. A requirement that says “accurate” would not do that work.

R1. The quality-of-feed index on the design spacing, inside Vmeter , shall be at least 0.95
      in the estimation model when the detector operates at the published precision and
      recall.

R2. The depth root-mean-square error shall be at most 4 mm inside the same set, against
      the 18 mm target.

R3. The vision-and-actuation delay shall be treated as a hard constraint on speed through
      Equation (1.9), not as a penalty that a large weight might override.

R4. The resonant neighbourhood of the timber flexure, where M (r) > 3, shall be excluded
      from steady running or traversed only while accelerating through it.

 R5. The coverage cost shall include mechanical work, time and failed placements, and the
      failed-placement term shall use the same miss model as the metering loop.

 R6. Each row shall be sensed and metered independently. A single vehicle-level camera
      does not satisfy the requirement.

 R7. The predictive tracker shall run at a sample period no longer than 100 ms on the

 R8. The estimation shall publish the open-loop baseline and the resonant-speed base-

 R9. No result shall be described as a field percentage for a named species. The language

      each carry a private speed set-point.

   Requirement R3 is the lesson of the 6 km/h reversal [13]. Requirement R4 is the lesson
of Equation (1.7) applied to a flexure that a steel planter would have pushed out of the
frequency band. Requirement R10 is the architectural claim of the thesis. A vision meter
that assumes a constant 1.5 m/s and a coverage planner that assumes the meter can tolerate
whatever speed saves fuel are two correct components and one incorrect system.

2.1.4     Unscented filter for a mixed observation

The process model is nonlinear because of Equation (2.8) and because the ultrasonic ge-

both. An unscented filter propagates a small set of sigma points through the nonlinear map
and matches the mean and covariance up to second order, which is the property Julier and
Uhlmann designed it for [36]. Welch and Bishop’s notes and Särkkä’s text are the imple-
mentation references for the weights and the Joseph-stabilised covariance update [68, 58].
Thrun, Burgard and Fox discuss the same update in the setting of a robot that must stamp
sensor events onto a moving base [64]. The pattern here is closer to their event filter than
to a simultaneous-localisation map: the “landmark” is the next cell, and it is consumed
when the seed passes the gate.

2.1.8     Four rows, clocking and what the filter does not estimate

The four row units are four copies of the same filter. They share the vehicle speed and the
sample clock, and nothing else in the nominal design. Sharing the clock matters because a
repair advance on row 1 and a repair advance on row 3 must not be queued on one processor
so that the second repair misses its gate. The build therefore gives each row a detector
thread with a reserved budget of 22 ms, and it drops a frame rather than extending τv .
A dropped frame lowers confidence, inflates the release-time process noise, and withholds
repair for that hole. Withholding repair is the safe side of Borja’s reversal.

   Chassis coupling, neglected by Assumption 1.2, would appear as a common δ component
on all four rows when the whole machine pitches. The inertial sensors will see that common
mode. A later revision can subtract the mean of the four δ̇ estimates before the repair law
runs. The present design does not. The estimation treats the omission as an unmodelled
disturbance inside the process noise already scheduled by M (r), and it does not claim
the four rows are independent in a statistical test. They are independent in the software
architecture.

   Seed identity across a double is not estimated. The filter counts a double. It does not
decide which of the two seeds is the one that should have been released. For a single-species
woody seed the distinction does not change the command. For a mixed-species restoration
hopper it would, and that case is outside Assumption 1.1.

2.2.3     Outer program

The outer program searches a finite set. Headings are taken at five-degree steps from 0°
to 90°, using the symmetry of a rectangular field with a single slope direction. Speeds are
taken at 0.1 m/s steps inside Vmeter , with the resonant interval [2.15 m/s, 2.55 m/s] removed
even if a future change of xref were to widen the latency set. For each candidate the
cost of Definition 1.2 is evaluated in closed form from Equations (2.32)–(2.34). Infeasible
candidates, those with modelled QFI < 0.955 or σz > 4 mm, are discarded before the cost
is compared. This is a lexicographic filter in front of a scalar cost, and it implements
Requirements R1 and R2 as constraints rather than as terms that a weight could bargain
away.

   The weights used for the scalar comparison are wT = 0.55 MJ/h and ws such that the
failure term enters as 4.0 ρfail in megajoule units on this field. The weights are planning
preferences. They say that an hour of machine time is worth a fraction of a megajoule of
the work budget, and that a failed placement is expensive relative to the energy differences,
which are themselves only a few tenths of a megajoule. A user who prices contractor time
higher can raise wT . The constraint filter does not move when the weights move. That
separation is intentional. It keeps a cheap hour from buying a resonant speed.

   For a non-rectangular field the same cost is evaluated along a coverage produced by the
trapezoidal decomposition of Oksanen and Visala, or by the three-dimensional decomposi-
tion of Jin and Tang when the elevation model is not a single plane [52, 35]. The rectangle
in the estimation is a plane of constant α, so the decomposition adds no region boundary
and the heading search is the whole outer program. The algorithm is written for the gen-
eral case so that a polygonal field does not require a different controller, only a different
enumeration of swaths.

2.2.5     What the inner cost will not do

Xu, Lai, Guo and Lu showed that a tracker allowed to care about efficiency can beat a
tracker that only cares about path error, by 8.56% in their simulation [70]. The same
freedom can be misused. If wρ is set to zero, Equation (2.37) becomes a conventional
tracker plus a weak resonance bowl, and a high wT in the outer program will push vwork to
the boundary of the constraint. The design therefore keeps the index limits in the outer
filter even when the weights change, and it keeps c(v, a) ≥ 0.85 as a hard inner constraint
even when wρ is small. Efficiency is allowed to choose among admissible speeds. It is not
allowed to define admissibility.

   The inner program also does not schedule plate speed. Plate speed remains Equa-
tion (2.10), driven by the speed the vehicle actually has, not by the speed the planner
Chapter 2. Proposed Technologies                                                               45


wanted a second ago. Using the realised v closes the interface in the safe direction. If slip
on the grade drops the realised speed, the plate slows and the spacing is held. If the plan-
ner’s vwork were sent straight to the plate while the wheels were still slow, the meter would
bunch seeds at the start of every swath. Mangus’s acceleration result is the field version
of that bunching [47]. Realised speed as the feedforward input is how the first technology
protects itself from a confident plan.

   Variable rate along a swath, in the precision-agriculture sense of a prescription map,
fits the same interface. A prescription xref (s) changes Equation (1.4) and changes ∆t, and
therefore changes Vmeter locally, because a shorter spacing at the same speed is a shorter hole
period. The outer program, if a prescription is present, evaluates c with the local spacing
and lowers vwork on the dense parts of the map. The estimation field uses a constant spacing,
so this branch is specified and not exercised. It is the reason xref is an input of Definition 1.1
rather than a constant buried in the plate law.


2.2.6     How the two technologies avoid a circular design

There is an apparent circle. The metering technology publishes Vmeter using τv , τa , fn
and λ. The coverage technology chooses v inside that set and then drives the flexure at
that v, which is what fn and λ were worried about. The circle is broken by the order of
computation. The flexure parameters and the latency are properties of the hardware and
of the detector. They do not depend on the heading. Equation (2.13) can be evaluated
before any coverage search. The coverage search never writes back into k, ζ, τv or τa . The
only write-back is the realised speed, which the plate law accepts as an exogenous input.
A fixed point is not being solved. A constraint computed from hardware is being obeyed
by a planner.

   The write-back that does exist is diagnostic. After a pass, the logged release times and
the logged δ̂ can update the estimate of fn and of λ, if the roughness of that field is not
the design wavelength. The updated parameters change Vmeter for the next pass. They are
not allowed to change it in the middle of a hole period. This is the same discipline as the
dropped-frame rule: a late adaptation is a worse fault than a slightly conservative speed.

that brake even if the time weight would have liked it. The headland is therefore not an
exception to the metering technology. It is the place the exception would have been, and
the constraint is written there on purpose.

   During the turn itself the plate may be inhibited by the boot-lift interlock if the headland
is unplanted, which is the usual layout [9]. Inhibition is cleaner than asking the meter to
singulate while the boot is out of the soil and the ultrasonic range is invalid. When the next
swath begins, the integral of the depth loop is released from its freeze, the plate feedforward
tracks the realised speed as the vehicle climbs back from 0.8 m/s to 1.4 m/s, and repairs are
authorised only once c has returned. The first metre of the new swath is the other place
a naive plan bunches seeds, by commanding the working plate speed before the wheels are
at the working speed. Using realised v in Equation (2.10) spreads that first metre at the
correct spacing instead. The cost is a slightly later start of full-rate planting, already paid
for in the headland length.


2.2.12     Logging that makes the next pass stricter rather than
           braver

Each release writes a row to the seed map: vehicle pose, filter release time, estimated
exit velocity, detector label, repair flag, realised delay, and filtered depth. The row is an
estimate, not a proof that a seed lies at the computed coordinate. Its first use is the one
Griepentrog, Nørremark, Nielsen and Blackmore described, a map a later operation can
follow [27]. Its second use is internal, and it is deliberately asymmetric. If the realised
Chapter 2. Proposed Technologies                                                                51


delay over a swath exceeds the budget, the next swath’s τ is raised and Vmeter shrinks.
If the delay is better than the budget, τ is not lowered on the same day. A fast swath
can be luck of the scheduler. A slow swath is evidence the inequality is tighter than the
specification. The same asymmetry applies to fn . A resonance that appears in δ̂ widens
the forbidden interval for the next pass. A quiet swath does not delete the interval, because
the roughness wavelength of one swath is not the wavelength of the field. Adaptation that
only ever tightens the constraint cannot talk a machine into the regime Borja’s loop entered
when the processor fell behind [13]. Adaptation that updates in both directions can.

   The log is also the object a later germination count has to be joined to, if the survival
function S(σz ) is ever replaced by a measured curve. Joining requires the landing coordinate
from Equation (1.12), not only the vehicle coordinate at the moment the detector fired.
Those two coordinates differ by the flight transport, about 0.41 m at the design speed, which
is two target spacings. A survival count joined to the wrong coordinate would teach the
next version of the cost to fear the wrong places. The build therefore stores both stamps.
None of this changes the quadratic program. It is the reason the release log is a row in the
interface table and not an optional debug print.


2.2.13      A calibration pass before the first planted swath

The stiffness k is a specification of the timber link. The soil coefficient ksoil in Equation (2.3)
is not, because it belongs to the field. The build therefore begins a planting day with a
calibration pass rather than with a planted swath. The pass is one swath length, plates
disengaged, boot in the soil, depth screw held at three set-points around the 18 mm target,
for a few metres each. Boot force and ultrasonic depth are logged. The slope of force against
depth is ksoil . A value of order a few newtons per millimetre is what the linearisation expects;
the 80 N static force at the target depth is the intercept F0 (z ⋆ ), and it is the number that
produced the 9.8 mm undeflected offset F0 /k. If the calibration intercept is 110 N rather
than 80 N, the undeflected offset is 110/8200 = 13.4 mm, still inside the screw travel, and
the integral term is required to work harder. The filter does not need a new structure for
that. It needs the new intercept written into Fsoil before the first repair is authorised.

   The same pass estimates the roughness wavelength that the design took as λ = 0.45 m.
The force residual after the linear depth term is removed is a time series along the swath.
Its dominant spatial frequency, if one is visible, replaces λ in the magnification formula
Chapter 2. Proposed Technologies                                                          52


before Vmeter is published. If the residual is broadband, the design wavelength is kept and
the process noise on δ stays on the magnification schedule already stated. Publishing Vmeter
after this pass, rather than from the workshop specification alone, is the one adaptation
allowed before motion. It is still not an adaptation inside a hole period. The tracker does
not move until the set has been published, which is the order of computation that broke
the apparent circle between the two technologies.

   A calibration that cannot be finished, because the ultrasonic return is invalid or the
force signal is saturated, inhibits planting. The alternative is to run the design values and
hope the field matches the specification. The interlock refuses that alternative. A wooden
machine is light enough that an unexpected soil force moves the boot by millimetres, which
is the whole reason the depth loop exists, and a depth loop without a force intercept is an
integral winding up against an unknown load. The pass costs one swath of time, already
small beside the 1.4 h the estimation assigns to the field, and it is the price of keeping
Equation (2.19) attached to the soil under the machine rather than to the soil imagined in
the problem set.

3.1.1    Mechanical arrangement

modulus assumed in k is the modulus the arm still has after a season [69]. Each row unit

3.1.4     Safety interlocks

A planter has a small number of states in which the correct command is to stop metering,
and the build treats them as interlocks rather than as expensive regions of the cost. The
plate is inhibited when the boot is lifted, when realised τv + τa exceeds the budget for longer
than three hole periods, when the hopper-level sensor declares a row empty, and when the
tracker reports that no speed inside Vmeter is feasible on the current slope patch. The last of
these is the situation Proposition 2.2 excludes on the design grade and includes on a grade
or a roughness the specification did not see. Inhibiting the plate loses the rest of the swath
and saves a resonant or a late-repair passage, which on the model of Figure 2.3 is the right
side of the cliff to stand on.

and the last safe force is held. A held force is not a depth guarantee. It is a refusal to

3.2.4    Where the gain comes from

The gain splits into a part the first technology would produce on a fixed heading and a
part that appears only when the heading is free. On the uphill heading at 1.4 m/s, the
closed-loop model already reaches QFI = 0.978 and σz = 2.15 mm, with failure probability
about 0.067. That is almost the whole of the index gain of B3 over B1, and it is available
without any coverage technology at all, at the price of the uphill work 4.780 MJ. The
Chapter 3. System Build and Estimation                                                     65


coverage technology then moves the same metering performance onto a heading that costs
4.415 MJ. Readers who want the singulation result without a new planner can stop at
the first technology and a disciplined speed. Readers who want the work result need the
second technology, and they only keep the singulation result if the planner is forbidden
from spending the work saving on a higher speed. The joint design is the statement that
the work saving will not be spent that way.

   A further split, inside the first technology, separates the repair of empty cells from the
depth servo. At 1.4 m/s the open-loop miss probability from Equation (2.17) is 0.125 and
the closed-loop miss probability is 0.008. That gap is the repair term, and it exists only
while c = 1. The open-loop depth error is 6.65 mm and the closed-loop depth error is
2.14 mm. That gap is the servo, and Proposition 2.1 says it will shrink if the machine
is driven toward resonance. Table 3.3 at 2.4 m/s is the shrinkage: the closed loop still
improves depth relative to the open loop, from 22.0 mm to 13.2 mm, and 13.2 mm is still a
failed band. Vision metering without the speed constraint is not the technology that was
specified. The tables that show a 0.978 index are tables in which the constraint is active as
a restriction on the search, even though at the chosen point it is not active as a saturated
inequality.

3.2.6     Comparison with the nearest published systems

A fair comparison states what was measured there and what is only modelled here. Borja’s
vision-assisted maize meter reduced missed hills at 2 km/h and 4 km/h and increased them
at 6 km/h [13]. The present model reproduces the shape of that reversal, through c(v, a),
and moves the speed at which the reversal begins by assuming a 22 ms vision budget instead
of a microcontroller budget. It does not reproduce their hill counts. Mangus’s electric-drive
meter held a mean singulation of 98.45% over a wide speed range and lost accuracy when
acceleration or seed rate rose [47]. The present closed-loop plateau at 0.97 to 0.98 is a model
expression of that kind of singulation, restricted to the speeds at which a repair can finish,
and the acceleration term in Equation (2.17) is a model expression of their acceleration
finding. Blaser’s and Zhao’s detectors supply the error rates the repair formula is allowed
to believe [8, 74]. They do not supply a wooden tube in the training set.

   On the planning side, Vahdanjoo, Gislum and Sørensen showed that a three-dimensional
energy criterion moves the preferred seeding direction by enough to matter [66]. The oblique
heading in Table 3.5 is the same kind of decision, on a simpler elevation model, with extra
constraints. Zhang and colleagues’ seeding-robot planner reports a missed-sowing rate of
Chapter 3. System Build and Estimation                                                  67


1.8% together with a large energy reduction in their own trials [71]. The present model at
B3 assigns a miss probability near 0.008 on the plateau, which is a lower miss rate than
1.8% and is not a competing experimental claim. It is a statement that, if the detector and
the stepper perform as the repair formula assumes, empty-cell misses are almost removed
and the remaining failure is depth and the occasional multiple. Their planner and this one
can be combined: their search can carry Equation (1.18) as an edge cost, and the inner
tracker of this thesis can track the path they return, provided the path’s speed profile is
projected onto Vmeter before tracking begins.

   No published system reviewed in Chapter 1 closes the loop from a timber natural fre-
quency to a coverage constraint. That closure, rather than a new record on maize singula-
tion, is the comparison this estimation is built to illustrate.


3.2.7     Limits

The limits are the assumptions, restated against the tables so that the largest numbers in
the chapter stay attached to them.

   The failure probabilities inherit S(σz ) and κ = 1/2. A species whose emergence is
tolerant of a 7 mm depth error would shrink the gap between B1 and B3. A species as
intolerant as the spinifex in Masarei, Guzzomi, Merritt and Erickson’s trial would enlarge
it and would also force a smaller σz,max than 4 mm [48]. The design case at 18 mm with an
8 mm band is neither of those species.

   The open-loop intercepts in Equation (2.17) were chosen to sit in a plausible band for
irregular seed. A plate that has already been tuned for the woody species might start
from a better open-loop index, and the vision benefit on misses would then be smaller.
The resonant depth peak would remain, because it is produced by M (r) and by the 4 mm
unresolved bias, not by the miss intercept.

   Chassis coupling, mixed species, wheel slip beyond the force model, and detector perfor-
mance on a dusty wooden tube are outside the numerical evaluation. The build sequence
is where they stop being outside the project. Until that sequence is run, Table 3.5 is a
comparison of plans under a declared model. It is evidence that the constraints are co-
herent and that they bind in the right places: latency at one speed, resonance at another,
grade and turns in the heading. It is not evidence that a particular laminated arm has
k = 8200 N/m, and it is not evidence that a stand of woody seedlings will emerge in the
Chapter 3. System Build and Estimation                                                     68


ratio 1 − 0.068.

Requirement R8 asked for the baselines to sit in the same table as the proposal, and

3.2.11     Reading the energy prize at the scale of one hectare

The 0.37 MJ difference between B1 and B3 is 0.10 kWh. On a 1.44 hectare field that is a
small energy quantity beside the fuel a truck spends to deliver the machine, and it is the
right order of magnitude for a direction choice on a moderate grade. Reported direction
savings of 9.5 to 15.6% on other seeding fields are the same kind of quantity: a fraction of an
already-modest in-field energy, not a new power source [66]. The estimation would mislead
a reader who remembered only the percentage and forgot the base. 7.7% of 4.78 MJ is the
prize. The singulation and depth columns are where the technologies change the operation
by a factor rather than by a fraction. Failure probability falls from 0.561 to 0.068, a factor
of about eight, under a survival function that treats a 7 mm depth error as already serious.
Even if a later germination trial halves that factor, the index gap remains the reason to
build the vision loop, and the energy gap remains a constraint on how the planner is allowed
to spend the time the loop requires.

   Per hectare, using 1.44 ha as the divisor, B3 uses about 3.07 MJ/ha and about 0.98 h/ha,
and it assigns a failed-placement fraction of 0.068. B1 uses about 3.32 MJ/ha and about
0.85 h/ha, with a failed-placement fraction of 0.561. A contractor who bills only time will
prefer B1 and will plant a stand the model regards as mostly outside the depth band. A
contractor who bills for established plants, which is the usual contract in restoration, will
prefer B3 even before the energy line is read. Pedersen, Fountas, Have and Blackmore
made the same structural point for agricultural robots in general: the robot pays when
the operation it enables is one the cheaper machine misses, not when it is a few percent
cheaper at the same operation [54]. The wooden planter with these two technologies is in
that category if, and only if, the depth and singulation gaps survive the measurement sheet.
The energy line is not the category.

3.2.12     The same rectangle turned the other way

The estimation field was chosen so that a contour heading has short rows. That choice is
doing real work, and it should be shown by computing the opposite geometry with the same
equations and the same coefficients. Turn the rectangle so that the long side, 180 m, lies
on the contour and the short side, 80 m, runs up the 8% grade. At 1.4 m/s the work model
then assigns 4.16 MJ and 1.37 h to the contour heading, with 34 turns, and 5.12 MJ and
1.53 h to the uphill heading, with 75 turns. Contour travel is cheaper per metre because
Γ(90◦ ) = 1, and it is also shorter in time because the long rows cut the number of turns.
Both terms agree. Algorithm 2 and a planner that minimises only work return the same
heading. The singulation constraint still chooses the speed, because nothing in the rotation
moves fn or τ , and the selected speed remains 1.4 m/s on the plateau of Figure 2.3.

   On the original field the agreement breaks. Uphill travel at 1.4 m/s costs 4.78 MJ and
1.37 h. Contour travel costs 4.45 MJ and 1.53 h. The 55° heading returned by the joint
search costs 4.41 MJ and 1.41 h. Pure minimum work and the joint search are close, and
they are not identical: the turn term and the time weight pull the optimum off the pure
contour, by a few tenths of a megajoule, toward a longer row. A reader who only remembers
that “the planner chose an oblique heading” has missed the smaller and more useful fact.
The oblique heading is the compromise on a field that punishes the contour with turns.
On a field that rewards the contour with both energy and time, the compromise disappears
and the constraint that remains is the speed ceiling from the wooden arm and the vision
budget. The two technologies are still both active. One of them has nothing left to trade.

   This rotation is the last numerical check the estimation makes. It uses no new coefficient.
It shows that the heading result in Table 3.5 is a property of the field orientation fixed in
the problem set, not a universal preference for 55°. The speed result is the one that travels.
Wherever the long side points, steady running stays off the resonance and inside the repair
inequality, or the outer program returns no plan.

   Within those limits the estimation answers the three research questions in the only sense
a design thesis can answer them before a prototype. The plate law and the flexure filter of
Section 2.1 repair empty cells only while the repair fraction is high, and they assign mean
depth to a servo whose bandwidth sits below fn . The outer search and the inner quadratic
program of Section 2.2 select a heading and a speed inside that repair set, with grade work,
turn time and failed placements in one cost. On the estimation field the selected plan is 55°
Chapter 3. System Build and Estimation                                                  73


from uphill at 1.4 m/s, with modelled quality of feed 0.978, depth error 2.15 mm and non-
conservative work 7.7% below the uphill open-loop plan at 1.6 m/s. The resonant open-loop
plan is faster and, in the same model, does not establish the crop. The prototype specified
in Section 3.1 is the instrument that would turn these statements into measurements.
