                    Singulation-Constrained Terrain Coverage
                      for a Wooden Seed-Planting Machine
                                    System Design Study
            Short paper on the second innovation of the wooden seed-planter thesis


                                                 Abstract
        Coverage planners for seeding choose a heading to save energy on a slope. Metering mea-
     surements show that the same machine loses seeds when it accelerates or when the seed interval
     becomes shorter than the sensing delay. On a timber row unit there is a further excluded speed,
     near the resonance of the arm. This paper puts those limits inside coverage. An outer search picks
     a heading and a steady speed inside the set the meter can still repair. An inner linear time-varying
     predictive tracker may slow down for a headland and may not enter the resonant interval or au-
     thorise a late repair. On a 1.44 ha rectangle at an eight-percent grade, the search returns 55◦ from
     uphill at 1.4 m/s. Modelled non-conservative work falls by about 7.7% relative to an open-loop
     uphill plan, and the failed-placement fraction falls from 0.56 to 0.07.

Keywords: coverage planning, model predictive control, seeding, terrain, wooden planter.


1    Introduction
Field coverage is usually stated as a geometric problem: paint a polygon with straight swaths, turn
on a headland, and keep the non-working distance small [1, 2, 3]. On a slope the heading is no longer
only a statement about how many turns fit along the short side. Grade changes the non-conservative
work of rolling and slip, and three-dimensional coverage has reduced seeding energy by up to about
10 to 16% on sample fields by choosing the driving direction [4, 5]. Trackers that follow the resulting
path are now routinely predictive. They can respect steering rate, estimate the state they control,
and even carry an efficiency term inside the horizon rather than tracking a frozen path so faithfully
that the job takes longer [6, 7, 8, 9].
    A planter adds a constraint those planners do not carry. Seed singulation of an electric-drive meter
stayed near 98% in steady running and worsened when speed changed quickly or when the meter was
asked for too many seeds per minute [10]. A vision repair that is accurate and late increases misses
[11]. The companion metering design for this machine publishes a feasible speed set from that delay
and from the resonance of a laminated row unit. This paper is the coverage half. It minimises work,
time and failed placements, and it treats the feasible set as a hard constraint. Efficiency may choose
among admissible speeds. It may not define admissibility.
    The split follows the usual time scales. Heading is chosen before motion, or when the machine is
stopped. Steering and speed are chosen every tenth of a second. Plate speed stays on the row and
uses the realised ground speed, not the speed the plan hoped for a second earlier. Using the request
instead of the realisation bunches seeds at the start of a swath whenever the wheels are still slow.

Work along a swath is F v/ηd integrated in distance. The coefficients used here are M = 460 kg,
Crr = 0.085, Cs = 2, γ = 1.15 and ηd = 0.70. They are chosen so that an eight-percent grade changes
work by several percent, the same order as published direction savings, without copying a percentage
from another field [5]. Drag at these speeds is about a newton and does not decide the heading.
    Turns are not free. Each swath ends in a turn of radius 4.2 m, driven at 0.8 m/s, with an extra
eight percent on the force. A heading with short rows buys a lower grade factor and pays more
turns. The distance spent accelerating at each end of a row, at 0.7 m/s2 , is charged with the meter’s
accelerated miss model rather than with the steady model. Short rows therefore hurt singulation only
when that fraction becomes large. At 1.5 m/s on a 180 m row the fraction is under two percent. On
an 80 m row it is about four percent. Heading is an energy and time decision inside the singulation
plateau, and a singulation decision only near the plateau’s edge.
    The failure term mixes misses, half the multiples, and a depth survival exp(−σz2 /(2 · 6.52 )) with σz
in millimetres. The half-weight treats a multiple as wasted seed that is not always a total loss. The
survival curve encodes a narrow depth band. Both are estimation choices, not a measured germination
function. They are the same function for every plan, so a ranking is not an artefact of a special penalty
on one baseline.
    Candidates are discarded before the scalar cost is compared if the modelled quality-of-feed index
is below 0.955 or the depth root-mean-square error is above 4 mm, and if the speed lies in the resonant
interval from 2.15 to 2.55 m/s. The scalar cost then adds work, a time weight of 0.55 MJ per hour,
and a failure weight that makes a failed placement expensive beside the few tenths of a megajoule
that separate headings. Changing the weights moves the choice among feasible plans. It does not
move the filter.


3    Search and tracker
Headings are searched from uphill to contour. Speeds are searched in steps of 0.1 m/s inside the meter’s
feasible set. For each pair the work, time, accelerated fraction and failure probability are closed forms.
The swath joins are then smoothed so curvature and curvature rate stay inside the steering limits [8].
If no speed profile on the join keeps the repair fraction at or above 0.85, the headland is lengthened.
Unplanted edge is counted as missed seed, so a planner cannot hide a singulation problem by calling
it headland.
    The tracker linearises the kinematic bicycle, plus longitudinal acceleration, about the reference
every 100 ms [12, 8]. The horizon is 1.5 s, fifteen steps. Lateral error is weighted more than longitudinal
error, because a drifted row plants the neighbour’s strip, whereas a lag along the row is removed by
the plate once the realised speed is known. Speed error in the tracking cost is light. Speed is already
constrained. The hard constraints are the feasible set, acceleration and steering limits, the steering
rate, and the repair-fraction inequality. A quadratic bowl penalises speed inside the resonant interval

    A feasible reference in the interior of the constraints, with slip smaller than the margin between

a hard constraint. It arrives about one flight time late, which is the wrong signal to require at the
current step.
    A prescription that shortens the spacing also shortens the hole period and therefore shrinks the
feasible set. The search lowers speed on the dense patch before the tracker runs. It does not rotate
the field heading for a constraint a speed change already meets. The plate never receives a speed
command that bypasses the repair test.


4    Estimation on one rectangle
The field is 80 m across the slope and 180 m along it, so a contour heading has short rows and an
uphill heading has long ones. The grade is eight percent. The area is 1.44 ha. Three plans use the
same work model and the same flexure. The first is an open-loop plate at 1.6 m/s straight uphill.
The second is the same heading at 2.4 m/s, on the timber resonance. The third is the search result.
     Table 1 is the comparison. Work falls from 4.78 MJ to 4.42 MJ, about 7.7%. Time rises from
1.22 h to 1.41 h. The failed-placement fraction falls from 0.56 to 0.07. The resonant plan is the fastest
and, under the depth curve, agronomically empty: the open-loop depth error at 2.4 m/s is about
22 mm. Most of the index gain is available from the metering loop alone, on a fixed uphill heading,
at a feasible speed. The coverage layer moves that same metering performance onto a heading that
costs less work. It keeps the gain only because it is forbidden to spend the saving on a higher speed.
     At 1.5 m/s the uphill heading costs 4.78 MJ and the 55◦ heading 4.42 MJ. The pure contour costs
4.45 MJ and takes longer, because 75 turns replace 34. The search therefore stops short of the contour.
The turn term, not a preference for oblique driving as such, breaks the tie. Turn the rectangle so the
long side lies on the contour and both work and time prefer the contour: about 4.16 MJ and 1.37 h
against about 5.12 MJ and 1.53 h uphill, at the same 1.4 m/s. The speed ceiling does not move. The
heading result is a property of which side is long.
     The 7.7% gap is a little below the 9.5 to 15.6% direction savings reported on other seeding fields
[5]. This rectangle is a moderate grade with a slip term that does not dominate rolling resistance. In
energy units the prize is about 0.10 kWh on 1.44 ha. The singulation and depth columns are where the
plan changes the operation by a factor rather than by a fraction. A contract that pays for time alone
prefers the open-loop uphill run. A contract that pays for established plants prefers the constrained
plan even before the energy line is read [16].


5    A headland, and the tracker cost
The stage cost is tracking error plus a light input penalty, a weight on the failure probability at
the predicted speed and acceleration, and the resonant bowl. Lateral error dominates the tracking
term. On a straight swath at 1.4 m/s with a 1.6 m wheelbase, a steering step of 0.05 rad changes

heading by about 0.004 rad in one 100 ms sample and lateral position by well under a centimetre on
the next sample. The horizon is long enough to see a growing lateral error and short enough that
the linearisation is recomputed before the vehicle has wrapped a turn inside one linear model. The
turn itself lives in the smoothed reference. Freezing the linearisation for a whole headland would be
a linear controller about the wrong point.
    Consider the brake from 1.4 m/s to the turn speed 0.8 m/s at 0.7 m/s2 . The distance required
is (1.42 − 0.82 )/(2 · 0.7) = 0.94 m, about eight to ten tracker steps, inside the 1.5 s horizon. The
repair fraction at that speed and acceleration remains 1, because the hole period is 0.143 s and the
acceleration surcharge is only about 8 ms. A pure time-optimal tracker would postpone the brake and
then brake harder. A 1.2 m/s2 brake saves a fraction of a second and drops the repair fraction toward
0.7. The hard constraint forbids that brake. During an unplanted headland the plate is inhibited
rather than asked to singulate with the boot out of the soil. On the next swath the depth integral is
released, and the plate follows realised speed so the first metre is not bunched.
    If a solve exceeds the 40 ms budget inside the 100 ms sample, the tracker applies the next move of
the previous feasible solution. A hold remains feasible. An infeasible scramble does not. The warm
start is the shifted previous solution with the terminal linear-quadratic move appended.


6    What moves the plan, and what does not
Per hectare the constrained plan uses about 3.1 MJ and about 0.98 h, with a failed-placement fraction
of 0.07. The open-loop uphill plan at 1.6 m/s uses about 3.3 MJ and about 0.85 h, with a failed-
placement fraction of 0.56. The energy prize on the whole field is about 0.10 kWh. It is a direction
effect of a few percent on a moderate grade, consistent in kind with published direction savings and
smaller than those reported on steeper sample fields. The factor-of-eight drop in the failure fraction
is the reason to accept the extra quarter of an hour. That factor inherits the depth curve. A species
that tolerates 7 mm of depth error would shrink the gap. A species as shallow as the spinifex optimum
near 5 mm would enlarge it and would also tighten the 4 mm ceiling.
    Three specification changes move different columns, which is how √ the two technologies stay sepa-
rable. Doubling the timber stiffness raises the natural frequency by 2 and pushes resonance out past
the speeds a planter is likely to ask for. The latency cut remains. A late detector, with inference at
40 ms instead of 14 ms, shrinks the plateau from the right and the search returns a slower speed, or no
plan if the plateau disappears. Doubling the grade widens the work gap between uphill and oblique
headings and does not move the metering table. A field trial that found a larger energy difference
and the same speed ceiling would be confirming that split. A trial that found no resonant peak would
recompute the forbidden interval from the measured frequency. It would not delete the interval after
one smooth run.
    The rotated rectangle, long side on the contour, is the check that 55◦ is not a universal answer.
There the contour wins both work and time, and the search agrees with a pure energy planner. The
speed remains 1.4 m/s because nothing in the rotation changes the delay or the natural frequency.
Heading is a property of which side is long. The speed ceiling travels.


7    Conclusion
Singulation-constrained coverage chooses heading and speed inside the set a vision meter can still
repair, and it keeps the timber resonance out of steady running. On the estimation rectangle the
selected plan is 55◦ from uphill at 1.4 m/s, with a small work reduction against an uphill baseline and
a large reduction in modelled failed placements. A faster resonant plan wins the clock and fails the
depth band. The figures are evaluations of a declared model. Replacing the rolling coefficient, the
delay, or the depth curve changes the numbers. It does not give the tracker a private speed set.
