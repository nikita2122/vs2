1.2  The one-sentence answer
----------------------------

The student is not cut, pruned or compressed out of the teacher. It is a separate, much
smaller network that is trained from scratch, or from ImageNet weights, to (1) predict the
true crack masks and (2) imitate the outputs and internal features of an already-trained,
frozen teacher. After training, the teacher is thrown away and only the student is
deployed. [GENERAL METHOD]

==========================================================================================
2. BACKGROUND YOU NEED FIRST
==========================================================================================

2.1  Segmentation is classification, once per pixel
---------------------------------------------------

[GENERAL METHOD] A crack segmentation network takes an image of size H x W and outputs a
mask of the same size. Each pixel gets a class, for example crack or background. So
segmentation is really H x W small classification problems solved together.

2.2  Logits, softmax and probabilities
--------------------------------------

For every pixel i, the last layer of the network outputs one raw score per class. These
scores are called logits, written z_(i,c) for class c. Logits can be any real number. To
turn them into probabilities, we use the softmax function:

        p_(i,c) = exp(z_(i,c)) / sum_{k=1..C} exp(z_(i,k))

where C is the number of classes (C = 2 for crack vs. background). The probabilities for a
pixel are all positive and add up to 1.

2.3  Hard labels vs. soft labels
--------------------------------

  - A hard label (the ground truth) says only "this pixel is crack (1)" or "this pixel is
    background (0)".
  - A soft label is a probability, for example "this pixel is 70% crack, 30% background".
    A trained teacher produces soft labels. They carry extra information: how confident
    the teacher is, which pixels are ambiguous (blurred crack edges, hairline cracks,
    stains that look like cracks), and how similar the classes are. Hinton et al. (2015)
    call this extra information "dark knowledge".

This difference is the whole reason knowledge distillation works.

==========================================================================================
3. WHAT KNOWLEDGE DISTILLATION IS, AND WHY USE IT
==========================================================================================

3.1  Definition
---------------

[GENERAL METHOD] Knowledge distillation (KD) is a training technique. A small model (the
student) is trained to reproduce the behaviour of a large, accurate model (the teacher).
It was popularised by Hinton, Vinyals & Dean (2015, the paper's ref. 42). Gou et al.
(2021, ref. 28) survey it in detail.

3.2  Why it is needed for crack segmentation
--------------------------------------------

[CONFIRMED] The paper's goal, stated in its title, is a lightweight crack segmentation
network. [GENERAL METHOD] The usual motivation is this:

  1. Large models (for example transformers) segment cracks accurately, but they are slow
     and memory-hungry. That makes them hard to run on drones, inspection robots, phones
     or embedded boards.
  2. Small models (for example MobileNet-based CNNs) are fast, but when trained only on
     labels they are less accurate. This is especially true on thin cracks and crack
     boundaries.
  3. KD closes part of this gap. The small model gets a richer training signal from the
     teacher, so it ends up more accurate than the same small model trained alone, while
     it keeps its small size and high speed.

3.3  The three kinds of "knowledge"
-----------------------------------

The KD survey (Gou et al., 2021) groups the transferable knowledge into three families:

+----------------+-----------------------------------+-----------------------------------+
| Family         | What is copied                    | Example in segmentation           |
+================+===================================+===================================+
| Response-based | The teacher's final outputs (soft | Per-pixel soft crack              |
|                | logits)                           | probabilities (Hinton-style KD)   |
| Feature-based  | The teacher's intermediate        | Matching encoder/decoder features |
|                | feature maps                      | (FitNets-style hints, Knowledge   |
|                |                                   | Review, PKD)                      |
| Relation-based | Relations between pixels,         | Pairwise pixel similarity,        |
|                | channels or samples               | correlation structure             |
+----------------+-----------------------------------+-----------------------------------+

The survey also groups training schemes. In offline KD, which is the most common, the
teacher is trained first and then frozen. In online KD, teacher and student are trained
together. In self-distillation, a network teaches itself. [LIKELY] This paper most likely
uses offline KD, the standard setting.

==========================================================================================
4. THE TEACHER MODEL
==========================================================================================

4.1  Role of the teacher
------------------------

[GENERAL METHOD] The teacher is a large, high-capacity, accurate model. Its only job is to
be a good "source of knowledge" during the student's training. Its speed does not matter,
because it is never deployed.

4.2  Likely architecture: Swin Transformer + UPerNet
----------------------------------------------------

[LIKELY] The paper's reference list includes:

  - Swin Transformer (Liu et al., 2021; ref. 30). This is a hierarchical vision
    transformer that computes self-attention inside shifted local windows.
  - Layer Normalization (ref. 31) and GELU (ref. 32). These are the normalisation and
    activation functions used inside Swin blocks.
  - Feature Pyramid Networks (ref. 33) and UPerNet / Unified Perceptual Parsing (ref. 47).
    UPerNet is the decoder the Swin authors used for semantic segmentation.

Together, these strongly suggest a Swin Transformer backbone with an UPerNet (FPN +
pyramid pooling) decoder as the teacher. This is the standard "Swin + UPerNet" segmenter.
A transformer suits a crack teacher because self-attention captures long-range context,
and cracks are long, thin and continuous structures.

For a sense of scale, the Swin paper itself reports these sizes for its UPerNet segmenters
on ADE20K. These are the Swin authors' numbers, not this paper's numbers.

+----------------------------------------------+------------+------------------------+
| Teacher candidate (from Swin paper, Table 3) | Parameters | FLOPs (512×2048 input) |
+==============================================+============+========================+
| UPerNet + Swin-T                             | 60 M       | 945 G                  |
| UPerNet + Swin-S                             | 81 M       | 1038 G                 |
| UPerNet + Swin-B                             | 121 M      | 1841 G                 |
+----------------------------------------------+------------+------------------------+

4.3  How the teacher is trained, then frozen
--------------------------------------------

[GENERAL METHOD]

  1. Initialise the Swin backbone, usually with ImageNet pre-trained weights.
  2. Train the teacher normally on the crack dataset with a standard segmentation loss
     (cross-entropy and/or Dice) until it converges.
  3. Freeze it: put it in evaluation mode, turn off gradient computation, and never update
     its weights again.

From this point on, the teacher is a fixed function. It only provides targets for the
student.

==========================================================================================
5. THE STUDENT MODEL
==========================================================================================

5.1  Role of the student
------------------------

The student is the model the paper actually delivers: the "lightweight crack segmentation
network" of the title [CONFIRMED]. It must be small and fast, and as accurate as possible.

5.2  Likely architecture: MobileNet-style CNN
---------------------------------------------

[LIKELY] The paper cites MobileNets (Howard et al., 2017; ref. 35) and MobileNetV2
(Sandler et al., 2018; ref. 34). It also cites classic segmentation designs: FCN (ref.
45), U-Net (ref. 46) and DeepLabv3+ (ref. 48). The student is therefore very likely a
MobileNet(V2)-based encoder with a light decoder, possibly in an FCN/U-Net/DeepLabv3+
style. For reference, the MobileNetV2 classification backbone has about 3.4 M parameters
and 300 M multiply-adds at 224×224 (Sandler et al., 2018, Table 4). Again, this is not the
paper's number.

5.3  The student is NOT pruned from the teacher
-----------------------------------------------

This is the most common misunderstanding, so it is worth stating clearly. [GENERAL METHOD]

  - Pruning removes weights or channels from an existing network. The result is a thinner
    version of the same network.
  - Distillation trains a different, independently designed network. The teacher (a
    transformer) and the student (a CNN) can have completely different architectures. No
    weights are copied from the teacher to the student. Only training signals (soft
    outputs and features) flow from teacher to student.

The student's weights start either randomly or from ImageNet pre-training of its own
backbone. They are never initialised from the teacher.

6. WHAT KNOWLEDGE IS TRANSFERRED, AND HOW (WITH EQUATIONS)
==========================================================================================

Figure 1 shows the whole picture. The same image goes into both networks. The frozen
teacher produces soft outputs and features. The student is pushed to match them, and also
to match the ground truth.

    ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
                                  [ Crack image ]
                                   |           |
                 +-----------------+           +-----------------+
                 v                                               v
  +------------------------------+              +------------------------------+
  | TEACHER (frozen, no grads)   |              | STUDENT (trainable)          |
  | likely Swin Transformer      |              | likely MobileNet-style       |
  | + UPerNet decoder            |              | encoder + light decoder      |
  | large, accurate, slow        |              | small, fast                  |
  +------------------------------+              +------------------------------+
     | features F^t   | logits z^t                 | features F^s | logits z^s
     |                |                            v              |
     |                |                    [ 1x1 adapter phi ]    |
     |                |                            |              |
     v                v                            v              v
  +------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
  | (A) RESPONSE LOSS  L_KD   = T^2 * KL( softmax(z^t/T) || softmax(z^s/T) )     |
  | (B) FEATURE LOSS   L_feat = d( phi(F^s), F^t )   (MSE / Pearson / Review)    |
  | (C) SEGMENT. LOSS  L_seg  = CE / Dice( student prediction, ground-truth mask )|
  +------------------------------------------------------------------------------+

6.1  Response (logit) distillation with temperature
---------------------------------------------------

[GENERAL METHOD] This is Hinton et al.'s (2015) original idea (ref. 42), applied
independently at every pixel.

Step 1: soften both networks' outputs with a temperature T >= 1. Let z^t_(i,c) and
z^s_(i,c) be the teacher's and student's logits at pixel i for class c:

        p^t_(i,c)(T) = exp(z^t_(i,c) / T) / sum_{k=1..C} exp(z^t_(i,k) / T)
        p^s_(i,c)(T) = exp(z^s_(i,c) / T) / sum_{k=1..C} exp(z^s_(i,k) / T)

  - With T = 1 you get the normal softmax.
  - With a larger T, the probabilities become softer, meaning closer to each other. The
    teacher's small differences (its "dark knowledge") become visible instead of being
    squashed to 0 or 1.

Step 2: measure how different the two distributions are, using the Kullback–Leibler (KL)
divergence at each pixel:

        KL( p^t_i || p^s_i )
            = sum_{c=1..C} p^t_(i,c)(T) * log( p^t_(i,c)(T) / p^s_(i,c)(T) )

KL is 0 when the student's soft output exactly equals the teacher's, and positive
otherwise. Note the direction: the teacher is the target distribution.

Step 3: average over all pixels and multiply by T^2:

        L_KD = (T^2 / N) * sum_{i=1..N} KL( p^t_i(T) || p^s_i(T) ),
               N = H x W (x batch size)

Why multiply by T^2? Dividing logits by T makes the gradients of the KL term shrink by
roughly 1/T^2. Hinton et al. multiply by T^2 so that the KD term keeps about the same
strength whatever temperature you choose. Then you can change T without re-tuning the loss
weight. Section 11 shows this with numbers.

Why it helps (intuition). The gradient of KL with respect to the student's logit is simply

        d KL / d z^s_(i,c) = (1/T) * ( p^s_(i,c)(T) - p^t_(i,c)(T) )

So at every pixel, the student is pushed toward exactly the teacher's level of confidence.
That includes "the teacher is only 60% sure this edge pixel is crack". A hard label cannot
say this.

Binary (sigmoid) version. If the network outputs a single crack logit z_i per pixel, the
same idea uses a tempered sigmoid q_i = sigmoid(z_i/T) = 1 / (1 + exp(-z_i/T)) and binary
cross-entropy (or binary KL) between teacher and student:

        L_KD_bin = (T^2 / N) * sum_i [ q^t_i * log(q^t_i / q^s_i)
                                      + (1 - q^t_i) * log((1 - q^t_i) / (1 - q^s_i)) ],
           where q_i = sigmoid(z_i / T)

6.2  Feature (intermediate) distillation
----------------------------------------

[GENERAL METHOD] Matching only the final output is a weak signal for a deep network.
Feature distillation gives the student "hints" in its middle layers: its intermediate
feature maps are trained to resemble the teacher's. This idea goes back to FitNets (Romero
et al., 2015), which this paper does not cite.

A generic feature-distillation loss for matched stages l = 1,...,L is:

        L_feat = sum_{l=1..L} (1 / |F^t_l|) * || phi_l(F^s_l) - F^t_l ||_2^2

where

  - F^s_l and F^t_l are the student's and teacher's feature maps at stage l;
  - phi_l is a small adapter, typically a 1 x 1 convolution (sometimes with BatchNorm),
    plus resizing if needed. It maps the student's channel count and resolution to the
    teacher's. The adapter is trained together with the student and discarded after
    training;
  - |F^t_l| is the number of elements, so the loss is a mean squared error.

Why this is harder for a transformer teacher and a CNN student. The two are heterogeneous:
their features have different channel counts, value ranges (magnitudes) and statistics.
Plain MSE can then be dominated by the scale mismatch rather than by the actual "content".
The two methods the paper cites, described next, address exactly this. [LIKELY]

6.3  Variant A: Knowledge Review (Chen et al., 2021; ref. 36)
-------------------------------------------------------------

[GENERAL METHOD] In standard feature KD, stage l of the student copies stage l of the
teacher. Knowledge Review observes that a student's deeper stage should also learn from
the teacher's shallower stages ("reviewing" old knowledge). To make this efficient, it:

  1. Fuses student features from deep to shallow with an Attention-Based Fusion (ABF)
     module. ABF merges the current-stage feature with the already-fused deeper feature,
     using learned spatial attention maps.
  2. Compares each fused student feature with the teacher feature of the same level, using
     a Hierarchical Context Loss (HCL). HCL spatially pyramid-pools both feature maps at
     several scales and sums the L_2 distances at every scale. This transfers both fine
     detail and global context.

Schematically:

        L_review = sum_{j=1..n} HCL( ABF(F^s_j, ..., F^s_n), F^t_j )
        HCL(A, B) = sum_k || pool_k(A) - pool_k(B) ||_2^2

6.4  Variant B: Pearson-correlation feature distillation (PKD, Cao et al., 2022; ref. 43)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

[GENERAL METHOD] PKD was designed for heterogeneous teacher–student pairs, where feature
magnitudes differ. It normalises each channel of the student and teacher features to zero
mean and unit variance, computed over the batch and spatial positions. Then it minimises
the MSE between the normalised features:

        s_hat = (s - mu_s) / sigma_s        t_hat = (t - mu_t) / sigma_t
        L_PKD = (1/m) * sum_{i=1..m} (s_hat_i - t_hat_i)^2

This is mathematically equivalent to maximising the Pearson correlation coefficient r
between the raw features, because (1/m) * sum_i (s_hat_i - t_hat_i)^2 = 2 * (1 - r). So
the student learns the pattern of the teacher's activations (where they are high and where
they are low) without having to copy their scale. That makes it a natural fit for a Swin
teacher and a MobileNet student.

6.5  The supervised (ground-truth) segmentation loss
----------------------------------------------------

[GENERAL METHOD] The student also learns from the real masks y_(i,c) in {0,1}, at normal
temperature T=1.

Cross-entropy:

        L_CE = -(1/N) * sum_{i=1..N} sum_{c=1..C} y_(i,c) * log p^s_(i,c)

Dice loss. This loss is useful because crack pixels are rare (often only a few percent of
the image), so plain CE can be dominated by background:

        L_Dice = 1 - ( 2 * sum_i p^s_i * y_i + eps ) / ( sum_i p^s_i + sum_i y_i + eps )

where p^s_i is the student's crack probability, y_i the true crack label, and eps a small
constant. A common choice is L_seg = L_CE + L_Dice, or a weighted combination of the two.

==========================================================================================
7. THE TOTAL LOSS: PUTTING IT TOGETHER
==========================================================================================

[GENERAL METHOD] The student is trained on a weighted sum of the three signals:

        L_total = lambda1 * L_seg(p^s, y)
                + lambda2 * L_KD(p^t(T), p^s(T))
                + lambda3 * L_feat(F^s, F^t)

Hinton's original formulation often writes the first two terms as (1-alpha) L_seg + alpha
L_KD with alpha in [0,1]. This is the same idea with different notation.

    ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
  WHAT IS COMPARED             LOSS TERM                           WEIGHT
  ---------------------------  ----------------------------------  -----------
  Student prediction vs.   --> L_seg  = L_CE + L_Dice              x lambda1 --+
  ground truth                 ("learn the right answer")                      |
                                                                               |
  Student vs. teacher      --> L_KD   = T^2 * KL(p^t_T || p^s_T)   x lambda2 --+-> L_total
  softened outputs             ("learn how sure to be")                        |
                                                                               |
  Student vs. teacher      --> L_feat = sum d(phi(F^s), F^t)       x lambda3 --+
  intermediate features        ("learn how to see")

          (the weights lambda and the temperature T: CHECK IN PAPER)
    ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
    Figure 2. How the total training loss of the student is composed (general form).

+---------------------------+-----------------------------+------------------------------+
| Symbol                    | Meaning                     | Typical role                 |
+===========================+=============================+==============================+
| L_seg                     | Student vs. ground truth    | Keeps the student correct;   |
|                           |                             | the teacher can be wrong     |
| L_KD                      | Student vs. teacher soft    | Transfers confidence / dark  |
|                           | outputs                     | knowledge                    |
| L_feat                    | Student vs. teacher         | Transfers internal           |
|                           | features                    | representations ("how to     |
|                           |                             | see")                        |
| lambda1, lambda2, lambda3 | Loss weights                | Balance labels vs. teacher;  |
|                           |                             | tuned experimentally         |
| T                         | Temperature                 | Controls softness of the     |
|                           |                             | outputs; used only in L_KD   |
+---------------------------+-----------------------------+------------------------------+

Why keep the ground-truth loss? The teacher is not perfect. If the student learned only
from the teacher, it would copy the teacher's mistakes. The label term anchors the student
to the truth, and the teacher terms add extra guidance on top.

8. STEP-BY-STEP TRAINING PROCEDURE
==========================================================================================

[GENERAL METHOD] with details marked [LIKELY] where they come from the reference list.

  1. Prepare the data. Split the crack images and masks into train/validation/test sets,
     and apply data augmentation such as flips, rotations, crops and colour jitter.
     [LIKELY] The paper cites a concrete crack segmentation dataset (Özgenel et al., ref.
     39) and an augmentation survey (Shorten & Khoshgoftaar, ref. 40).
  2. Stage 1: train the teacher. Train the Swin+UPerNet teacher on the crack data with
     L_seg only. Keep the best checkpoint.
  3. Freeze the teacher. Set it to evaluation mode and disable gradients. Its weights will
     not change again.
  4. Build the student. Create the MobileNet-based student, initialised randomly or from
     ImageNet weights, plus small adapters phi_l for every feature pair that will be
     distilled.
  5. Stage 2: distillation training loop. For each mini-batch:
      a. Feed the images to the teacher (forward pass only, no gradients). Store its
         logits z^t and features F^t.
      b. Feed the same images to the student. Get its logits z^s and features F^s.
      c. Compute L_seg (student vs. masks), L_KD (softened student vs. softened teacher)
         and L_feat (adapted student features vs. teacher features).
      d. Combine them into L_total and back-propagate.
      e. Update only the student and the adapters with the optimiser. [LIKELY] The
         optimiser is AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, ref. 38) and the framework is PyTorch
         (ref. 37).
  6. Validate the student on the validation set (mIoU, F1, precision/recall) and keep the
     best checkpoint.
  7. Compare. A good KD paper reports at least three models: the teacher, the student
     trained without KD (baseline), and the student trained with KD. The KD gain is the
     difference between the last two. [LIKELY] The paper also cites Grad-CAM (ref. 49),
     probably to visualise where the student "looks".
  8. Deploy only the student. Discard the teacher and the adapters.

8.1  Pseudocode (PyTorch-style)
-------------------------------

    ------------------------------------------------------------
    # ---------- Stage 1: teacher (trained beforehand) ----------
    teacher = SwinUPerNet(...)            # likely; check paper
    teacher.load_state_dict(torch.load("teacher_best.pth"))
    teacher.eval()
    for p in teacher.parameters():
        p.requires_grad = False          # FREEZE: teacher never updates
    
    # ---------- Stage 2: student + adapters ----------
    student  = MobileNetSeg(...)          # likely; check paper
    adapters = nn.ModuleList([nn.Conv2d(c_s, c_t, 1) for c_s, c_t in pairs])
    opt = torch.optim.AdamW(list(student.parameters()) +
                            list(adapters.parameters()), lr=LR)   # LR: check paper
    
    T, lam1, lam2, lam3 = T_PAPER, L1, L2, L3                     # check paper
    
    for images, masks in train_loader:
        with torch.no_grad():                         # no gradients for teacher
            t_logits, t_feats = teacher(images, return_features=True)
    
        s_logits, s_feats = student(images, return_features=True)
    
        # 1) supervised loss (T = 1)
        L_seg = cross_entropy(s_logits, masks) + dice_loss(s_logits, masks)
    
        # 2) response distillation (per pixel, softened by T)
        log_ps = F.log_softmax(s_logits / T, dim=1)   # dim=1 is the class axis
        pt     = F.softmax(t_logits / T, dim=1)
        L_kd   = F.kl_div(log_ps, pt, reduction="none").sum(1).mean() * T * T
    
        # 3) feature distillation (e.g. MSE or Pearson-normalised MSE)
        L_feat = 0
        for a, fs, ft in zip(adapters, s_feats, t_feats):
            fs = F.interpolate(a(fs), size=ft.shape[-2:], mode="bilinear")
            L_feat += feature_loss(fs, ft)            # mse / pkd / review-style
    
        loss = lam1 * L_seg + lam2 * L_kd + lam3 * L_feat
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()  # updates STUDENT (+adapters)
    
    torch.save(student.state_dict(), "student_final.pth")   # teacher discarded
    ------------------------------------------------------------

The key lines are torch.no_grad() and requires_grad = False for the teacher. They are what
make the teacher a fixed source of targets.

[GENERAL METHOD] The teacher, the temperature, the adapters and all losses exist only
during training. At inference (deployment) the student runs alone, at normal temperature,
and outputs the crack mask.

                +--------------------+
           +--->| Teacher (frozen)   |---- knowledge ---+
           |    +--------------------+                  v

==========================================================================================
10. WHY THE STUDENT IS SMALLER AND FASTER
==========================================================================================

[GENERAL METHOD] At inference, the cost is entirely the student's. The student is cheaper
for three reasons.

10.1  Depthwise-separable convolutions (MobileNet)
--------------------------------------------------

A standard k x k convolution from C_in to C_out channels on an H x W feature map costs:

        Params_std = k^2 * C_in * C_out
        FLOPs_std  ~ k^2 * C_in * C_out * H * W

MobileNet splits it into a depthwise k x k convolution (one filter per input channel)
followed by a pointwise 1 x 1 convolution (which mixes channels):

        Params_dw = k^2 * C_in + C_in * C_out
        cost_dw / cost_std = 1/C_out + 1/k^2

    ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
  STANDARD CONV (one big step)
  +--------------------------+
  | k x k x C_in x C_out     |      params = k^2 * C_in * C_out
  +--------------------------+

  DEPTHWISE-SEPARABLE CONV (MobileNet building block) = two cheap steps
  +--------------------------+        +--------------------------+
  | Depthwise k x k,         |  then  | Pointwise 1 x 1,         |
  | one filter per channel   | -----> | C_in x C_out (mixes the  |
  | (C_in filters)           |        | channels)                |
  +--------------------------+        +--------------------------+
   params = k^2 * C_in                 params = C_in * C_out
    ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
    Figure 4. Standard convolution vs. depthwise-separable convolution.

Worked example. Take k = 3, C_in=C_out=256 and a 64 x 64 feature map:

+---------------------+----------------------------+---------------+
|                     | Parameters                 | Multiply-adds |
+=====================+============================+===============+
| Standard 3×3 conv   | 3·3·256·256 = 589,824      | ≈ 2.42 G      |
| Depthwise-separable | 3·3·256 + 256·256 = 67,840 | ≈ 0.28 G      |
| Ratio               | ≈ 8.7× smaller             | ≈ 8.7× fewer  |
+---------------------+----------------------------+---------------+

This matches the "8 to 9 times" saving reported by the MobileNet papers (refs. 34, 35).
MobileNetV2 adds inverted residual blocks with linear bottlenecks, which keep the
expensive channel width only inside the blocks.

10.2  No (or much less) self-attention
--------------------------------------

Swin's window self-attention on an h x w map with C channels and window size M costs (Liu
et al., 2021, Eq. 2):

        Omega(W-MSA) = 4*h*w*C^2 + 2*M^2*h*w*C

That is on top of its MLP layers, at four stages, with wide channels, plus a heavy UPerNet
decoder. A MobileNet student avoids most of this.

10.3  Fewer channels, layers and a lighter decoder
--------------------------------------------------

The student is designed with narrower layers and a simpler decoder, so its parameter count
and FLOPs are typically one or two orders of magnitude below a Swin+UPerNet teacher.
Compare roughly 60–120 M parameters for the teacher candidates in Section 4 with a few
million for MobileNet-class models.

The key point: KD does not make the student faster. The student's architecture makes it
fast. KD makes the fast student more accurate than it would be when trained alone.

11. WORKED TOY EXAMPLE: WHAT TEMPERATURE DOES
==========================================================================================

[GENERAL METHOD] These numbers are invented purely to illustrate the mechanics. They are
not from the paper.

Consider one pixel on a blurry crack edge. The classes are (crack, background).

  - Teacher logits: z^t = (2.0, 0.5). The teacher thinks "probably crack".
  - Student logits early in training: z^s = (0.5, 1.0). The student wrongly leans toward
    background.

11.1  Step 1: soften with different temperatures
------------------------------------------------

For T = 1: p^t = ( e^2.0/(e^2.0+e^0.5), e^0.5/(e^2.0+e^0.5) ) = ( 7.389/(7.389+1.649),
1.649/9.038 ) = (0.818, 0.182).

+---------------+----------------------+-----------------------+----------------+--------+
| Temperature T | Teacher p^t (crack,  | Student p^s (crack,   | KL(p^t || p^s) | T^2*KL |
|               | bg)                  | bg)                   |                |        |
+===============+======================+=======================+================+========+
| 1             | (0.818, 0.182)       | (0.378, 0.622)        | 0.408          | 0.408  |
| 2             | (0.679, 0.321)       | (0.438, 0.562)        | 0.118          | 0.473  |
| 4             | (0.593, 0.407)       | (0.469, 0.531)        | 0.031          | 0.493  |
| 8             | (0.547, 0.453)       | (0.484, 0.516)        | 0.008          | 0.498  |
+---------------+----------------------+-----------------------+----------------+--------+

    ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
  Teacher probabilities for the toy pixel (crack logit 2.0, background logit 0.5)
  (each # = 0.025)

          P(crack)                                  P(background)
  T = 1   ################################# 0.818   ####### 0.182
  T = 2   ########################### 0.679         ############# 0.321
  T = 4   ######################## 0.593            ################ 0.407
  T = 8   ###################### 0.547              ################## 0.453
    ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
    Figure 5. Teacher probabilities for the toy pixel at increasing temperature.

11.2  Step 2: what to notice
----------------------------

  1. Higher T gives softer targets. At T=1 the teacher says 82% crack. At T=4 it says 59%
     crack. The ranking (crack > background) is preserved, but the relative information
     becomes easier for the student to match.
  2. Raw KL shrinks as T grows (0.408 → 0.008). Without a correction, the KD term would
     almost vanish at high T.
  3. Multiplying by T^2 fixes this. T^2*KL stays around 0.41–0.50 for every T. This is
     exactly why Hinton et al. scale the loss by T^2.
  4. Compare with a confident pixel. For an obvious background pixel with teacher logits
     (-2, 6), the softmax at T=1 gives (0.0003, 0.9997), essentially a hard label carrying
     no extra information. At T = 4 it gives (0.119, 0.881), which still says
     "background", but now with a usable gradient signal.
  5. Gradient direction. With T=1 the gradient on the student's crack logit is (p^s - p^t)
     = 0.378 - 0.818 = -0.44. Gradient descent therefore increases the student's crack
     logit, moving it toward the teacher's belief.

12. COMMON CONFUSIONS (FAQ)
==========================================================================================

Q1. Is the student a smaller copy, or a pruned version, of the teacher?
No. It is a separately designed network (likely a CNN, while the teacher is likely a
transformer). No weights are copied. Only training targets flow from teacher to student.

Q2. Are the teacher and student trained at the same time?
In standard (offline) KD, no. The teacher is fully trained first and then frozen. During
student training the teacher only does forward passes. Check in paper whether this is
offline KD.

Q3. Does the teacher's accuracy limit the student?
Mostly, yes: a student rarely beats its teacher. But the student is still trained on the
ground truth too, so it is not forced to copy every teacher error. Occasionally a student
slightly exceeds its teacher, because the soft targets act as a regulariser.

Q4. Why not just train the small network on the labels?
You can, and that is the "student without KD" baseline. KD adds a richer signal (soft
confidences and feature hints), which typically improves accuracy at zero extra inference
cost.

Q5. Is the temperature used at test time?
No. T appears only inside L_KD during training. At inference the student uses its normal
output (T=1, or simply argmax/threshold).

Q6. What is the "dark knowledge" when there are only two classes?
With crack vs. background there is no "which wrong class is more similar" information. The
dark knowledge is mainly per-pixel confidence: uncertain boundaries, faint hairline
cracks, crack-like stains. Feature distillation adds the rest.

Q7. Why are adapters (1×1 convs) needed?
The teacher's and student's feature maps usually have different numbers of channels (and
possibly resolutions), so they cannot be subtracted directly. The adapter projects student
features into the teacher's space. It is thrown away after training.

Q8. Why do Pearson/PKD or Knowledge Review matter for a transformer → CNN pair?
The two architectures produce features with very different magnitudes and statistics. PKD
normalises them so that only the pattern is matched. Knowledge Review lets each student
stage learn from multiple teacher levels with multi-scale context.

Q9. Does KD make the network faster?
No. Speed comes from the student's architecture. KD only improves the student's accuracy.

Q10. What exactly is "obtained" at the end?
A set of student weights (one checkpoint file). It is the trained lightweight network,
which is used on its own.
