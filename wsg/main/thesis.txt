                                         Abstract


High-gravity brewing ferments a concentrated beer and then blends it, in line, with deaer-

ated dilution water to the original extract required by the brand. On the blending skid

considered in this thesis the actuators are three linear control valves: one on the high-

gravity beer, one on the dilution water, and one on the outlet of the blend header. The

measurements that are actually available for fast control are the liquid speeds in the three

lines and the supply and header pressures. The original-extract analyser sits downstream

of the mix point, behind a transport delay of about ten seconds.

   Two control difficulties follow from that arrangement. The beer valve and the water

valve are coupled by the header pressure, and the flow gain of each linear valve changes

with the square root of the pressure drop, so a fixed-gain controller loses authority when

a supply pressure falls. The extract loop, if it is closed on the analyser alone, is too slow

to keep the blend inside the usual brewing tolerance of ±0.05◦ P during a recipe change

or a supply disturbance.

   This thesis proposes two techniques inside the active-disturbance-rejection framework.

The first, pressure-manifold decoupled linear active disturbance rejection control, puts the

installed interacting gain of each inlet valve into the observer as a known, measured coef-

ficient. Supply pressure, header pressure and the steady coupling through the blend node

are thereby removed from the total disturbance, while the small off-diagonal remainder,

the relative-gain array element being only 1.11, is left for the extended state observer.

The second, valve-authority coordinated delay-compensated active disturbance rejection

control, moves the outlet valve so that both inlet stems stay inside the linear portion of

their stroke, and it closes the extract loop on a mass-balance soft sensor whose bias is

corrected by the delayed analyser.
   A first-principles numerical model of the skid, with valve stiction, sensor noise and

the analyser lag, is used to estimate the two techniques against ratio proportional–

integral control and against linear active disturbance rejection control with fixed nom-

inal gains. Over a 560 s campaign of supply steps, water-pressure oscillation, a brand

change, a throughput increase, an unmeasured drift of the mother-beer extract and a loss

of beer-valve capacity, the proposed pair reduces the integrated absolute extract error

from 81.4 °P s to 52.4 °P s and the integrated absolute beer-flow error from 316 m3 s/h to

37.5 m3 s/h. The inlet stems remain away from the fully open seat in the cases where the

fixed-gain designs saturate.

Chapter 1


Research of previous work

The blending step that closes a high-gravity brewery is a small plant with a large economic

weight. A few seconds of wrong ratio put beer of the wrong strength into a bright-beer

tank, and a valve that spends its life against the seat wears out and starts to stick. The

actuators on the line studied here are ordinary linear control valves, and the fast mea-

surements are liquid speeds and pressures. Everything that follows is an attempt to use

those measurements well. This chapter sets out what is already known, in three parts.

Section 1.1 reviews the control of coupled valve flows and the disturbance-rejection the-

ory that the first technique builds on. Section 1.2 reviews delay compensation, inferential

sensing and header-pressure coordination, which the second technique builds on. Sec-

tion 1.3 states the blending problem in a form that those two techniques can be designed

against.

1.1.1     High-gravity beer and the dilution step

High-gravity brewing is the practice of fermenting wort at an original extract above the

extract of the beer that will be sold, and of restoring the sales gravity later by adding

water [1–3]. Stewart’s account of the method puts the usual high-gravity range at 15–

20 °P, against a sales beer that may lie anywhere from a light lager near 10 °P to a full-

bodied beer near 13 °P [1]. The water added at the end of the process is carbon-filtered

and stripped of oxygen, because oxygen picked up at this point shortens shelf life. The

same idea appears in grain-whisky fermentation, where worts of 18–21 °P are diluted after

distillation rather than before it [1, 4].

   The brewing literature is mainly about yeast and flavour, and it is worth recording

what it does and does not settle. High-gravity wort stresses yeast: vacuoles enlarge, the

cell surface changes, and the uptake of maltotriose, the largest fermentable sugar, becomes

incomplete [1, 5, 6]. Foam stability falls, and the ester profile of the finished beer can

drift away from the profile of a beer brewed at sales gravity [1, 3]. Those are biological

constraints. They fix the mother beer that the blender is allowed to receive. They do not

say how the blender should move its valves when the supply pressure changes.

   What the process books do fix is the dilution arithmetic [2, 7, 8]. Original extract is a

mass fraction. If a mass flow ṁh of high-gravity beer at original extract Ph is mixed with

a mass flow ṁw of water, the original extract of the mixture is

right cascade. Its weakness on this plant is the gain inside each flow loop, not the decision

to use a ratio.

A value near one means that a decentralised controller, one loop per valve, does not fight

1.1.3     What a fixed-gain flow loop cannot absorb

Process-control practice tunes the flow loops with a proportional–integral controller [19–

22]. For a first-order valve, the SIMC rules of Skogestad give a proportional gain inversely

proportional to the process gain and an integral time equal to the valve time constant,

provided the closed-loop time constant is chosen a little slower than the positioner [21].

The rules are explicit, and they are the baseline in Chapter 3. They have two limits that

matter here.

   The first limit is the gain schedule hidden inside (1.4). SIMC is derived at one lin-

earisation. When ph falls, ∆ph falls and the process gain falls with the square root. A

proportional gain that was correct becomes too small, the loop slows down, and the stem

runs toward the open stop in an attempt to recover the flow. Once the stem is above

about 0.9, the remaining travel produces little extra flow and the positioner works in the

region where seal friction is the least repeatable [13, 23]. Bialkowski’s survey of industrial

loops put the fraction of loops that perform well below one third, and named valve friction

as a principal cause [24]. Hägglund’s performance monitor detects the same oscillation

from the controller output alone [25]. The quantitative stiction models of Choudhury,

Thornhill and Shah, and the later detection methods collected by Jelali and Huang, de-

scribe a stick band and a slip jump [23, 26, 27]. A positioner reduces the apparent band,

but it does not remove it. A controller that continually nudges a valve inside the band

produces no flow change and then a jump.

   The second limit is dynamic coupling during the transient. Even if λ11 is near one,

a fast move of the water valve changes pm before the beer loop has finished its own

correction. Ratio control on setpoints keeps the commands in proportion. It does not

keep the delivered flows in proportion during that window [10, 28]. The extract error is

exactly the error in that proportion, through (1.2).

   Model predictive control can be written for the same plant, and the industrial record of


                                              6
predictive control on constrained multivariable processes is strong [29–32]. A predictive

controller needs a model that is kept up to date, a horizon long enough to cover the

analyser delay, and a quadratic program inside the cycle time of a flow loop. On a skid

whose fast loops are flow loops with a one-second time scale, that machinery is difficult

to justify when the dominant uncertainty is a pressure that is already measured. The

disturbance-rejection route uses the measurement directly and leaves the unmeasured

remainder to an observer.



1.1.4     From disturbance accommodation to the extended state

The idea of estimating a disturbance and cancelling it is older than active disturbance re-

jection. Johnson’s disturbance-accommodating control augments the plant with a model

of a disturbance that is generated by a known exosystem, and then estimates that state

[33]. The internal-model principle of Francis and Wonham gives the corresponding struc-

tural result: a regulator that rejects a disturbance asymptotically must contain a model

of the dynamics that generate it [34]. Ohishi and co-workers built the same idea into a

disturbance observer for a motor, subtracting an estimated load torque from the current

command [35]. Schrijver and van Dijk later showed the equivalence of several of these

observers for rigid mechanical plants and gave design conditions [36]. Chen, Yang, Guo

and Li reviewed the whole family and separated the methods by what they assume about

the disturbance channel [37, 38].

   Active disturbance rejection, introduced by Han and put into bandwidth form by Gao,

makes a sharper modelling choice [39–43]. Every effect that is not the chosen nominal

chain of integrators is collected into one signal, the total disturbance, and that signal is

treated as an extra state. For a first-order loop the plant is written

The two numbers ωc and ωo replace the three PID numbers, which is the practical con-

transition from an existing PID [45, 46].

   The theoretical support is now substantial. Guo and Zhao proved convergence of the

extended state observer for a class of nonlinear uncertain systems, and collected the theory

in monograph form [47, 48]. Huang and Xue set out the methodology and the separation

between the observer time scale and the controller time scale [49]. Xue and Huang gave

performance bounds for uncertain linear plants [50]. Zheng, Gao and Gao validated the

observer experimentally and showed that the estimate z2 tracks a real load when ωo is

connected the same observer to differential flatness and demonstrated it on a permanent-

magnet motor [52, 53]. Gao’s later essay argued that disturbance rejection, rather than


                                               8
model following, is the central job of a feedback loop in industry [54]. Madonski and

co-workers rewrote the two-degree-of-freedom form as a single error-driven block that fits

an industrial function-block diagram [55].

   Two limitations of the plain observer matter for a valve.

   The gain b0 is a design choice, and a bad choice is expensive. If b0 is far from the true

b, the quantity z2 must carry (b − b0 )u as well as the physical disturbance. Fu and Tan

showed how to fold known plant information into the observer so that z2 carries only the

unknown part, and they gave tuning rules for that case [56]. Tan and Fu showed that

linear active disturbance rejection for a first-order or second-order plant is an internal-

model controller with a particular filter, which explains both its robustness and the noise

penalty of a high ωo [20, 57]. On the blending skid the dominant known part is (1.4)

evaluated at the measured pressures. Leaving that part inside f forces ωo up, and the

higher observer gain passes flow-meter noise into the stem command. Putting it into b0 (t)

is exactly the use of known plant information that Fu and Tan recommend, specialised to

a valve whose gain is a measured pressure.

   The second limitation is noise. A linear observer of bandwidth ωo differentiates, in

effect, with a gain that grows as ωo2 for the disturbance channel. Madonski and Herman

surveyed the modifications that reduce this cost [58]. Lakomy and Madonski cascade

two observers so that a high bandwidth can be used without the full noise gain of a

single high-gain observer [59]. Khalil and Praly reviewed the same trade-off for high-

gain observers in nonlinear output feedback [60, 61], and Freidovich and Khalil quantified

the performance recovery of feedback-linearising designs based on such observers [62].

Xue, Bai and co-workers adapted the observer gain on an engine air–fuel loop, where the

disturbance spectrum is not stationary [63]. The blending valves do not need a cascade

observer if b0 is relieved of the pressure term, because ωo can then stay a few times ωc

rather than an order of magnitude above it. The noise analysis is still the reason for



                                             9
that restraint, and it is the reason the outlet-valve bandwidth in the second technique is

scheduled rather than fixed at the highest stable value.

   Disturbance decoupling, as distinct from disturbance estimation, subtracts a measured

disturbance through a feedforward channel [28, 64]. Zheng, Chen and Gao gave a practical

form that does not require the plant inverse to be exact, because the feedback path removes

the residual [64]. Supply pressure and header pressure on this skid are measured. They

are the natural feedforward channels. The first technique is that feedforward, realised by

making b0 the installed gain, together with an observer for what the feedforward cannot

see.



1.1.5    The gap that the first technique addresses

The pieces reviewed above do not, by themselves, give a flow controller for this blender.

Valve handbooks give (1.4) and warn about the installed gain [13, 15]. Ratio-control texts

give the setpoint structure [10, 11]. Disturbance-rejection theory gives (1.10)–(1.12) and

the advice to use a known gain when one exists [42, 56]. Decoupling theory says that a

measured pressure should be cancelled in feedforward and that a relative gain near one

should not be inverted [16, 64]. None of these sources writes the installed gain of two

coupled sanitary valves, obtained by implicit differentiation of the blend-node balance,

into the b0 of a linear extended state observer. That is the first technique. It is a

decentralised controller on purpose. The off-diagonal gains are estimated, not inverted.

long by the standard of a flow loop.

   Smith’s predictor is the classical remedy [65]. A model of the delay-free plant is

simulated in parallel, the controller is designed as if the delay were absent, and the model

error, including the disturbance, is fed back through the delay so that the loop corrects

it. Normey-Rico and Camacho surveyed the fifty years of modifications that followed: the

filtered Smith predictor, predictors for integrating and unstable plants, and the discrete

implementations used in distributed control systems [66, 67]. The predictor is only as

good as the delay-free model. On a blender that model is (1.2), which is algebraic once

the speeds are known, so the usual worry about an uncertain high-order model is much

smaller here than it is for a distillation column.

   Active disturbance rejection does not remove a delay by itself. The observer in (1.10)

compares u and y at the same instant. If y is delayed and u is not, the observer attributes

the delay to f and the estimate is late by τd . Zhao and Gao delay the copy of u that

enters the observer, so that the observer sees a consistent pair and can run at a higher

skid it is known, because vb is measured and L is a pipe length: τd in (1.13) is a measured

variable delay, not a constant to be identified.



1.2.2     Inferential control and the mass balance

When the primary measurement is late, industrial practice closes a fast loop on a sec-

ondary measurement and uses the primary measurement to update a bias [12, 28, 32].

Distillation columns have used tray temperature this way for decades. The secondary

measurement must move when the primary variable moves, and the bias update must be

slow enough that the delay does not make it oscillate.

   For beer blending the secondary measurement is not a proxy. It is the mass balance

(1.2) itself. The speeds are the same speeds the flow loops already use. The densities are

known to a few parts per thousand for a given mother beer and for deaerated water at

a controlled temperature [2]. The one quantity in (1.2) that the skid does not measure

on line, in the basic instrument set, is Ph , the original extract of the mother beer. It is

known from the fermentation record and it drifts when a new tank is brought on. A bias

on (1.2), identified from the analyser, is precisely an estimate of that drift, expressed in

degrees Plato at the blend.

   A bias update of the form ḃ = (Pmeas − P̂ (t − τd ))/Tb looks harmless and is not. If the

controller regulates P̂ + b and the innovation does not depend on b, any transient error

that entered b is frozen and becomes a permanent extract offset. The innovation has to

be

                             eb = Pmeas (t) − P̂ (t − τd ) + b(t) ,                    (1.14)


so that a wrong bias is visible even after the plant has settled. This is the same consistency

Chapter 2 writes the observer in that form. The literature on inferential control states

the architecture [11, 12]. It does not write (1.14) for a variable transport delay that is

computed from a measured beer speed, with the delay-free prediction taken from (1.2).

     Kalman’s filter and Luenberger’s observer are the general tools for combining a fast

model with a slow measurement [70, 71]. A Kalman filter would be the right estimator

if the extract dynamics were a stochastic linear system with a known covariance. Here

the fast part is an algebraic balance and the slow part is a pure delay plus a first-order

instrument lag. A single bias state with the innovation (1.14) is the Luenberger observer

for that structure. Adding the machinery of a Kalman filter would not remove the delay,

and it would hide the one tuning number Tb .



1.2.3      Header pressure as a means of keeping valve authority

The outlet valve is often described as a back-pressure regulator. Its job, in the usual

piping and instrumentation diagram, is to hold pm at a value that keeps the carbonation

or the downstream tank happy [2, 11, 72]. That description is incomplete on a line whose

inlet valves are linear and whose supply pressures move.

If pm is held constant while ph falls, xh rises. The valve handbook’s recommended window

                                              13
is the installed gain one designed for, and where a stick band of one or two percent of

stroke is a small flow error [13, 14]. Outside that window the valve is either too close to

the seat, and small stem errors become large flow errors, or too close to the stop, and the

flow loop has no remaining authority. Equation (1.15) says that pm is the variable that

puts xh back in the window, because ph is a disturbance and Q⋆h is a production setpoint.

The outlet valve is the actuator for pm . Using it only to hold a constant pressure spends

the one degree of freedom that protects the inlet valves.

   This coordination has to be slow relative to the flow loops, or the three valves will

fight. The general principle is time-scale separation, the same separation that justifies

cascade control and singular-perturbation arguments [12, 61, 73, 74]. Flow loops see a

valve time constant of a few seconds and can be given a bandwidth near one radian per

second. The header loop should sit several times slower, so that a flow loop treats a header

move as a disturbance it is fast enough to reject. Scheduling the slow bandwidth upward

when a stem nevertheless leaves the window is the same idea as the adaptive observer

gain of Xue and co-workers [63]: spend bandwidth when the disturbance is large, and

withhold it when the only thing a higher gain would do is pass noise and excite stiction

[23, 59].

   A proportional–integral pressure loop can implement a constant setpoint, and SIMC

will tune it [21]. It will not compute the setpoint from (1.15), and it will wind up if the

outlet valve sits in a stick band while the inlet valve runs out of travel. The extended state

observer avoids the wind-up of an integrator that is blind to the fact that the valve has

not moved, because the observer is driven by the measured pressure and by the command

that was actually sent [22, 41]. That property is useful here, and it is not a substitute for

the setpoint calculation.

1.2.4        The gap that the second technique addresses

Delay compensation, inferential control and pressure control are each mature [12, 66, 68,

69]. The blending skid needs them as one loop. The outlet valve must follow a header

setpoint that is computed so the inlet stems stay in the linear window, with a bandwidth

that rises only when a stem leaves that window. The extract controller must run on

(1.2) corrected by (1.14), with τd taken from the measured outlet speed, and it must stay

slower than the flow loops. A fixed-gain active-disturbance-rejection controller on the

analyser, of the kind Zhao and Gao would detune for a ten-second delay [68], leaves both

the authority problem and the delay in place. The second technique is the combination

that removes them.

1.3.4    Statement of the control problem

The controlled variables are the beer flow Qh , or equivalently the beer speed vh , and the

original extract P . The water flow is not an independent quality variable. It is the means

of setting P through R, and of keeping the ratio during a throughput change. The header

pressure is not a quality variable either. It is the means of keeping xh and xw inside an

interval [0.30, 0.78] whenever the boundary pressures allow it.

   The requirements are the following.

  1. After a supply-pressure step, P returns to and stays inside ±0.05◦ P, and Qh returns

     to its setpoint. The beer stem does not remain against the open stop.

  2. During the water-pressure oscillation the peak extract error stays below the error

     of a fixed-gain loop.

  3. After the brand-change ramp, P enters the ±0.05◦ P band and the ratio reaches the

     value implied by (1.16) at the new setpoint.

  4. During the throughput ramp, Qh tracks Q⋆h with a small integrated error, including

     the case in which the beer supply pressure is still low.

  5. After the mother-beer extract has drifted, the bias update removes the offset without

     a large overshoot of P .

  6. After the loss of Kv , the beer flow is recovered by stem movement, and the extract

     loop does not interpret the transient as a permanent composition error.

  7. Stem commands are not noisy enough to keep a valve in continuous motion inside

     its stick band. Valve travel is reported as well as extract error, because a quiet

     inaccurate loop is not a success and a noisy accurate loop will not be left in service

     [24, 25].

                                            19
   The manipulated variables are the three stem commands uh , uw and ub , each in [0, 1],

and the ratio command R inside the extract controller. No other actuator is available. In

particular there is no variable-speed pump in the problem as posed: the supply pressures

are disturbances, not controls.

Assumption 1.1. On the time scale of the positioners the header is algebraic. Equa-

tion (1.7) is solved for pm at every instant from the current stems and boundary pressures.

Acoustic dynamics and pipe elasticity are neglected.

Assumption 1.2. Each valve has a linear inherent characteristic, a known Kv at the

start of a run, and a positioner that is a first-order lag of known time constant, subject to

a stick band. The ten percent Kv loss of disturbance 6 is not announced to the controller.

Assumption 1.3. The analyser delay is τd = L/vb plus a known instrument lag. The

length L is known. The speed vb is measured.

Assumption 1.4. The controller’s copy of Ph is constant and equal to the design value.

The true Ph may drift. Densities used in (1.4) are the design densities.

   Under these assumptions the plant from the three commands to the two quality vari-

ables is a set of three first-order actuators, an algebraic pressure node, a static extract

map, and a delay. The uncertainty sits in Kv , in Ph , in the stick band, and in the bound-

ary pressures. Active disturbance rejection is suited to that split because the pressures

are measured and the rest can be carried by z2 [54, 56].



1.3.5     What the two techniques are required to add

The first technique addresses the inlet valves. It must compute b0 for each flow loop from

the installed gains (1.18), using the measured pressures and the measured flows, so that

a change in supply pressure changes the controller gain immediately rather than after z2

has observed the flow error. It must not invert the off-diagonal gains. It must remain

                                             20
decentralised, with one observer per valve, so that it can be implemented as two function

blocks [44, 55].

   The second technique addresses the outlet valve and the extract. It must compute a

header setpoint that minimises the departure of the three stems from the design stems

in Table 1.1, subject to a minimum drop across every valve. It must track that setpoint

with a linear observer whose bandwidth rises only when a stem leaves the linear window.

It must regulate P by manipulating R, using (1.2) as the delay-free feedback and (1.14)

as the analyser correction, with τd from Assumption 1.3.

   The two techniques share the measurements and they must be separated in bandwidth.

Flow loops are the fastest, the header loop is slower, and the extract loop is slower again,

because it drives the water-flow setpoint. Chapter 2 writes both designs. Chapter 3 builds

the plant that these assumptions describe and estimates the designs against a SIMC ratio

controller [10, 21] and against linear active disturbance rejection that uses the design-point

gains and the raw analyser [42, 68].

   The partial gain at frozen header pressure would have been Qh /xh = 7.50/0.58 =

12.93 m3/h per unit stem, almost twice Ghh . A flow controller tuned on that partial gain

commands too little stem when the header is free to move, and then overshoots when the

header does not move as the tuning assumed. The installed gain is the smaller and the

correct one. The numerical factor (Sw +Sb )/D = (0.703+2.711)/(2.344+0.703+2.711) =

0.593 converts the partial gain into Ghh and should be recomputed whenever the three

flows or the three drops change, which is every sample in the first technique.

   A supply step makes the same arithmetic urgent. With ph fallen to 3.05 bar and

pm held at 2.40 bar, ∆ph falls from 1.60 bar to 0.65 bar. The square-root gain falls by

The steady map from (∆uh , ∆uw ) to (∆Qh , ∆Qw ) is the matrix in (1.18). A static de-

desired gains. The condition number of G is moderate, but the relative gain λ11 = 1.106

says that the diagonal inverse is already within about ten percent of the full inverse. The

residual
                                     Ghw Gwh

is the fraction of the interaction that a diagonal controller ignores. An inverse decoupler

would cancel that fraction and would replace it with the error of the gain estimates. Those

estimates are noisy at the level of the flow transmitters and they are wrong while a valve is

stuck, because Q/x during a stick interval does not describe the impending slip. Bristol’s

rule and the robustness results for large relative gains point the same way: do not invert

[16–18].

   The off-diagonal terms therefore stay inside fh and fw . They are observable. A move

of the water command changes beer flow within one positioner time constant, the beer

observer sees the flow error, and z2 cancels it. The cost is a transient of a fraction of a

second, which is the transient a decoupler would also have if its gains were late by the

2.5 s filter. The benefit is that a wrong Ghw cannot push the beer valve.

2.2.12     Order of computation in one sample

The two techniques share a sample order, and the order is part of the design because

several blocks read each other’s previous output.


                                              43
  1. Read filtered flows, stems and pressures. Compute τd from vb .

  2. Update P̂ , the analyser state and the bias (2.36). Form Pc .

  3. Compute the installed gains and filter them. Apply (2.15) inside each observer that

     is about to step.

  4. Step the extract observer and obtain R. Form Q⋆w = RQ⋆h .

  5. Step the two flow observers and obtain uh and uw .

  6. Evaluate J and move p⋆m through the dead zone and (2.30). Update α from the

     stems. Step the header observer and obtain ub .

  7. Lag the three commands by 0.40 s and give the lagged commands to the positioners

     and to the observers as the applied input of the next sample.

   The extract loop is stepped before the flow loops so that the water setpoint used in

the same sample already contains the latest ratio. The header loop is stepped after the

flow commands so that its view of the stems includes the latest inlet commands only

through the plant, one sample later. A one-sample delay at 10 ms is negligible beside

a 2.4 s positioner. Closing the three algebraic loops inside one sample, by iterating the

observers to convergence, was tried in early builds and changed nothing measurable, at

the cost of an iteration that a function block does not need.



2.2.13     What a shared bandwidth does

The bandwidth order in Table 2.1 was not the first order tried. A header observer at

ωc,p = 0.55 rad/s and ωo,p = 2.4 rad/s, close to the flow loops, is stable on the unit-gain

test of Section 2.2 and unstable on the plant. The mechanism is visible in a few seconds

of a run that was discarded.

   At the design point the outlet stem sits inside its stick band. A header setpoint that

differs from 2.40 bar by only a few hundredths of a bar, which is what a grid minimiser

                                            44
returns before the dead zone is applied, integrates in z2,p until the command exceeds the

stick release of 0.014. The outlet stem then slips. Header pressure moves, both inlet

flows move, and both flow observers correct in the same second. Their corrections move

the header again. With the three loops in the same band, the correction arrives with

the wrong phase and the slip grows into a cycle of period about 4 s, with header pressure

swinging by several tenths of a bar and the beer command swinging by two tenths of stem.

The extract leaves the tolerance even though no external disturbance has been applied.

Slowing the header loop to (2.32), and ignoring setpoint changes inside 0.04 bar, removes

the cycle. The same plant with those two changes holds the design extract for the 50 s

noise-only interval of Chapter 3, with zero valve travel.

   The lesson is the ordinary one of cascade control, confirmed here on a sticky valve

rather than on a tank level [12, 28, 74]. The inner loops must be allowed to treat the

outer loop as a slow disturbance. A sticky valve makes the requirement sharper, because

the outer loop’s first movement is a jump of size equal to the stick band, not a small linear

step. The dead zone on p⋆m exists so that this jump is not provoked by the grid.



2.2.14     The bias innovation that freezes an offset

An earlier form of (2.36) used the innovation Pmeas − P̂ (t − τd ) and did not subtract bP .

It looks like a standard bias update and it is not observable. Suppose the soft sensor

is perfect, so P = P̂ whenever the signals are still, and suppose a transient has left bP

at 0.2◦ P. The extract controller regulates P̂ + bP to P ⋆ , which it does by driving P̂ to

P ⋆ − 0.2. The true extract follows P̂ . The analyser then reads P ⋆ − 0.2, the delayed soft

sensor reads the same value, and the innovation is zero. The bias sits at 0.2◦ P forever,

and the beer is permanently 0.2◦ P light. Nothing in the measurements contradicts the

bias, because the controller has moved the plant to hide it.

   Subtracting bP inside the innovation, as (2.36) does, makes the same situation an


                                             45
innovation of −0.2◦ P. The bias is driven back to zero and the extract returns to the

setpoint. When the mother beer really has changed, P − P̂ is a nonzero constant, the

innovation remains until bP equals that constant, and the controller then trims R. The

subtraction is the difference between a bias estimator and an integrator that memorises

its own transient. It is the same consistency condition as the model-error channel of a

Smith predictor [65, 66, 69]. The estimation in Chapter 3 uses only the consistent form.

The inconsistent form is recorded here because it is the natural mistake, and because a

campaign that used it would show a standing offset after the supply step and would be

blamed, wrongly, on the flow loops.



2.2.15     Noise budget of the flow command

The flow-meter standard deviation used in the build is σQ = 0.012 m3/h. The measurement

filter of time constant 0.30 s attenuates the white component above a few radians per

second. What remains is seen by (2.23). A rough bound on the command noise, using the

high-frequency gain of that transfer function divided by b0,h ≈ 3.2 and the filtered noise,

is a few thousandths of stem. The stick band is 0.014. The command noise therefore does

not, by itself, keep the valve in motion at the design point, which is what the zero travel

in the first 50 s confirms. Raising ωo from 2.5 to 8 multiplies the disturbance-channel gain

by about 8/2.5 and the command noise by the square of that ratio in the mid band. Early

trials at that higher bandwidth produced continuous stem motion of several hundredths

per second and were rejected on the travel index before any disturbance was applied. The

known-gain term is what makes ωo = 2.5 sufficient. Without it, f˙h during the supply

step contains the derivative of a square-root gain, the bound |f˙h |/ωo at ωo = 2.5 is no

longer small, and the observer must be faster than the noise budget allows. That is the

quantitative form of the argument in Section 1.1.

The numbers in Table 2.1 are the result of a sequence that can be repeated on another

  1. Identify τv from a stem step with the flow loops in manual, and identify Kv from

     test of Section 3.1.

  2. Set the flow bandwidth from the positioner. A loop that treats the valve as an un-

     known gain is usually placed near 1/(3τv ) to 1/(2τv ), which here is 0.14 to 0.21 rad/s.

     With the installed gain inside b0 the same positioner supports ωc = 0.85, about 2/τv .

     If the derivative test fails, lower ωc before touching ωo .

  3. Set ωo = 3ωc and read the stem travel over a minute of steady operation. If the

     travel is not zero, the command is crossing the stick band and ωo is too high for the

     flow-meter noise. Do not raise ωo to repair a wrong b0 .

  4. Set the header bandwidth at about a quarter of the flow bandwidth, and add the

     dead zone of a few hundredths of a bar. Confirm that a minute of steady operation

     still has zero outlet travel. If the outlet creeps, the dead zone is smaller than the

     grid noise or the stick band.

  5. Set the extract bandwidth at about a seventh of the flow bandwidth when the

     feedback is Pc , and at about half of that when the feedback is the raw analyser.

     The second setting is the baseline, not the design. Set Tb between two and three

     times the total delay, and confirm on a mother-beer step that the bias approaches

     ∂P/∂Ph · ∆Ph without crossing the setpoint by more than the tolerance.

  6. Only then schedule the header bandwidth. The schedule is a guard. If it sits at its

     ceiling for long intervals, the outlet valve is too small for the production rate and

     the remedy is Kv,b , not a larger α.

System build and estimation

The two techniques are estimated on a numerical plant that is the same plant the de-

sign equations describe. Section 3.1 records the build: the algebraic header, the sticky

positioners, the transmitters, the analyser, and the three controllers that are compared.

Section 3.2 records the campaign and reads the extract error, the beer-flow error and the

valve travel. The campaign is not a factory trial. It is a controlled estimation in which

every disturbance is known and every controller sees the same noise sequence, so that

differences can be attributed to the control law.



3.1      System build

3.1.1     Numerical plant

The plant state is the three stems. Everything else is algebraic or is a filter on a measure-

ment. At each step of 10 ms the following is computed, in order.

   The boundary pressures and the external setpoints are set by the scenario clock of

Section 3.2. The beer-valve coefficient is the design Kv,h until 480 s and ninety percent of

that value afterwards. Header pressure is the unique root of (1.7). With that pressure,

                                             53
mother-beer extract gives the true original extract at the mix point. The true extract is

   Transmitters add independent Gaussian noise and a 0.30 s lag. The standard devia-

3.2.3    Water-supply oscillation

From 130 s to 210 s the water supply oscillates with amplitude 0.28 bar. Peak extract

errors are 0.109, 0.140 and 0.086◦ P. Time spent outside ±0.05◦ P is 30 s, 32 s and 7.8 s.

The proposed beer-flow integral on the window is 4.89, against 10.2 for the fixed-gain

observer and 6.62 for proportional–integral control.

   The oscillation is a measured disturbance. It enters Gww through ∆pw on the sample it

occurs. The water-loop b0,w therefore moves with the oscillation, lagged by the 2.5 s gain

filter, and the command partially anticipates the gain change. The fixed-gain observer

must put the whole oscillation into z2 . It does reject it, which is why the extract error

stays near a tenth of a degree rather than growing, but the rejection is late by a fraction

of the observer bandwidth and the peak is the largest of the three. Proportional–integral

control has no observer and rejects the oscillation by the integral state, with a peak

between the other two. The header loop of the proposed controller moves as well, because

the coordinator sees a changing water supply; the outlet travel on the window is 0.55,

and the stem plot shows a slow modulation rather than a copy of the 26 s sine at high

amplitude.



3.2.4    Brand change

The setpoint ramp from 12.0 °P to 11.2 °P occupies 230–255 s. The window used for the

indices runs to 320 s, so it includes the ramp and 65 s of settling. Extract integrals are

76.2, 39.6 and 19.2 ◦ P · s. The proposed controller re-enters the ±0.05◦ P band 89 s after

the start of the window, that is about 64 s after the ramp has finished. Neither baseline

has settled when the throughput ramp begins.

   This is the delay comparison. The required ratio change is from 0.338 to 0.434, a water-

flow change of about 0.72 m3/h at the pre-ramp beer flow. The soft sensor sees the extract

move as soon as the water flow moves, and the extract bandwidth 0.12 rad/s finishes most

of the change inside a minute. The analyser-based observer at 0.055 rad/s, behind eleven

seconds of delay, is still 0.48 °P high at 280 s (11.68 against 11.20). Proportional–integral

control on the analyser is further behind, at 12.15. Figure 3.3 shows the three trajectories:

the proposed extract follows the ramp with a lag of a few seconds, and the baselines follow

   The peak error of the proposed controller on this window is 0.51 °P, which occurs

build was tuned to, not the lowest integral achievable by a faster unstable loop.



3.2.5     Throughput ramp under the reduced supply

From 320 s the beer-flow setpoint rises to 9 m3/h while the beer supply is still 3.05 bar. This

is the joint authority test. Beer-flow integrals on 320–400 s are 74.9, 69.3 and 4.83 m3 s/h.

Extract integrals are 77.3, 6.25 and 3.68 ◦ P · s.

   Figure 3.6 shows why the baseline flow integrals are large. Both baseline beer valves

reach the open limit near 0.98 and the delivered beer flow stays near 8 m3/h or below.

The fixed-gain extract controller then holds the extract reasonably well, at the wrong

throughput: at 360 s its extract is 11.16 °P against 11.20 °P, while its beer flow is 7.96 m3/h.

specification does not, because the bright-beer tank fills slowly and the missing flow is

not recovered later in the run. The proposed beer flow at the same instant is 8.90 m3/h

and at 400 s it has reached the setpoint. The header is near 1.45 bar and the three stems

are near 0.70.

3.2.7      Loss of beer-valve capacity

At 480 s the beer Kv falls by ten percent. Nothing in the controller is told. On 480–560 s

the beer-flow integrals are 121, 135 and 4.60 m3 s/h. Extract integrals are 216, 7.22 and

6.05 ◦ P · s.

    The installed-gain formula does not use Kv . It uses the flow that the weakened valve is

actually passing. Within the 2.5 s gain filter, b0,h falls and the beer command opens. The

stem moves from about 0.70 to 0.77, the flow returns to 9.06 m3/h, and the extract excur-

sion peaks at 0.38 °P. The baseline beer valves are already at the stop, so a further loss of

Kv cannot be met by stem movement. Their beer-flow integrals on this window are the

integrals of a flow that is stuck near 7.3 m3/h against a setpoint of 9 m3/h. Proportional–

integral extract control drives the water valve shut in an attempt to raise the extract, and

the extract reaches 13.8 °P. The fixed-gain extract observer, slower and delayed, holds the

extract near the setpoint by a water flow that happens to match the reduced beer flow,

and abandons the rate.

    Figure 3.8 shows the scheduled header bandwidth. It stays at the base value 0.20 rad/s

for most of the campaign, because the coordinator keeps the stems inside the window that

(2.33) treats as acceptable. It rises only near the end, when the beer stem passes 0.74

during the Kv loss. The schedule is therefore a guard rather than a permanent retuning.

A plant that needed the ceiling bandwidth for long intervals would be a plant whose outlet

valve was too small for the requested flow, and the right response would be a larger Kv,b ,

not a faster observer. The outlet valve in Table 1.1 was sized at stem 0.38 precisely so

that this guard would be enough.

3.2.9     What the estimation does not claim

The plant is the design model, integrated at 10 ms, with the disturbances of Table 3.1

and one noise draw. It does not include a pump curve, a bright-beer tank level, dissolved-

oxygen dynamics, or the hysteresis of a valve that has been in service for years beyond the

stick band used here [26, 27]. A valve with a stick band of several percent would change

the travel numbers and might force a lower ωo . The build acceptance tests of Section 3.1

would still be the right first check on a factory skid: design-point flows, and the initial

flow derivative after a small stem step, which confirms b0 .

   The proportional–integral and fixed-gain observers were tuned by the same rules as the

proposed loops, at the design point, and were not retuned against the later disturbances.

Retuning the baselines at the low supply pressure would improve their behaviour at that

pressure and worsen it at the design pressure. The proposed b0 (t) is the retuning, done

by the measurements, which is the comparison the technique is meant to win. A retuning

that uses knowledge of the scenario clock would not be an admissible controller.

   No index in this chapter is a flavour index. Stewart’s review of high-gravity brewing

records foam and ester differences that a correct original extract does not remove [1, 3, 5].

The techniques keep the extract and the flow at the values the brand and the production

plan specify. They do not enlarge the set of mother beers the brewery can ferment.

   Within those limits the estimation is unambiguous. On a line with three linear

valves, measured speeds and measured pressures, the pressure-manifold gain and the

valve-authority setpoint are what keep the beer valve off the stop, and the mass-balance

soft sensor with a delayed bias is what lets the extract loop run at the cascade bandwidth

rather than at the delay bandwidth. The numerical values are those of Table 3.2.

3.2.11     Mean errors

Dividing an integral by the length of the window gives a mean absolute error, which is

easier to compare with the tolerance 0.05 °P. On the brand-change window of 90 s the

                                             70
proposed mean absolute extract error is 19.2/90 = 0.21 P, most of it accumulated while

the setpoint is still moving. On the pressure-step window of 80 s it is 3.36/80 = 0.042 P,

inside the tolerance on average, with the settling time of 46 s saying that the first half of

the window contains the excursion and the second half does not. The fixed-gain observer

on that window averages 5.53/80 = 0.069 P and does not settle. Proportional–integral

control averages 4.03/80 = 0.050 P and settles in 72 s, with the beer stem already at

0.91. Average extract error alone would rank proportional–integral control close to the

proposed controller on the supply step. The stem and the later throughput window are

what separate them: the proportional–integral loop has spent the authority the later

ramp needed.

   On the full run the proposed mean absolute extract error is 52.4/560 = 0.094 P and the

mean absolute beer-flow error is 37.5/560 = 0.067 m3/h. The fixed-gain observer’s means

are 0.145 °P and 0.57 m3/h. The proportional–integral means are 0.94 °P and 0.51 m3/h.

The flow means of the two baselines are similar because both lose the beer valve for the

same physical reason. Their extract means differ because the observer still trims water

from the analyser and the proportional–integral loop, once the beer flow is stuck, trims

water from a ratio that no longer describes the plant.
