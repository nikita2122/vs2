                                         Abstract


High-gravity brewing ferments a concentrated mother beer and then blends it with cold
deaerated water to the alcohol, original extract and carbonation of the beer that is sold.
On the blend skid the actuators that actually set the product are three control valves—one
on the high-gravity beer, one on the dilution water, and one on the line that leaves the
skid—together with a carbon-dioxide injector. The beer flow, the water flow and the
pressure at the mix point are one hydraulic network. A ratio controller that trims the
water valve from a delayed extract reading, and a separate pressure controller on the final
valve, can hold a quiet operating point. They do not plan the move that a brand change
requires, and they have no model of the pressure at which dissolved carbon dioxide comes
out of solution inside a valve.

   This thesis formulates that skid as a computer-science problem in constrained opti-
misation and in estimation. Two technologies are developed. The first is a three-valve
nonlinear model predictive controller. At each sample it chooses the three valve lifts and
the carbon-dioxide dose over a short horizon so that original extract, alcohol, carbon
dioxide and product flow approach their setpoints, while the predicted pressure at each
vena contracta stays above the composition-dependent equilibrium pressure. Original
extract is computed from the nonlinear Balling map, so the controller does not treat extract
and alcohol as two independent dilutions of the same ratio. The second technology is a
physics-informed pressure digital twin of the same network. A commissioning experiment
estimates the three valve capacities and the bias of the beer-header pressure transmitter
to well inside one percent. Pipe roughness and the lumped minor-loss coefficient are not
separately identifiable; their sum, an effective loss coefficient, is recovered to about two
and a half percent. A residual neural network is trained with a mass-balance penalty and
is then gated off, because on held-out step tests it increases the flow error. The predictive
controller that uses the calibrated twin brings a lager-to-light grade change inside a band
of 0.05◦ P in 16 s. A decay at the mixer residence time, from an inlet placed exactly on the
new brand, would still be outside that band; the controller gets inside it by spending the
valve rate limit on a lighter inlet. The same predictive controller, given only the nominal
and biased model, leaves an offset of about 0.19◦ P and does no better on extract than
the ratio controller. When the mother beer changes so that extract and alcohol can no
longer be met together, the calibrated controller sits on the tolerance-weighted compromise.
Dissolved oxygen that enters with the dilution water is estimated and is not removed by
any valve motion.

   The study is a reproducible dynamic simulation of an 80 L mix volume at 20 m3 h−1 .
It is a design and estimation result, not a brewery trial.

Chapter 1

Research on Previous Work

The blend skid that this thesis designs is a small machine with a large effect on the beer
that leaves the brewery. It is also a standard problem in multivariable control once it is
written down in equations: three valves, one carbon-dioxide injector, a handful of fast
hydraulic measurements, and a slower laboratory-grade statement of what the beer is.
The previous work that matters splits into two bodies. The first is the engineering of
high-gravity blending, of control valves, and of model predictive control. The second is
the engineering of digital twins, hybrid models and constrained estimation. This chapter
reviews those two bodies and then writes the problem that neither of them has closed.



1.1     Research on coordinated valve control for high-

        gravity blending

1.1.1    High-gravity brewing and the moment of dilution

High-gravity brewing is the practice of mashing, boiling and fermenting a wort that is
stronger than the beer that will be sold, and of restoring the sales strength by adding
water later [1, 2]. The capacity gain is real. The brewhouse, the fermenters and often the
filters process less liquid per unit of sales beer, so a brewery can raise output without a
new tank farm. Stewart [1] records the usual band of original gravities, about 15◦ to 20◦ P,



                                             9
and the usual penalty: foam proteins are lost out of proportion to the dilution, yeast is
stressed, and the flavour of the diluted beer does not automatically match a beer that was
brewed at sales gravity. The same points are developed for very-high-gravity fermentations
by Puligundla et al. [3] and, for the yeast cell itself, by Gibson et al. [4] and Casey et al.
[5]. Stewart et al. [6] returns to the foam question and notes that a beer fermented at
20◦ P and then diluted can retain about half the hydrophobic polypeptide of the matching
sales-gravity beer. Boulton and Quain [7] and the brewing handbooks [8, 9] place that
stress in the wider account of how yeast handles strong worts.

   Those fermentation facts set the boundary of this thesis. The blend skid cannot put
hydrophobic polypeptides back into the beer. It can avoid adding a second, independent
damage at the moment of dilution: oxygen, a wrong alcohol, a wrong original extract, or
a loss of carbon dioxide because a valve was allowed to flash. Bamforth [10] treats beer
quality as a set of properties that are fixed at particular process steps. Flavour stability is
one of them. Vanderhaegen et al. [11] reviews the chemistry by which oxygen and storage
time produce the carbonyls of stale beer, and Uchida and Ono [12] identifies the hydroxyl
radical as a route from dissolved oxygen to that staling. The practical consequence on the
skid is a dissolved-oxygen specification measured in tens of parts per billion, not in parts
per million.

   Industrial descriptions of the skid agree on the hardware and on the tolerances, and they
agree that the control law is ratio control. Centec states a dosing accuracy of 0.05◦ P on
original gravity and 0.03 % by volume on alcohol, with inline sensors and a ratio controller,
and offers carbon dioxide injection on the same frame [13]. Corosys describes the same duty
and adds the water specification that makes the duty possible: after filtration, dilution
water is cooled and deaerated to a residual oxygen below 10 ppb to 20 ppb, and the control
system must follow changes in flow and pressure quickly [14]. Palmer and Kaminski [15]
adds a chemical constraint that no flow controller can satisfy by itself. The calcium of the
dilution water has to stay below the calcium of the concentrated beer, or calcium oxalate
can precipitate in the package and seed gushing. The skid can detect a bad water analysis.
It cannot blend the calcium away without also moving the extract.

   The water is therefore a raw material with its own certificate, not a free manipulated

                                              10
variable. The manipulated variables are the openings of the valves and the carbon-dioxide
dose. That distinction is the start of the control problem.



1.1.2     The quality map that a ratio must hit

Original extract is not a substance that is still in the beer. It is the extract the wort
would have had before fermentation, reconstructed from the alcohol and the real extract
that remain. Balling’s mass balance, in the form used by Kunze [2], Holle [16] and the
laboratory methods [17–19], is

                                       100 (2.0665 Aw + Er )
                               pOE =                         .                        (1.1)
                                         100 + 1.0665 Aw

The coefficient 2.0665 is the grams of extract consumed to produce one gram of alcohol,
and 1.0665 corrects the denominator for the mass that left the liquid as carbon dioxide and
yeast. Aw and Er are both percent by weight. Apparent extract, the number a hydrometer
reads in alcoholic beer, is a different quantity. The classical laboratory separation is
Tabarie’s relation between the specific gravity of the beer, the distillate and the residue.
Inline instruments replace that separation by a pair of physical measurements, discussed
below. Equation (1.1) remains the definition that the controller has to hit.

parameter of the second technology. The physical content that the first technology needs
is already here: equilibrium pressure is a function of the beer that is in the pipe at that

1.1.3     Ratio control and the relative-gain array

Ratio control is the classical way to run two flows at a prescribed proportion [25, 26]. One
stream is the wild stream. The other is set to R times the measured wild flow, and a slow
outer loop trims R from a composition measurement. On a high-gravity skid the wild
stream is usually the beer, the manipulated stream is the water, and the outer measurement
is original extract or alcohol [13, 14]. A third loop holds a pressure by moving the final
valve. Nothing in that structure writes down the fact that opening the final valve increases
both inlet flows, or that the extract loop and the pressure loop therefore share actuators.

   Bristol [27] defined the relative-gain array for exactly this question. If G is the matrix
of steady gains from valve lifts to the chosen outputs, the relative-gain array is


                                      Λ = G ◦ (G−1 )T ,                                 (1.6)


where ◦ is the element-wise product. A diagonal entry near one supports a single-loop
pairing. A large off-diagonal entry says that the loop will be detuned, or even destabilised,
by the other loops when they are closed. Skogestad [28] and Downs and Skogestad [29]
put the same test inside plantwide design: inventory and pressure loops are arranged first,
and quality loops are closed on what remains. Skogestad [30] then gives the SIMC rules
that tune each loop once the pairing is chosen. Those rules are the right way to build the
ratio-and-pressure baseline of this thesis. They do not by themselves coordinate a move in
which beer flow, water flow and pressure must all change together while a composition
constraint depends on the pressure.

   The baseline used later is therefore not a straw man. It has Balling feedforward from
the inlet analysis, incremental proportional–integral loops on the two flows and on the
mix pressure, and carbon-dioxide feedforward from the measured flows. Its limitation is
structural. The pressure setpoint is a number chosen by the designer. The extract loop
does not know that a large water move will unload the final valve. The alcohol and the
extract share one ratio, so an integral controller that is told to remove extract error will

                                             13
spend the whole ratio on extract and leave the alcohol where it falls.

FP is the piping-geometry factor and FR is the Reynolds-number factor. Both are near

                                         Φ(l) = R l−1 ,                                   (1.8)

should not be asked to regulate there.

1.1.5      What can be measured on the pipe

Three families of measurement appear on a modern skid.

    Coriolis meters measure mass flow and, on the same tube, density [38]. Velocity follows
from the volumetric flow and the pipe area. Density is the observation that identifies the
small contraction term in the equation of state. The thesis uses volumetric flow in the
valve law because that is how Kv is defined, and it converts with the density the meter or
the model supplies.

    Original extract and alcohol are inferred, not weighed. The industrial inline route
combines oscillating-tube density with the speed of sound [39, 40]. Alcohol raises the speed
of sound and lowers density. Extract raises density and moves the speed of sound only
moderately. The two effects are independent enough to invert. Schöck et al. [40] showed
that dissolved carbon dioxide itself moves the speed of sound and the compressibility in
ethanol–sugar solutions, which is why a beer monitor adds a carbon-dioxide measurement
before it reports extract. Near-infrared and mid-infrared spectra are the laboratory and
at-line alternative [41–44]. They are selective, and they are also sensitive to the sugar
spectrum of the particular mash. Grassi et al. [44] follow fermentation by FT-NIR; Iñón

                                            16
et al. [43] combine mid- and near-infrared for finished beer; Lachenmeier [42] uses FTIR
for a rapid multivariate check of beer and spirits. The EBC and ASBC methods still
define the reference analyses those calibrations are judged against [17–19]. For control, the
relevant facts are the accuracy, which the instrument makers put near 0.02 % by weight
on alcohol and 0.04 % by weight on extract [39], and the delay. A sensor downstream of a
mixer reports the beer that left the mixer one transport time ago, plus its own filter. On
the skid studied here that delay is 12 s, comparable to the residence time. A controller
that waits for the outlet analysis before it moves has already missed one mixer volume.

   Dissolved oxygen and dissolved carbon dioxide are measured by separate optical or
thermal-conductivity probes. They enter the estimator as outputs. Oxygen does not enter
the manipulated vector.



1.1.6     Model predictive control, and where brewing has used it

Model predictive control solves, at each sample, a finite-horizon optimal-control problem
and applies the first move [45–50]. The industrial record reviewed by Qin and Badgwell
[46] is dominated by linear models and quadratic costs on refining and chemical plants.
The stability theory reviewed by Mayne et al. [47] adds a terminal cost or a terminal
constraint so that the receding-horizon law inherits the decrease of a Lyapunov function.
Nonlinear model predictive control carries the same idea onto a nonlinear predictor [51].
Economic model predictive control replaces, or supplements, the quadratic stage cost with
a stage cost that is the actual operating objective [52]. On the blend skid the stage cost is
already economic in that limited sense: it is the squared violation of a quality tolerance,
not the distance to an arbitrary linearisation.

   The computation is a nonlinear program. A direct multiple-shooting or single-shooting
transcription produces a structured program that an interior-point solver such as IPOPT
can handle [53, 54]. For a horizon of a few seconds and four inputs, the real-time iteration
of Diehl et al. [55] and its later development by Gros et al. [56] evaluates one quadratic
program per sample, warm-started from the previous solution, and is fast enough for a
valve. The case study in this thesis uses a short single-shooting program and a sequential


                                             17
quadratic solver, with the previous move as the initial guess. The production form of the
same problem is the real-time iteration. Nothing in the formulation depends on which of
those two solvers is called, provided the solver returns a feasible decrease of the same cost.

   Brewing has used this machinery on the fermenter, not on the blend skid. Gee and
Ramirez [57] built a flavour model. Andrés-Toro et al. [58] identified a kinetic model under
industrial conditions. Carrillo-Ureta et al. [59] computed a temperature trajectory by a
genetic algorithm. Trelea et al. [60] posed aroma production as a dynamic optimisation.
Rodman and Gerogiorgis [61] treated the same fermentation as a multi-objective dynamic
program. The manipulated variable in that literature is temperature, sometimes an
aeration rate, and the horizon is measured in days. The measurements are slow. The
blend skid is the opposite object: the horizon is a minute, the gains are hydraulic, and the
quality constraint is an algebraic function of pressure. The fermentation papers are the
evidence that brewing will accept a model-based move. They are not a controller for the
three valves.

   The gap is therefore specific. Ratio control is the installed law [13, 14, 25]. Predictive
control is a mature algorithm [46, 48]. Valve equations and carbon-dioxide solubility are
both published [21, 31]. They have not been put in one program that moves the beer
valve, the water valve and the final valve together, under a Balling quality map and a
vena-contracta constraint written with peq rather than with the vapour pressure of water.
That program is the first technology of the thesis.



1.2      Research on physics-informed digital twins

1.2.1     What is being duplicated

A digital twin is a running model joined to a particular piece of equipment by data that
flow in both directions [62–64]. Kritzinger et al. [65] separate three objects that are often
given the same name. A digital model is updated by hand. A digital shadow receives
automatic measurements and does not act. A digital twin receives measurements and also
sends decisions back to the equipment. The classification matters here because a monitor


                                             18
of original extract, however elaborate, is a shadow. The object proposed in Chapter 2 is a
twin in the strict sense: its parameters are estimated from the skid, and the predictive
controller that moves the valves is the channel through which the twin acts.

   Reviews of enabling technology [62–64] list the same practical requirements: a model
that is fast enough to run ahead of the process, a data interface, a rule for when the model
is allowed to disagree with a sensor, and a bound on what the twin is permitted to change.
On a blend skid the last of these is a safety statement. The twin may revise a valve
setpoint inside a rate limit. It may not open a valve that the programmable controller has
interlocked, and it may not lower the bright-tank pressure below the relief setting. ISA-88
is the batch model in which a brand change is already a phase with a start, a run and
a hold [66]. OPC Unified Architecture is the information model in which the lifts, the
flows and the quality record can be named once and read by both the controller and the
twin [67]. Those two standards are not the contribution. They are the shelf on which the
contribution has to sit if a brewery is to run it.



1.2.2     Twins that have been built for brewing

The published brewing twins are about fermentation and about the brewhouse, not about
the three valves. Goffi et al. [68] report a bidirectional twin of a fermentation sampling
chamber. The physical side is pressurised to about seven bar, the digital side can command
non-critical parameters, and the measured labour of sampling falls by about ninety percent.
The safety argument of that paper—interlocks around a pressurised vessel, a progressive
commissioning, a human-readable view of state—transfers directly to a blend skid, which
is also a pressurised food line. The process model does not transfer. Their twin watches a
fermenter. It does not solve a valve network, and it does not estimate a flow coefficient.

   Plant-wide energy twins and brewhouse dashboards exist as industrial practice. They
optimise steam and refrigeration, or they display a batch against a chosen golden batch.
They are shadows or operational-research models of utility systems. They do not identify
Kv from a step test, and they do not predict the pressure in a vena contracta. The absence
of a blend-skid twin is not an accident of the search. The skid has been treated as a


                                             19
packaged ratio controller [13]. The research literature followed the fermenter, where the
biochemistry is.



1.2.3     Hybrid models and physics-informed networks

Two traditions supply the model class.

   The first is hybrid semi-parametric modelling [69–71]. A first-principles skeleton carries
the balances that are known, and a data-driven block carries the kinetics, the sensor drift
or the hydraulic residual that are not. Kadlec et al. [71] review soft sensors in the process
industries and emphasise maintenance: a soft sensor that is not recalibrated becomes a
biased instrument. von Stosch et al. [69] and Sansana et al. [70] argue that the useful
industrial model is the hybrid one, because a pure network does not extrapolate to a new
brand and a pure mechanistic model does not absorb a worn valve. The blend skid is a
favourable case for that argument. Mass balances, the valve equation and Henry’s law are
known. The worn Kv , the pressure-transmitter bias and the small density contraction are
not, and they are constant for long enough to be estimated.

   The second tradition writes the physical residual into the training loss of a network
[72–74]. Raissi et al. [72] penalise the violation of a differential equation at collocation
points. Karniadakis et al. [73] review the approach across physics. Cai et al. [74] review
it for fluids, which is the closest neighbour of a valve network. The same idea has been
carried into predictive control. Zheng and Wu [75] estimate uncertain parameters online
inside a physics-informed recurrent model and trigger a model update when the prediction
error grows. Wu et al. [76] embed a population balance in a recurrent network and use
the network as the predictor of a crystalliser controller, with less data than a purely
empirical network required. Both papers are about chemical reactors with uncertain
kinetics. Neither is about a valve. The transfer that this thesis makes is the penalty, not
the network architecture: after a physical model has predicted the three flows, a residual
network may correct them, but the corrected flows are charged for any mass they fail to
conserve. A network that improves the fit by inventing beer is rejected.

   There is a second, less comfortable result in that literature, and the case study confirms


                                             20
it. If the physical parameters already put the prediction inside the sensor noise, a residual
network trained on a short commissioning set fits the noise. Kadlec et al. [71] describe the
same failure mode for soft sensors that are over-parameterised relative to the excitation.
The correct engineering response is a gate. The residual block is deployed only when it
lowers the error on step tests that it did not train on. Chapter 3 reports that on this skid
the gate stays shut. That negative result is part of the technology, not a reason to omit
the block from the design. The block is the place where an unmodelled valve defect would
appear on the next skid, and the gate is what stops it from appearing when it is not there.



1.2.4     Estimation, observability and stiction

The classical recursive estimator is the Kalman filter [77]. The unscented filter extends
it to a nonlinear map without a hand-written Jacobian [78]. Moving-horizon estimation
replaces the recursion by a constrained least-squares problem on a window [79]. The
window form is the one that matches this skid. Valve lifts are bounded, capacities are
positive, and a pressure bias of several bar is not a credible estimate even if it lowers the
residual for one sample. A single commissioning campaign, rather than a recursion at
every second, is also the right experiment: the capacities drift over weeks, not over the
minute of a grade change. During the grade change the fast state—the composition in the
mixer—is reconstructed by integrating the measured flows, and the slow parameters stay
at the commissioned values. That split is an application of the same time-scale separation
already used for the pressure in (1.14).

   Identifiability is not guaranteed by the existence of a least-squares problem. Two
parameters that enter one pressure drop only as a sum cannot be separated by experiments
that observe the sum. The Darcy term f (ε)L/D and the minor-loss coefficient K are such
a pair. Chapter 3 shows the numerical consequence: the individual estimates wander and
their standard errors explode, while the effective resistance

   Valve stiction is the other defect the data do contain. Choudhury et al. [80] model
the stick–slip cycle with a stick band and a slip jump. Jelali and Huang [81] collect
the industrial methods that detect the cycle from the controller output and the process
variable. Kano and Ogawa [82], in a survey of chemical-process control practice, put
stiction among the faults that operators still compensate by hand. On the blend skid the
water valve is the likely site: it runs at a lower flow, it may sit for long periods at one
brand, and a deadband of a few percent of lift is enough to move the extract by more than
the tolerance once the valve finally slips. The twin uses a reduced form of the Choudhury
model, a deadband with no slip jump, because that is the defect inserted in the case study.
The compensator is the inverse deadband: the command leads the desired lift, in the
direction of travel, by the estimated band. A predictive controller that does not know the
band keeps integrating a flow error until the valve breaks, and then overshoots. Chapter 3
compares those two behaviours.



1.2.5     The gap the twin has to fill

The twin literature tells us how to classify the object [65], how to join a balance to a
network [69, 72, 75], and how to estimate under constraints [79]. The brewing literature
tells us what a beer twin has been asked to do [68]. The valve literature tells us how
a worn trim and a sticky stem appear in the data [31, 80]. None of these writes the
estimation problem for the blend skid: three capacities, one header bias, one solubility
scale, one density contraction, one effective line resistance, and a gated residual, with the
commissioned model handed to the predictive controller of Section 1.1. That estimation
problem, and the twin that solves it, is the second technology.

0.21 % by volume. Restoring the alcohol raises the original extract by about half a degree
Plato. The optimisation problem has to say which miss is cheaper. A single-loop extract
controller will not say it. It will spend the ratio on extract.

1.3.5     What is not known well enough to leave in a ratio station

The valve equation is only as good as Kv . A trim that has eroded, or a sizing calculation
that was done for water and left uncorrected for beer, moves the true gain by several
percent. Several percent of the beer-valve gain at the design point is several cubic metres
per hour, which is several tenths of a degree Plato during a grade change. A pressure
transmitter that reads 0.10 bar high, and a model that believes it, assigns the wrong drop
to the beer valve and then attributes the wrong flow to Kv . These are the parameters the
twin has to estimate before the predictive controller is entitled to its model.

   The line resistance is real and, as (1.15) says, only partly structured. The solubility
scale κζ is real and is not observable from a hydraulic step test, because a step test that
stays inside the margin never flashes. It is observable from a bench equilibrium pressure
on one sample of known composition. The density contraction is observable because the
Coriolis meter reports density directly. A residual network is available for whatever those
parameters leave behind, and it is required to conserve mass.

   Stiction on the water valve is a discrete defect on top of this continuous model. It is
estimated from the smallest command that produces a flow change, and it is compensated
by a lead in the command. It is not absorbed into Kv , because a deadband and a wrong
gain have different signatures: the gain scales every move, and the deadband swallows the
small ones.



1.3.6     The two technologies, stated as answers to this problem

The first technology is three-valve nonlinear model predictive control. At each control
sample it minimises the sum of (1.27) along a prediction of (1.7)–(1.18), subject to (1.21),
and it applies the first block of u. The predictor uses the Balling map, so the linear-ratio
offset computed above is zero in the model. The predictor uses peq from (1.4) inside (1.12),

                                            28
so a move that would flash carbon dioxide is expensive before it happens. The three lifts
are one decision vector, so the final valve is not left to discover, after the fact, that the
inlet valves have changed the flow it must pass.

   The second technology is the physics-informed pressure digital twin that supplies that
predictor. It estimates Kv,h , Kv,w , Kv,f , the beer-header bias, κζ , the density contraction
and the effective loss CL from a short campaign of valve steps, a bench solubility point
and the Coriolis density. It trains a residual network under a mass-balance penalty and
deploys the network only if held-out steps improve. It reconstructs the mixer composition
from the measured flows so that the predictive controller does not wait out the analyser
delay, and it inverts a measured water-valve deadband.

   The ratio-and-pressure station defined in Section 1.1 remains the baseline. It is
the controller a skid has today, retuned by the same gain rules, with the same Balling
feedforward an attentive designer would add. The estimation question of Chapter 3 is
whether the two technologies improve on that station by enough to matter at the tolerances
the brewers already publish, and whether the improvement survives a mother beer that
makes the brand infeasible, a sticky water valve, and dilution water that arrives with too
much oxygen.

   The scope is the skid. Fermentation policy, foam-protein loss, and the design of the
deaeration column are upstream. The bright-tank pressure is a boundary condition. The
thesis does not claim a plant trial. It claims a formulated problem, two algorithms, and
a numerical estimation of how those algorithms behave on a plant model that is more
detailed than the model inside the controller.

The Two Technologies

This chapter specifies the two technologies named in Section 1.3. The first is the predictive
controller that moves the three valves and the carbon-dioxide injector. The second is
the twin that estimates the model inside that controller. Both are written so that a
programmer can implement them, and so that the numerical choices of the case study are
the same choices the text describes.



2.1      Three-valve nonlinear model predictive control

2.1.3     Composition prediction and carbon-dioxide loss

From a solved hydraulic node the composition is stepped by the forward Euler form of
(1.16) and (1.18). The eigenvalue of the mixer is −1/τ ≈ −0.07 s−1 . A step of 2 s multiplies
a composition error by about 0.87 per step and does not oscillate. Euler is therefore
enough. A production code can substitute a single exponential on the linear mixer without
touching the optimiser.

contracta lies below equilibrium, a fraction of the supersaturation leaves the liquid in one

   Bounds are the lift limits and a dose limit of 15 g s−1 . The initial guess is the move that
was applied at the previous sample, copied into both blocks. A sequential least-squares
quadratic program then reduces the cost. In the case study the iteration limit is six, which
is short on purpose: the warm start is already near a good move, and a valve controller

transcription of the same shooting problem is the real-time iteration [55, 56] solved by an
interior-point code [53, 54]. The case-study solver is a stand-in that minimises the same
function. It is not a claim that six sequential-quadratic iterations are a certified real-time
iteration.

2.1.6     The ratio station used as the baseline

comparison is against a competent station rather than against an untuned one.

2.1.7     Offset, feasibility and what the horizon does not prove

A predictive controller that re-optimises from a measured state removes a constant
disturbance that the model can see. It does not remove a constant error in its own gain.
If Kv is wrong, the predicted flow of a given lift is wrong, the optimiser asks for the wrong
lift, and the outlet integral of a ratio station may still be the faster way home. That is
why the first technology, deployed on the nominal model, is not expected to dominate the
baseline. The second technology exists to make the model that the first technology trusts.

   Feasibility of the inequalities is not guaranteed for every mother beer. When extract
and alcohol are inconsistent, the hard bounds on the lifts remain feasible and the quality
targets become a soft compromise through (1.27). When a header pressure collapses so
far that the product flow cannot be passed without entering the vena contracta, the soft
pressure penalty outranks the flow penalty and the controller gives up flow. The case
study does not include a collapse of that size. The header step in Chapter 3 is 0.45 bar,
and every controller keeps a positive margin.

   A terminal constraint to the steady set, of the kind Mayne et al. [47] use to prove
stability, is the correct production addition to Algorithm 1. The case study does not
implement it. The horizon is short, the rate limit is hard, and the steady program is feasible


                                             36
at both brands. The numerical evidence in Chapter 3 is an estimation of closed-loop
error, not a Lyapunov certificate. Saying so keeps the first technology in the class of
engineering predictive control reviewed by Qin and Badgwell [46] and Maciejowski [50],
where certificates are added when the model and the constraints have settled.



2.1.8     A grade change, computed before the valves move

The light brand asks for w = 0.58 rather than 0.70. Product mass flow at the lager density
is about 5.61 kg s−1 . The beer mass flow therefore falls from about 3.92 kg s−1 to about
3.25 kg s−1 , and the water mass flow rises by the same 0.67 kg s−1 . In volumetric units the
beer setpoint moves from 13.96 m3 h−1 toward about 11.6 m3 h−1 , and the water setpoint
rises from 6.06 m3 h−1 toward about 8.4 m3 h−1 . Divided by the diagonal gains, those are
lift changes of a few hundredths on the beer valve and about a tenth on the water valve.
At 0.04 lift/s that water-valve move takes about three seconds. Holding the inlet exactly at
the new brand after that move would still leave an extract error near 0.7◦ P one residence
time later, because e−1 times the 1.93◦ P step has not yet entered the 0.05◦ P band. A
controller that is inside the band in 16 s has therefore used an inlet lighter than the new
brand while the vessel empties. Section 2.1.14 records that move on the commissioned
model. A controller that is still outside the band two minutes later is limited by its model
or by its tuning, not by the pipe. Chapter 3 uses that comparison directly.

   The carbon-dioxide setpoint falls from 5.20 g L−1 to 5.00 g L−1 at the same moment.
Because the water fraction rises, the dose does not fall in proportion: more uncarbonated
water has to be made up. The predictive controller sees both effects in (1.18). The baseline
sees them in (1.19), which is algebraically the same at steady state and is blind to the
trajectory between.

2.1.10      Bristol’s array from the measured gain

The relative-gain array is G multiplied elementwise by the transpose of G−1 [27]. The
point of writing the arithmetic is to show that the pairing in Section 1.3 is a property of
these three numbers and not a convention copied from a flow–flow–pressure textbook.

   Denote the rows of (2.10) by the beer flow, the water flow and the mix pressure. The
determinant is dominated by the product of the diagonal, 48.05 × 23.09 × (−2.14), which


                                              38
is about −2370, and the off-diagonal corrections do not change its sign. The (1, 1) entry
of G−1 is the beer-flow response to a beer-lift move after the other two outputs have been
held at zero by the other two inputs. Multiplied back by G11 it is Λ11 = 0.87. The same
construction gives Λ22 = 0.95 and Λ33 = 0.82. Each diagonal entry is the fraction of
the open-loop gain that survives when the other loops are closed perfectly. A value near
one means the other loops do not steal the actuator. A value near zero would mean the
recommended pairing is the wrong one. A negative value would mean the loops fight.

   Thirteen percent of the beer-flow relative gain sits on the final valve, and five percent
of the water-flow relative gain sits there too. Those are the terms a grade change excites,
because a grade change moves all three lifts in the same few seconds, and perfect closure
of the other loops is exactly what has not happened yet. At steady state, after the ratio
station has settled, the same thirteen percent is a small trim on the pressure loop. The
array therefore explains both observations in Chapter 3: the ratio station can hold a brand,
and it is slow when the brand changes. It does not by itself choose the nonlinear map.
Balling’s denominator is outside G, and no arrangement of these nine gains puts it back.



2.1.11       How long the horizon has to be

The mixer time constant at the design flow is τ = 14.4 s. The control sample is ∆tc = 4 s.
The prediction uses two move blocks and three Euler steps of 2 s inside each block, so the
horizon is
                                   TH = 2 × 3 × 2 = 12 s,                                (2.17)

which is 0.83 τ . A step in the inlet composition has, by (2.35), reached a fraction
1 − e−TH /τ = 1 − e−0.83 = 0.56 of its final value at the end of the horizon. The controller
therefore sees a little more than half of the quality move that a valve decision will eventually
produce. It does not see the whole settling tail. The tail is left to the next sample, which
is the receding-horizon argument: only the first block is applied, and the remainder of the
response is re-predicted when the state has moved.

   Lengthening the horizon to 2τ , about 29 s, would show 86 percent of the step and
would multiply the number of network solves by about two and a half. On a skid whose

                                              39
disturbance is a brand change that is known at the sample it begins, the extra tail is
a smaller extract error at the far nodes, already shrinking by e−α each step, and the
decision that matters is the first block. The case study keeps TH = 12 s for that reason. A
disturbance that arrived as an unmeasured ramp over a minute, for example a slow header
decay, would justify the longer horizon. The header step in the second scenario is a step,
and 12 s covers its onset.

   The two blocks are not two independent valve trajectories. Inside a block the command
is held, so the eight-vector z is

The grade-change arithmetic of the previous subsection needs only about a tenth of a lift
on that valve. The rate limit is not what makes the vessel take 14 s. The volume is.



2.1.12      A move that the rate limit does price

Travel in Table 3.5 is the sum of absolute lift increments over the run. On the grade
change the ratio station travels 7.09 and both predictive controllers travel about 1.65. The
difference is not a smoother optimum in the abstract. It is the incremental law (2.12)
reacting to a flow error that the mixer has not yet produced. At the sample of the setpoint
change the ratio station computes new flow setpoints immediately, the flows are still the
lager flows, and the errors eh and ew are the full step of about 2.4 and 2.3 cubic metres
per hour. The proportional increments are 0.018 × 2.4 and 0.028 × 2.3, a few hundredths
of lift, and the integral keeps adding while the vessel is still exchanging. Each later sample
sees a flow error of the opposite sign as the valves overshoot the new ratio, and the stems
reverse. The sum of those reversals is the travel of 7.

                                                  40
   The predictive controller applies one block that already points at the new steady lifts,
then a second block that is close to the first, and then stops. The stage cost charges
0.4 ∥∆l∥2 /0.01 for that one move and charges nothing further once the prediction says
the vessel will arrive. The travel of 1.65 is that move plus the small trims that follow
the analyser. It is larger than the one-tenth lift of the steady-state arithmetic because
the final valve and the dose move as well, and because the first sample cannot jump the
whole way when the warm start is the lager command and the iteration budget is six. The
budget spends itself on the first sample of the grade change. Later samples start next to a
solution and barely move.

   The same accounting explains the third scenario, where the commissioned controller
travels 0.63 and the ratio station travels 0.20. The extra travel is the deadband lead. The
stem is commanded across a band of width 0.03 that produces no flow, so that the flow,
when it starts, is the flow the brand asked for. A travel column that is read as “smaller
is always better” would punish the only controller that has understood the valve. The
column is reported so that a reversal hunt, of the kind the untuned absolute-form law
produced before the gains of (2.12) were fixed, cannot hide inside a good extract integral.



2.1.13      Where the carbon-dioxide dose sits in the same vector

The dose is the fourth coordinate of each block and it does not appear in G, because G
was identified on the three lifts with the dose doing whatever holds 5.20 g L−1 . Its own
gain is algebraic. One gram per second of carbon dioxide, divided by 20 m3 h−1 , is

                                1 g s−1 × 3600
                                               = 0.180 g L−1                             (2.19)
                                20 × 103 L h−1

at the design flow, before the mixer lags it. The stage cost treats 0.05 g L−1 as one tolerance,
so a dose error of 0.05/0.180 = 0.28 g s−1 costs the same, at steady state, as one tolerance
of original extract. The optimiser can therefore spend a fraction of a gram per second to
protect carbonation without giving up the brand, and it cannot hide a one-gram-per-second
mistake. That scaling is why the dose belongs in z rather than in a slave loop with an
unrelated gain. A slave loop can still implement the command. The predictive program

                                              41
decides it, jointly with the lifts, because a water-valve move changes the uncarbonated
inlet at the same moment the dose should change.

   At the lager point the required dose is 7.507 g s−1 . The upper bound in (1.21) is 15 g s−1 ,
which is twice the design and is the injector size used in the study. The bound is inactive
at both brands. It would become active if the mother beer arrived nearly flat and the
product specification stayed at 5.2 g L−1 , a case the commissioning range does not include.
The program already has the inequality. No new term is required.

   The equilibrium pressure moves when the dose moves, because c moves and peq =
c/(κζ ζ). A dose increase of 1 g s−1 raises c by about 0.18 g L−1 and peq by about 0.18/2.73 =
0.066 bar once the mixer has caught up. The margin constraint sees that rise. A controller
that carbonates in a slave loop and only afterwards checks the valve throat will discover
the lost margin one analyser delay later. The joint program discovers it inside the shooting
node, while the dose is still a decision.

2.2      Physics-informed pressure digital twin

2.2.1     What the twin contains

The twin is a second copy of the equations of Section 2.1, with a parameter vector that
the skid is allowed to disagree about, a residual correction that is allowed to be zero, and
an interface that hands the commissioned copy to Algorithm 1. In the classification of

2.2.4     The residual network and the mass-balance gate

After the physical parameters are fixed, the flow residual on the training points is a target
for a small network. The input is the standardised vector (lh , lw , lf , Aw , Er , pm ). The
network has one hidden layer of eight hyperbolic-tangent units and a linear output of
three flow corrections. Twenty-two of the twenty-eight points train it. Six are held out.
Training is Adam for 900 steps at learning rate 0.02, with a weight decay of 2 × 10−3 .



                                               46
   Every fifth step adds a physics penalty. If q is the physically predicted flow plus the
network correction, the penalty is the squared mass imbalance

steady flow. Cai et al. [74] make the same substitution whenever a fluid constraint is a
flux balance rather than a momentum residual.

   The gate is the held-out root-mean-square flow error. If the network increases it,
the deployed twin uses ∆Q = 0. The architecture stays in the software so that a later
campaign, on a valve whose characteristic is not equal-percentage, can open the gate
without a rewrite. Wu et al. [76] and Zheng and Wu [75] deploy the network because their
reactors have a kinetic discrepancy that the parameters do not absorb. This skid, after Kv
and bh are estimated, does not. The gate is how the twin tells those two situations apart.

2.2.5     The fast state, and the deadband

During a run the slow parameters stay at the commissioned values. The mixer state is
integrated from the measured flows and the delayed mother-beer analysis. This observer
is a soft sensor in the sense of Kadlec et al. [71], and it is almost a balance rather than
a regression: if the flows and the inlet composition are right, the mixer composition is
determined. Noise of a hundredth of a percent by weight is added in the case study to

                                                47
represent the imperfection of that statement. An unscented filter [78] or a moving-horizon
estimator [79] is the right replacement when an inlet meter drops out and the outlet
analysis has to carry the state alone. With all three Coriolis meters healthy, the balance is
the estimator.

   Water-valve stiction is detected as a deadband S: the stem does not move until the
command has led the reported position by S. The case-study fault is S = 0.03 lift, with
no slip jump, which is the stick band of Choudhury et al. [80] reduced to one parameter.
The inverse used by the twin-backed controller is


                           lw,cmd = lw,des + S sign(lw,des − lw,pos ),                  (2.27)


applied only when the desired move exceeds a small threshold, so that a valve already at
the setpoint is not dithered. Jelali and Huang [81] discuss the limit cycle that a naive
inverse can excite. The threshold and the rate limit are what keep (2.27) from becoming a
knocker. The predictive controller that does not receive S has no inverse. It sees a flow
that refuses small moves and, if its gain is wrong as well, it walks away from the setpoint.



2.2.6     How the twin changes the first technology

Algorithm 1 does not contain θ. It contains a model. Commissioning replaces that model.
Three substitutions are visible in the closed loop of Chapter 3.

   The capacities and the header bias are the substitutions that repair the grade change.
An eight-percent error in Kv,h is an error of about 1 m3 h−1 at the lager point, because the
valve is not fully open and the equal-percentage gain is steep. The predictive controller
converts that flow error into a composition error and then holds it, because the model
believes the composition is already right. The ratio station, which integrates measured
flow rather than predicted flow, is less sensitive to Kv and more sensitive to the interaction
during the move. The numerical comparison falls out of that difference.

   The solubility scale and the contraction are the substitutions that keep the pressure
constraint and the alcohol conversion honest. At the design point κζ = 0.93 lowers ζ from



                                               48
the bare polynomial by seven percent and raises peq by the same fraction. A controller
that ignores the scale guards the wrong pressure. The contraction shifts density by about
4 kg m−3 on the mother beer and by about 2 kg m−3 on the lager. Alcohol by volume,
through (1.2), moves in proportion. The shift is smaller than the alcohol tolerance and is
still worth removing, because the tolerance budget should be spent on the mother beer,
not on the equation of state.

   The deadband inverse is the substitution that matters when the water valve sticks. It
does not change the steady mathematics of w. It changes the first samples after a small
flow error appears.

   The residual network, in this campaign, is the substitution that is not made. Publishing
that fact is a duty of the estimator. A twin that always adds its network, after the network
has failed a hold-out test, is a softer sensor with a harder name.



2.2.7     Information, interfaces and the safety boundary

The twin reads flows, pressures, stem positions, temperature and quality through the OPC
UA model of the skid [67]. It writes a recommended (lh , lw , lf , ṁCO2 ) at 4 s intervals. The
programmable controller remains the only device that energises a solenoid. It enforces the
rate limit again, the lift limits again, and a set of interlocks the optimiser is not allowed
to relax: a low bright-tank pressure, a high mixer pressure, a loss of the water-deaeration
permissive, and a quality hold if dissolved oxygen or a water analysis crosses the alarm.
ISA-88 names the brand change as a phase whose running state may be held by those
permissives [66]. The predictive controller is a step inside the running state. It is not a
replacement for the phase logic.

   This boundary is the practical content of the warning in Fuller et al. [63] and Goffi et al.
[68] that a twin which can move equipment has to be commissioned inside the interlocks,
not beside them. The estimation problem is unaffected. The interlocks define a subset of
u that the least-squares model never sees, because the campaign is run with the interlocks
healthy. Extrapolating the twin into a tripped skid is not a use this thesis supports.

2.2.9     The mixer as a discrete linear system

Once the flows are known, each composition channel is a linear filter. Let α = Qf ∆t/V
with ∆t = 2 s and V = 0.08 m3 at the design flow, so α = 2/14.4 = 0.139. The exact
discretisation of a single well-mixed volume is

                                                         
                               xk+1 = e−α xk + 1 − e−α xin ,                            (2.35)


and the Euler step used in the shooting code is the first-order form xk+1 = (1 − α)xk + αxin .
At this α the two gains on the old state are e−α = 0.870 and 1 − α = 0.861. The relative
difference is one percent. Over a six-step horizon the compounded difference in a step
response is a few hundredths of the step, which is smaller than the analyser noise on original
extract. Euler is kept because its derivative with respect to the flow, which changes α, is



                                               51
obvious to the shooting code. A production build can switch to (2.35) without touching
the cost.

   The four channels Aw , Er , c and cDO share α and do not share xin . Alcohol and extract
see a mass-weighted inlet. Carbon dioxide and oxygen see a volume-weighted inlet plus,
for carbon dioxide, the dose divided by Qf . The state transition is therefore diagonal. The
output map is not. Original extract and alcohol are nonlinear functions of Aw , Er and ρ,
and peq is a nonlinear function of those and of c. That is why a linear model predictive
controller built on G in (2.10) can coordinate the valves and still miss the brand: G does
not contain Balling’s denominator. The nonlinear shooting exists for the output map, not
because the mixer is a difficult dynamic system.

   A linear controller is still the right tool for the diagnosis in (2.11). It is the wrong
tool for the setpoint. The numerical size of the mistake was computed in Section 1.3: a
dilution proportional to original extract misses the true original extract by 0.19◦ P. Any
linear gain from flow ratio to original extract that was identified at one brand will carry an
offset of that order at the next brand. Integral action removes the offset after the analyser
delay. The nonlinear map removes it in the prediction.



2.2.10      What one tolerance costs, in the units of the stage cost

The denominators in (1.27) are the tolerances, so the numerical weight of each channel is
a statement about which miss the brewery will trade. It is worth evaluating the cost at a
few concrete errors so the weights are not an unexamined vector.

   One tolerance of original extract, with every other error zero, costs 1. One tolerance
of alcohol costs 1. One tolerance of carbon dioxide costs 1. A flow error of 0.5 m3 h−1 ,
which is 2.5 % of the design flow, costs 0.35. A valve move of 0.1 lift from the previous
command costs 0.4 (0.1)2 /0.01 = 0.4. A mix pressure 0.1 bar inside the forbidden side of
the margin costs 6 × 0.01 = 0.06, and 0.5 bar inside it costs 1.5. The pressure penalty
therefore overtakes a quality tolerance only when the violation is several tenths of a bar.
Small numerical noise in pm does not dominate the brand. A flashing valve does.

   Apply the same arithmetic to the infeasible mother beer of Section 3.2.4. The extract-


                                             52
only point misses alcohol by 0.207 % by volume, which is 0.207/0.03 = 6.9 tolerances and
a cost of about 48. The alcohol-only point misses original extract by 0.493◦ P, which is
9.9 tolerances and a cost of about 97. The weighted point misses extract by 0.167◦ P and
alcohol by 0.137 % by volume, a cost of (3.34)2 + (4.57)2 ≈ 32. The predictive controller is
not applying a hidden preference for extract. It is applying the squares of the published
tolerances. A brewery that considers a tenth of a percent of alcohol more serious than a
tenth of a degree Plato changes two numbers in (1.27) and recommissions nothing else.

   The flow weight is deliberately smaller than a quality tolerance. On this skid a shortfall
of one cubic metre per hour is a scheduling inconvenience, and a shortfall of one degree
Plato is a beer that cannot be released. If the skid were the filler and the shortfall were a
missed production slot of a high-volume brand, the flow weight would rise. The formulation
already has a place for that number.



2.2.11      One shooting step, in order

A single evaluation of the horizon at the warm start, which is “hold the lager command”,
during the first sample of the light-brand grade change, does the following.

   The mother-beer analysis is unchanged. The setpoint has become 9.687◦ P, 4.000 % by
volume, 5.00 g L−1 and 20 m3 h−1 . The mixer state is still the lager composition, because
the valves have not moved. Both blocks of z are (2.9). The network solve returns the lager
flows. The mixer step moves the state by α toward an inlet that is still the lager blend,
so the predicted original extract stays near 11.61◦ P. The stage cost on extract alone is
((11.61 − 9.69)/0.05)2 ≈ 1.5 × 103 per step, and six steps cost about 104 . That number is
the signal the solver follows. It is large because the beer in the vessel is still the previous
brand, which is a fact, and because the command has not yet been asked to change, which
is the degree of freedom.

   The solver then shortens the beer lift and lengthens the water lift, subject to the rate
limit of 0.16 lift per four-second sample and to the slower rate inside the prediction. The
new flows change xin through (1.3). The predicted extract falls on the time constant (2.1).
Carbon dioxide is recomputed with the new water fraction and the new dose; the dose rises


                                              53
slightly because more water has to be carbonated even though the target concentration
has fallen. The pressure residual is re-solved at every node so that the final valve opens as
the total flow and the mix density change, instead of letting the pressure loop discover
the interaction one sample later. The first block of the improved z is applied. The next
sample repeats the procedure from the new mixer state and the new previous command.

   Nothing in that sequence requires the outlet analyser. The analyser arrives 12 s later
and trims whatever the flow integration got wrong. On the commissioned model that
trim is at the level of the sensor noise. On the nominal model the trim cannot remove
a bias that the model interprets as a correct steady state, which is the tail of 0.19◦ P in
Section 3.2.3.



2.2.12     The production solver

The case-study program is a single-shooting nonlinear program in eight variables with
bound constraints and a smooth cost, apart from the hinges in the soft penalties. Those
hinges are squared, so the cost is continuously differentiable and the second derivative
jumps. A sequential quadratic solver handles that. The iteration limit of six is a real-time
budget, not an optimality certificate. Warm-starting from the previous sample is what
makes six iterations enough when the brand is not changing, and what makes the first
sample of a grade change the expensive one.

   The production form replaces those six iterations by one quadratic program per sample,
which is the real-time iteration [55, 56]. Linearise the shooting map at the previous
solution (z, x), build the quadratic model of (1.27), solve it, and apply the first block
before the next preparation phase has finished. The preparation of the derivatives can
overlap the sample. IPOPT is a reliable way to solve the quadratic model and also the
full nonlinear program during commissioning, when there is no sample clock [53]. CasADi
is a reliable way to obtain the derivatives of the network residual and of Balling’s map
without hand-written Jacobians [54]. The thesis does not depend on either package being
present in the case-study script. It depends on the shooting problem being the one those
packages would be given.


                                             54
   Two implementation constraints follow. The scalar pressure solve has to be differentiated
through, or replaced by a residual that the nonlinear program carries as an equality. Brent’s
method on a scalar is easy to run and awkward to differentiate. A production code lifts
pm into the decision vector and writes Qvalve
                                        f     − Qmass
                                                 f    = 0 as a constraint at each node.
The local derivative of that residual with respect to pm is negative and bounded away
from zero whenever both inlet valves have a positive drop, which they do inside the lift
bounds of (1.21). The constraint qualification is then the ordinary one for a hydraulic
network with a free internal pressure. The second constraint is that the rate limit and
the interlocks are enforced in the programmable controller even if the quadratic program
returns a point on the wrong side of them. Section 2.2 already assigned that duty to the
PLC. The solver is not a safety system.



2.2.13     Why roughness and minor loss share one column

The line drop (1.9) depends on ε only through f (ε) and on K directly. At a fixed Reynolds
number the map (ε, K) 7→ CL is the only map the flows can see, because CL is the only
combination that enters ∆pline . Differentiating Haaland’s formula, f is a slowly increasing
function of ε/D. A positive step in ε and a negative step in K can be chosen so that
CL does not change. The two corresponding columns of the residual Jacobian are then
scalar multiples of each other, the Gram matrix J T J is singular, and the formal standard
error (2.23) diverges. That is what Table 3.4 records, in the decision to publish CL and to
withhold a roughness.

   The multiplicity is not removed by collecting more points at the same Reynolds number.
It is weakly removed by collecting points at very different velocities, because f depends
on Re and K does not, so the two parameters are not in a fixed ratio across the campaign.
The commissioning lifts do change the flow, but not by the factor of ten that would
separate a smooth-pipe friction factor from a minor loss. A campaign designed to identify
roughness would include a near-shut final valve and a wide-open one, and would still be
limited by the fact that a hygienic bore at these Reynolds numbers sits in a flat part of
the Moody chart. The engineering quantity is CL . The thesis estimates that quantity to


                                             55
two and a half percent and treats the split as unidentified.

   The capacities do not have this problem. Kv,h scales only Qh , and it scales it with the
square root of a drop that the header bias also shifts. Varying the beer lift changes the
drop and the flow in a combination a pure bias cannot imitate, because a bias shifts every
beer-valve point by the same pressure and a capacity scales them. The standard error of
0.08 m3 h−1 on a capacity of 32 is the numerical expression of that excitation. Twenty-eight
points were more than the rank of the problem required. They were not more than the
noise required.



2.2.14      The residual network as a function

Let ξ ∈ R6 be the standardised feature (lh , lw , lf , Aw , Er , pm ), with the mean and the scale
taken from the training points only. The network is


                         h = tanh(W1 ξ + b1 ),       ∆Q = W2 h + b2 ,                      (2.36)


with W1 ∈ R8×6 and W2 ∈ R3×8 . The corrected flow is the physical prediction plus ∆Q.
The training loss on a batch is the mean square of ∆Q against the measured residual,
plus the weight decay, plus, every fifth step, a multiple of (2.26). The mass penalty is a
function of ∆Q and therefore of W2 and, through h, of W1 . Its gradient is an ordinary
back-propagation of a linear form in the three flows. No collocation point in the sense of a
space-time residual of a partial differential equation is required [72]. The conservation law
is the one the flow already lives under.

   The hold-out is six points drawn as the last six of the campaign, not re-used in the
standardisation or the training. Root-mean-square flow error rises from 0.030 m3 h−1 to
0.037 m3 h−1 when ∆Q is added. The rise is the signature of a model whose physical part
has already reached the sensor noise. Adding eight hidden units, forty-eight input weights
and twenty-four output weights to a residual whose standard deviation is 0.03 m3 h−1 on
twenty-two points is enough capacity to fit the particular noise of those twenty-two points.
The gate compares the two errors and publishes the physical prediction. The weights are
kept on disk with the campaign so that a later campaign can be scored against the same

                                               56
architecture. They are not written into the controller.

   This is the opposite deployment decision from Wu et al. [76] and Zheng and Wu [75],
and it is the same rule. They keep the network because the hold-out improves. This skid
drops the network because the hold-out does not. A twin that kept the network anyway
would be adding 0.007 m3 h−1 of invented flow, which is a third of a percent at the beer
line and a pointless risk next to a capacity estimate that is already inside 0.1 m3 h−1 .



2.2.15      Observer, delay and the information the analyser still

            carries

The fast observer is the discrete mixer (2.35) driven by measured flows. Its only unknown,
between analyser updates, is the inlet composition, and that unknown is replaced by the
last mother-beer analysis after eight seconds. The outlet analysis is a measurement of the
state that existed 12 s, or six integration steps, earlier. A correction that simply overwrites
the current state with the delayed analysis assigns today’s vessel the beer of one residence
time ago, which is the lager during the first moments of the grade change, and pulls the
prediction backwards. The correction used here is therefore applied to the innovation
of the delayed prediction: the observer stores a short buffer of predicted outlet values,
subtracts the analysis when it arrives, and distributes a fraction of that innovation onto
the current state. With healthy flow meters the fraction can be small. The case study
adds composition noise of about 0.01 percentage points by weight so that the controller
does not receive the plant state as an oracle.

   If the beer flow meter failed, this observer would integrate a lie and the outlet analysis
would become the primary measurement. That is the situation in which the moving-horizon
estimator of Rao et al. [79] earns its window, and in which an unscented filter [78] is
a cheaper approximation. The thesis does not simulate a failed meter. It names the
estimator that would replace the balance, and it keeps the balance for the campaign that
was actually run. Kalman [77] is the linear special case of both, and it is the right filter
for the pressure and flow channels, which are already linear in their noise and are used
without a window.


                                              57
   Dissolved oxygen is observed on the same analyser and is not controlled. The observer
reports it. The phase logic compares it with 30 ppb. The predictive controller includes it
in ℓsoft so that a move which would add oxygen by flashing is expensive, and so that a
move which cannot remove oxygen already present is not invented. Those are different
uses of the same measurement. Conflating them is how a ratio station gets talked into
chasing a disturbance it has no actuator for.



2.2.16      Faults the cost already contains

Four faults are inside the formulation rather than on a list of future work.

   A biased beer-header transmitter is bh . It is estimated once and subtracted. Leaving it
inside the nominal model produces the grade-change offset of Section 3.2.3.

   A wrong capacity is an element of θ. It is the difference between the two predictive
controllers in every scenario. The ratio station is less sensitive to it because the flow loops
close on measured flow. The predictive controller is more sensitive to it because the flow it
closes on is a prediction. That asymmetry is the reason the twin is not an optional display
next to an otherwise finished controller.

   An infeasible brand is a positive minimum of (1.27). The controller returns the
minimiser. It does not return a feasibility flag unless the supervisory station computes
the same one-dimensional search in w that Section 3.2.4 used and compares the two pure
compromises with the weighted point. That search is cheap and should be part of the phase
start, so that an operator sees “this mother beer cannot hit both numbers” before the
valves move. The case study performs the search offline and confirms that the closed-loop
tail matches it.

   A sticky water valve is S in (2.27). Undetected, it turns a small flow correction into a
delayed jump. Detected, it is a known lead on one command. The eight-second detection
delay in the third scenario is a stand-in for a test that looks for a command change with
no stem change [80, 81]. It is not a claim that eight seconds is the best detector.

   Faults that are not inside the formulation are a plugged injector, a failed analyser, a
bright-tank pressure below peq , and a dilution water whose calcium or oxygen certificate is

                                              58
already outside the release limit. The first three are interlocks. The fourth is a hold on
the phase. The controller is not asked to blend its way around them.



2.2.17     What each technology is for

The first technology is a way to choose u when the model is believed. It coordinates the
three valves, it uses the nonlinear quality map, and it prices a flashing vena contracta
in the same sum as a missed extract. The second technology is a way to decide whether
the model should be believed, and to correct the particular parameters that a blend skid
gets wrong: capacities, a pressure bias, a solubility scale, a density contraction, a line
resistance that must be estimated as a sum, and a deadband. A residual network is part
of the second technology and is subject to a hold-out gate.

   The next chapter builds the numerical skid on which both are estimated, and it reports
what the beer does.

3.1      System build

3.1.1     Pipes, valves and the sizing that sets the design point

The beer line is a 66 mm bore, the water line a 53 mm bore, and the blend line an 81 mm
bore, 18 m from the mixer to the final valve. Those bores keep the design velocities near
1 m s−1 , which is fast enough to stay turbulent and slow enough for a hygienic line. The
static mixer and the adjacent pipe hold 80 L. Minor losses are lumped as K = 14, a
plausible sum for a mixer, a handful of bends and two instrument tees [35, 84]. Roughness
is 2 µm, a polished stainless bore [34]. Viscosity is 2.6 mPa s on the mother beer and
1.67 mPa s on the cold water; the blend-line Reynolds number at the design point is
4.0 × 104 , so FR in (1.7) is unity and the Haaland friction factor is 0.0218.

   Valve capacities were chosen so that the solved lifts of (2.9) sit near seven tenths for
the inlets and at 0.84 for the final valve, inside the equal-percentage region and off the

3.1.2    Sensors, delays and the numbers the estimator may see

Each inlet and the product have a Coriolis measurement of flow and density [38]. Pressure
is measured at both headers, at the mix node and at the tank. Temperature is measured
and, in this study, is constant at 2 ◦ C. Stem position is measured by the positioner.
Original extract, alcohol, carbon dioxide and dissolved oxygen are measured at the outlet
with a 12 s delay and with noise of 0.008◦ P, 0.008 % by volume, 0.015 g L−1 and 1.5 ppb.
The mother-beer analysis is delayed by 8 s. The beer-header transmitter reads 0.10 bar high
until commissioning removes the bias. These are the only observations Algorithms 1 and 2
receive. The true vena-contracta pressure, the true capacities and the true contraction are
withheld and used only as the score.

   The plant density uses γ = 1.2 × 10−4 . The plant solubility scale is 0.93. The plant
capacities are the sized values above. The controller’s nominal model, until the twin is
commissioned, uses the wrong capacities, the wrong roughness, no contraction, a unit
solubility scale and the biased header. That split is the whole point of having two
technologies rather than one.

seconds, three of them in each move block. The outlet analyser contributes a delay of
twelve plant steps, and the mother-beer analysis a delay of eight. A grade change that is
“inside the band in sixteen seconds” has therefore been seen by four control samples and
has not yet been seen by the outlet analyser at all. The sixteen seconds are a statement

3.1.4      The three upsets

All three scenarios start at the lager steady point (2.9) and apply the upset at t = 20 s.
Integral absolute error is accumulated from the upset to the end of the run. Settling time
is the time after the upset at which the original-extract error enters and remains inside
0.05◦ P. If that never happens, the table records a time near the length of the run.

   The first upset is a grade change to the light brand: original extract 9.687◦ P, alcohol
4.000 % by volume, carbon dioxide 5.00 g L−1 , flow unchanged. The run lasts 150 s. This
is the feasible move. Both extract and alcohol are the w = 0.58 image of the same mother
beer, so a correct model has a solution of zero quality cost.

falls by 0.45 bar. Real extract of the mother beer rises by 0.45 percentage points and

to 5.15 g L−1 . The run lasts 160 s. Section 3.2.4 shows that this mother beer cannot hit


                                               64
the lager extract and the lager alcohol together. The upset is a test of the compromise,
not of tracking.

is told the deadband 8 s after it appears, which is a detection delay of the size discussed
by Jelali and Huang [81]. The run lasts 140 s. The oxygen half of the upset is a test of
what estimation must refuse to control.

3.2.6    Pressure drop, station by station

The hand solution of Section 2.2.8 can be read as a commissioning sheet. At the lager
point the beer header is 4.80 bar. The beer valve consumes 1.945 bar. The mix node is
2.855 bar. The line, with CL = 18.85, consumes about 0.11 bar. The final valve consumes
about 0.34 bar and delivers the beer to a tank at 2.40 bar. The throat of that valve is not
at 2.40 bar. It is at 2.28 bar, because FL = 0.86 puts the vena contracta 0.46 bar below
the upstream pressure of the valve and the recovery does not give all of that drop back
before the throat. Equilibrium is 1.905 bar. The sheet therefore has two margins, 0.95 bar
at the transmitter and 0.37 bar at the throat, and only the second one is the flash margin.

   A pressure survey that records the transmitter and not the throat will accept a final
valve with a poor recovery factor. The controller cannot see the throat either. It computes
it from FL , which is a number from the valve data sheet [31, 33], and from the pressures it
can measure. Commissioning checks the computation by confirming that the measured mix
pressure, the measured tank pressure and the measured flow are consistent with the CL
and the Kv,f in Table 3.4. They are, to the standard errors in that table. A discrepancy
larger than those standard errors is a fouled mixer or a wrong recovery factor, and it is
a reason to reopen the gate on the residual network rather than a reason to detune the
brand.

   The velocities that go with this sheet are 1.134 m s−1 on the beer, 0.762 m s−1 on the
water and 1.078 m s−1 on the product. All three are in the turbulent hygienic range.
The water line is the slowest, which is one reason a sticky water valve is the fault worth
injecting: a valve that spends its life at a modest opening and a modest velocity is the
valve whose stem is most likely to sit. The beer valve, at a higher opening and a higher
gain, shows a stick as a flow error immediately. The water valve can hide a deadband of
0.03 lift inside a flow change of less than one cubic metre per hour until the grade change
asks it to move.

3.2.7     What was estimated, and what a brewery still has to

          measure

The parameter estimation is Table 3.4: capacities and header bias to a small fraction of
their values, solubility scale and contraction from their own one-point experiments, line
resistance as a sum and not as a roughness. The quality estimation is Table 3.5 together
with the weighted point of Section 3.2.4. On a feasible grade change the commissioned
controller is inside 0.05◦ P in one residence time, and the uncommissioned predictive
controller is not better than ratio control. On an infeasible mother beer the commissioned
controller sits on the tolerance-weighted compromise to three decimal places of the steady
calculation. On a sticky water valve it holds the brand, and on oxygenated dilution water
it reports an alarm that every controller’s product confirms at 31 ppb.

   Several limits are part of the estimate rather than footnotes under it. The plant and
the twin share the same equation structure. A real trim that is not equal-percentage, or a
mixer that is not a single volume, would open the residual-network gate and should be
tested by repeating the hold-out on plant step tests before the gate is trusted. The pressure
solver and the short sequential-quadratic program are not a certified real-time iteration;
the timing result is that a 12 s horizon of this size solves in well under the 4 s sample on a
desktop, which is necessary and not sufficient for an embedded controller. The terminal
constraint that would support a stability argument was not imposed. Sensor faults were
noise and one bias, not a failed meter. The bright-tank pressure was a boundary that had
already been raised high enough to make the vena contracta feasible. A cellar that cannot
provide that pressure has a different problem, and the controller will correctly spend flow
to protect peq only inside the limits of the headers it is given.

   Foam, yeast stress and the flavour match between a high-gravity beer and a sales-
gravity beer remain the fermentation questions reviewed by Stewart [1], Gibson et al. [4]
and Bamforth [10]. The skid does not answer them. What it can answer, and what this
estimation measured, is whether the beer that dilution is capable of making is the beer
that leaves the final valve. With the three lifts and the carbon-dioxide dose chosen by the
predictive controller, and with the model of that controller commissioned by the twin,
