                    A Physics-Informed Pressure Digital Twin
                       for a High-Gravity Beer Blend Skid
                                  Department of Computer Science

                                               October 2026


                                                  Abstract
      A nonlinear predictive controller for high-gravity blending is only as good as the valve capacities
      and the header pressure it believes. This paper builds a physics-informed pressure digital twin of
      the three-valve blend network. A commissioning campaign of twenty-eight noisy steady points
      estimates the three valve capacities and a beer-header pressure bias to well inside one percent.
      Pipe roughness and the lumped minor-loss coefficient are not separately identifiable; their sum,
      an effective line loss, is recovered to about two and a half percent. A residual network with a
      mass-balance penalty is trained on the same campaign and then gated off, because the hold-out
      flow error rises from 0.030 m3 h−1 to 0.037 m3 h−1 . Deployed as the model inside the predictive
      controller, the calibrated physical twin brings a lager-to-light grade change inside 0.05◦ P in
      16 s. The same controller on the uncommissioned model leaves an offset of about 0.19◦ P. The
      twin also inverts a measured water-valve deadband. It does not, and cannot, remove dissolved
      oxygen that arrives in the dilution water.


1    Introduction
A digital twin is a model kept aligned with a particular asset and used for a decision [1]. In brewing,
published twins have been built around fermentation sampling and monitoring [2]. The blend skid
is a different asset. Its decision is a valve move on a time scale of seconds, and the parameters that
move the beer are hydraulic: three capacities Kv , a possible bias on the beer-header transmitter,
and the friction loss of the blend line. A predictive controller that uses the wrong capacities asks
for the wrong lifts. A ratio station is less exposed, because its flow loops close on measured flow.
The twin exists so that the predictive controller is entitled to its model.
   Hybrid models and physics-informed networks are the usual way to attach data to a balance
[3–5]. Process applications keep the network when the hold-out improves and use the corrected
model inside predictive control [6, 7]. The rule adopted here is the same rule with the opposite
deployment: the network is kept only if the hold-out flow error falls. On this skid it rises, and the
deployed twin is the calibrated valve law alone.

The blend line adds a Darcy loss. At a fixed Reynolds number that loss depends on roughness ε
and on the minor-loss coefficient K only through

                                       CL = f (ε) L/D + K.                                        (2)

A positive step in ε and a compensating step in K leave CL , and therefore every flow, unchanged.
The two columns of the residual Jacobian are then scalar multiples, and the formal standard
errors diverge. The engineering parameter is CL . The campaign reports that sum and withholds a
roughness.
  Capacities do not share a column. Kv,h scales only the beer flow, and a change of beer lift
changes the drop and the flow in a combination that a pure header bias cannot imitate. The
beer-header bias bh is therefore estimated jointly with the three capacities, by nonlinear least
squares on steady points. Twenty-eight points are used, with flow noise of about 0.03 m3 h−1 and
pressure noise of 0.008 bar. The header reading is high by 0.10 bar until the bias is removed. The
true line is CL = 18.85, from a Haaland friction factor at ε = 2 µm plus K = 14 [9].
   Two further scalars are not in that regression. The solubility scale κζ is a single bench
point relating dissolved carbon dioxide to equilibrium pressure. The density contraction γ in
ρ ∝ (1 − γAw Er ) is the median of Coriolis densities. Both are identifiable from measurements the
flow campaign does not contain, and both are recovered to the plant values.
   Table 1 is the result. The capacity errors are a few hundredths of a cubic metre per hour on
valves of capacity 12 to 64. The bias error is 0.002 bar. The effective loss is about 2.5 % low. That
is accurate enough for the grade change in Section 4. It is not a licence to publish ε = 2 µm as an
identified roughness. A later campaign should estimate CL directly.


3    The residual network and the gate
Let ξ be the standardised feature of the three lifts, the two mother-beer analyses and the mix
pressure. A network with eight hidden units predicts a flow correction,

                            h = tanh(W1 ξ + b1 ),       ∆Q = W2 h + b2 ,                          (3)

with W1 ∈ R8×6 and W2 ∈ R3×8 . Training minimises the squared residual against the measured
flow error, plus weight decay, plus a penalty on ∆Qh + ∆Qw − ∆Qf every fifth step. The penalty
is the mass balance the three flows already obey. It is not a collocation residual of a partial
differential equation [4].
  Six points are held out. Root-mean-square flow error on those points is 0.0296 m3 h−1 for the
physical model and 0.0365 m3 h−1 after ∆Q is added. Eight hidden units on twenty-two training
points are enough capacity to fit the noise of a residual that is already at the sensor floor. The

4     Closed-loop consequence
The consumer of the twin is a three-valve nonlinear predictive controller. Two copies are run. One
uses the nominal model: capacities 29.4, 13.2 and 58, a rougher pipe, no density contraction, and
a header bias of +0.10 bar that the model believes. The other uses Table 1, with the bias removed
from the header measurement. The ratio station does not use either model for its flow loops. All
three see the same sensor noise.
   On a lager-to-light grade change the commissioned controller is inside 0.05◦ P at 16 s, with an
extract integral of 10.75◦ P·s and a tail of +0.012◦ P . The nominal controller’s extract integral
is 32.29◦ P·s, statistically the same as the ratio station’s 31.30◦ P·s, and its tail is +0.19◦ P . The
horizon, the weights and the rate limit are identical. The difference is the parameter vector. An
uncommissioned predictive controller is the expensive choice.
   On a mother beer that cannot hit extract and alcohol together, the commissioned tail matches the
tolerance-weighted optimum computed offline: 11.777◦ P and 4.695 % by volume against 11.780◦ P
and 4.696 % by volume. The nominal controller, following a biased gain, sits elsewhere. The twin
does not create a feasible point that the mother beer does not allow. It makes the compromise the
one the tolerances define.
    On a sticky water valve of band 0.03 lift, the twin learns the band after 8 s and leads the command.

5    Conclusion
The innovation is a pressure twin whose parameters are the ones the blend flows can actually
see, together with an explicit refusal to deploy a residual correction that fails the hold-out. Valve
capacities and the header bias are identified to a fraction of a percent. Roughness and minor loss
are identified only as the sum CL , to about two and a half percent. The residual network is part of
the method because the gate is part of the method: the correct action on this campaign is to leave
it off. Used as the model of a three-valve predictive controller, the twin is what turns a program
that ties ratio control into a program that meets a feasible brand change in 16 s and an infeasible
brand at the weighted compromise. The study commissions a simulated skid whose equations the
estimator already knows in structure. A trim that is not equal-percentage, or a mixer that is not
one volume, would be a reason to reopen the gate on new step tests. It would not be a reason to
skip the hold-out.
