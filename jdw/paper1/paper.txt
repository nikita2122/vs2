             Three-Valve Nonlinear Model Predictive Control
                    for High-Gravity Beer Blending
                                 Department of Computer Science

                                             October 2026


                                                Abstract
     High-gravity blending dilutes a concentrated mother beer with cold deaerated water. The
     actuators are three control valves—beer, dilution water, and the line leaving the skid—and a
     carbon-dioxide injector. Industrial practice closes a flow ratio and a separate pressure loop.
     That station can hold a quiet point. It does not plan a brand change, and it has no prediction
     of the pressure at which carbon dioxide leaves solution inside a valve. This paper puts the
     three lifts and the carbon-dioxide dose in one nonlinear model predictive program. Original
     extract is the Balling map, so extract and alcohol are not treated as two linear dilutions of one
     ratio. The throat pressure of each valve is required to stay above the composition-dependent
     equilibrium pressure. On an 80 L mixer at 20 m3 h−1 , the controller, given a commissioned
     hydraulic model, brings a lager-to-light grade change inside 0.05◦ P in 16 s. The same program
     on a biased nominal model leaves an offset of about 0.19◦ P and matches a well-tuned ratio
     station on extract. When the mother beer cannot meet extract and alcohol together, the
     program returns the tolerance-weighted compromise.


1    Introduction
High-gravity brewing ferments a wort stronger than the beer that will be sold and restores sales
strength by adding water [1, 2]. The blend skid is the step that sets original extract, alcohol and
carbonation. Commercial frames quote tolerances of about 0.05◦ P and 0.03 % by volume and
implement them with ratio control [3, 4]. Foam, yeast stress and flavour differences of the strong
fermentation are upstream of this skid. Dissolved oxygen that arrives in the dilution water is
likewise outside the authority of the valves. What the valves can still damage is the brand itself: a
wrong ratio, or a throat pressure low enough to strip carbon dioxide.
   The relative-gain array at the design point is diagonally dominant, so a decentralised pairing
of beer lift with beer flow, water lift with water flow, and final lift with mix pressure is a fair
steady-state station [5, 6]. The difficulty is the transient and the output map. Original extract is
not proportional to the mother-beer mass fraction. A ratio taken from original extracts misses
the true extract by about 0.19◦ P, four times the release tolerance. Carbon dioxide equilibrium
pressure depends on temperature, composition and concentration [7, 8]. A pressure loop with a
fixed setpoint does not follow that pressure when the brand changes.
  Model predictive control is the standard way to move several constrained actuators against a
nonlinear output map [9, 10]. Brewing applications have used it on fermentation temperature
over days. The contribution here is the hydraulic program for this skid: three valve lifts and
the carbon-dioxide dose, predicted with the Balling map and with the vena-contracta constraint
written on carbon-dioxide equilibrium pressure rather than on the vapour pressure of water.

Euler steps of 2 s, so the horizon is 12 s, about 0.83 residence times. The decision vector has length
eight. Lifts stay in [0.18, 0.90], the dose in [0, 15] grams per second, and the lift may change by at
most 0.16 in one sample. The stage cost normalises each quality error by its tolerance,

One tolerance of extract, alcohol or carbon dioxide costs 1. A flow error of 0.5 m3 h−1 costs 0.35.
A throat or mix pressure inside the forbidden side of the margin is penalised at 6 per bar squared,
so a flash outranks a quality tolerance and a small pressure noise does not. The case-study solver
is a warm-started sequential quadratic program with an iteration limit of six. A production
implementation would replace those iterations by one real-time iteration [10, 12]. The study does
not impose a terminal constraint, and it does not claim a Lyapunov certificate [13].
   The baseline is an incremental ratio station sized so that KI Gii ∆t is near 0.3 on the diagonal
gains [14]. Its pressure setpoint is fixed at the lager equilibrium plus 0.90 bar. It inverts Balling for
a feedforward mass fraction and trims extract with a slow integral. It cannot move the pressure
setpoint when peq changes, and it cannot trade extract against alcohol when both targets are
infeasible.

4    Numerical estimation
The plant is a dynamic simulation, not a brewery trial. Sensor noise is shared across controllers.
The outlet analysis is delayed 12 s; the mixer state used by the predictive controller is a flow
integration. Three upsets are applied at t = 20 s.
   On the grade change the commissioned model spends the whole rate limit on the first move:
every lift changes by 0.16. Beer flow falls from about 13.8 m3 h−1 to 6.4 m3 h−1 and water flow
rises to about 10.2 m3 h−1 , so the inlet is lighter than the light brand. Extract error falls from
1.92◦ P to 0.87◦ P in four seconds. A pure residence-time decay of the same step would still be at
1.46◦ P. The error enters and remains inside 0.05◦ P at 16 s. The extract integral is 10.75◦ P·s. The
ratio station and the predictive controller on the nominal model finish at 31.30◦ P·s and 32.29◦ P·s,
and neither enters the band. The nominal tail is +0.19◦ P . Valve travel is about 1.7 lift-units for
both predictive controllers and 7.1 for the ratio station, which reverses while the vessel is still
exchanging. Figure 1 shows the three quality traces.
   The second upset makes the brand infeasible. Mother-beer real extract rises by 0.45 percentage
points and alcohol falls by 0.25, and the beer header drops by 0.45 bar. The mass fraction that
minimises the tolerance cost is w = 0.712, at 11.780◦ P and 4.696 % by volume. The commissioned
tail is 11.777◦ P and 4.695 % by volume. The ratio station is extract-greedy. The nominal predictive
controller chases alcohol, because its gain is wrong. No second ratio creates the missing alcohol.
   The third upset raises dilution-water oxygen from 12 ppb to 85 ppb and sticks the water valve
with a deadband of 0.03 lift. Product oxygen reaches about 31 ppb for every controller. The valves
do not remove it. With the deadband inverted, the commissioned extract integral is 1.29◦ P·s and
the error never leaves the band. The nominal program, which does not know the stick, is the worst
of the three stations on extract.

infeasible mother beer it spends the only ratio on extract. The predictive program returns the
weighted compromise when both targets cannot be met, and it keeps the throat pressure above
equilibrium at the design point used here. Those closed-loop numbers hold when the hydraulic
model has been commissioned. On the nominal model the same program does not improve extract
over the ratio station. The companion paper treats that commissioning problem. The study is a
reproducible simulation of one skid. It is not a plant trial, and the short-horizon solver used here
is not a certified real-time iteration.
