Abstract
A school bag factory is a flexible job shop with a particular kind of coupling. Cutting,
embroidery, sewing, hardware assembly, inspection, and packing can each run on more
than one machine, but an operation is not free to start merely because a machine is idle. It
also needs a qualified operator, and often a shared token such as a cutting die or a colour-
matched thread lot. While the schedule is running, the machines are not constant. Cutter
wear, needle temperature, motor current, and thread-break counts change the time and the
energy of the next cycle, and they change the chance that the cycle will be interrupted by
a breakdown. The planner therefore has several targets at once: a short makespan, small
tardiness against order due dates, low energy, balanced workload, and few failures. A rule
that is excellent for one target is routinely poor for another.
This thesis studies that planning problem and proposes two technologies that are de-
signed to work as one system. The first, called resource-coupled heterogeneous graph at-
tention scheduling (RCH-GAT), represents the shop as a graph of operations, machines,
operators, and shared resource tokens. A preference-conditioned attention policy chooses,
at each decision epoch, an operation together with a machine and a resource bundle. One
policyservesafamilyoftargetweights, sothesamesystemcanbeaskedtoprotectduedates
in one shift and energy in the next. The second technology, called the status-synchronized
digital twin with chance-constrained rescheduling (SSDT-CC), keeps a live degradation
state for every machine. Processing times become functions of that state. A chance con-
straint refuses an assignment whose predicted failure probability exceeds a set tolerance,
and inserts a short preventive maintenance instead. Rescheduling is driven by the twin, not
by a fixed clock: the policy is called again when health, a breakdown, or a newly released
rush order makes the current plan unreliable.
The first chapter reviews the two bodies of work these technologies come from, and sets
the factory problem in a form that those bodies of work do not quite cover. The second
chapter gives the models, the formulas, the decision procedures, and worked numerical
steps. The third chapter describes the system that was built around the factory and reports
a controlled estimation on a school-bag shop simulator. The estimation program is part of
the thesis. On the nominal order book the full system improves the joint cost relative to
first-in-first-out, shortest processing time, earliest due date, and apparent tardiness cost,

Chapter 1
Research of Previous Work
A school bag looks like a simple product. The shop that makes it is not simple. One order
asks for a navy backpack in three sizes, the next for a red trolley bag with a short due
date, and a third for a khaki sports sack that must share the only khaki thread lot with
work already on a machine. The auto cutter is fast and already worn. The second cutter
is slow and healthy. Three sewing operators do not all have the same skill on the bartack
machine. Embroidery has a single head. Inspection and packing share people with the
hardware bench. A planner who treats this shop as a list of jobs and a list of machines
will build a schedule that looks feasible on paper and then loses the afternoon to a colour
change, a missing operator, or a cutter that was asked to run past a sensible wear limit.
The scheduling literature already contains powerful answers to pieces of this picture.
Flexible job shops, dispatching rules, evolutionary multi-objective search, and, more re-
cently, graph neural networks trained by reinforcement learning, all decide which operation
should run on which machine. A second literature, built around cyber-physical systems,
digital twins, and remaining-useful-life models, decides what a machine’s present health
means for the next hour. Textile research has started to treat operators as a second re-
source. Almost none of these lines decides the school-bag problem as it actually arrives:
several targets, a resource bundle around every operation, and a machine state that is
allowed to change the plan.
This chapter reviews those lines in the order the thesis uses them. Section 1.1 reviews
scheduling technology, from classical job shops to heterogeneous graph policies, and states
what that technology still does not represent. Section 1.2 reviews machine-status technol-
ogy, from degradation models to digital twins that influence a schedule, and states where

The integer-programming tradition begins with Manne’s assignment of binary order vari-

A school bag factory is not a classical job shop. The body of a backpack can be sewn on
either lockstitch machine. A strap can go to the bartack machine or, more slowly, to a lock-
stitch. Cutting can go to the automatic cutter or to the manual cutter. Brucker and Schlie

later work is the flexible job shop scheduling problem, in which each operationOij has an
eligible setM ij and a processing timepijk on each machinek∈M ij. Brandimarte solved

and used a localisation heuristic followed by an evolutionary search [10]. Genetic algo-
rithms with careful encodings became a standard benchmark family [11, 12, 13]. Chaudhry
and Khan reviewed the technique catalogue and showed how large it had become: dispatch-
ing rules, shifting bottlenecks, genetic algorithms, particle swarm methods, and hybrids,
almost all of them aimed at makespan on static instances [14].

The mathematical skeleton that later learning methods still use is the disjunctive graph
of Roy and Sussmann [4]. Operations are nodes. A conjunctive arc fixes the route inside
a job. A disjunctive pair connects two operations that might share a machine, and a
schedule is a choice of one direction for every pair, plus an assignment of flexible operations
to machines. Giffler and Thompson had already given a constructive way to build active
schedules, in which no operation can be moved earlier without pushing another operation
later [3]. Almost every genetic algorithm and every reinforcement-learning policy in this
chapter is, at bottom, a way of choosing those directions.

1.1.2 Dispatching rules and why they are still the factory default
Before metaheuristics and before learning, factories scheduled with priority rules. Pan-
walkar and Iskander collected more than a hundred of them [15]. Blackstone, Phillips,
and Hogg compared the rules that shops actually use [16]. Four of them matter for the
estimation in Chapter 3, because they are the baselines a production manager will believe.
First-in-first-out releases work in the order it became ready. It is fair and it is blind.
Shortest processing time clears small operations and often keeps the mean flow time down,
at the cost of starving a long trolley-bag shell. Earliest due date sorts by the order’s promise
date and is the natural rule when tardiness is the complaint that reaches the planner. The
apparent-tardiness-cost rule of Vepsalainen and Morton combines the two ideas [17]. For

discovered from the shop’s own transitions. They inherit the same three limits unless the
state is deliberately enlarged.
1.1.3 Evolutionary search for several targets
When a schedule can be encoded as a chromosome, a population can search it. The flexible-
job-shop encodings of Pezzella, Morganti, and Ciaschetti [11], Gao, Sun, and Gen [12], and
Li and Gao [13] assign each operation a machine and a priority, then decode the chromosome
with a list schedule that respects precedence. Hybrids of genetic search and tabu search or
variable-neighbourhood descent improve the makespan on the standard Brandimarte and
Kacem sets. They are offline procedures. A population of thirty individuals, each decoded
by a full shop simulation, is a reasonable night-time calculation and a poor answer to an
order that arrives at 10:15 with a same-day promise.
Multi-objective evolutionary algorithms changed the question from “the best schedule”
to “the set of schedules that are not beaten on every target.” Deb, Pratap, Agarwal,
and Meyarivan’s NSGA-II sorts a population into nondominated fronts and uses crowding
distance to keep the front spread out [18]. Zitzler and Thiele’s strength Pareto approach,
and the later hypervolume indicator, gave the field a way to say whether one front is better
than another [19, 20]. When the number of objectives passes three, crowding in NSGA-

===== PAGE 16 =====

II stops spreading the population well. Deb and Jain’s NSGA-III replaces crowding with
reference directions [21]. Gong, De Pessemier, Martens, and Joseph used that algorithm
on a flexible job shop whose five objectives were makespan, energy cost, labour cost, peak
workload, and total workload, under a time-varying electricity price [22]. Their result is
important for this thesis for two reasons. It shows that labour is a real objective, not a soft
constraint hidden inside the processing time. And it shows that a many-objective genetic
algorithm can expose the conflict between energy and labour, because the cheap electricity
hours are often the expensive wage hours.
Energy had already been brought into scheduling by Mouzon, Yildirim, and Twomey,
who showed that simply turning idle machines off is not the whole problem: the schedule
itself decides when the large loads run [23]. Dai, Tang, Giret, and Salido added trans-
portation time and energy to a flexible job shop and solved the bi-objective model with an
enhanced genetic algorithm [24]. Sequence-dependent setups, surveyed by Allahverdi and
co-authors across two large reviews, sit in the same family [25, 26]. A colour change on a
sewing machine is exactly such a setup. None of these papers, however, treats the setup,
the operator, the energy, and a live wear state as inputs of one online decision.
The practical objection to the evolutionary line, for the factory considered here, is not
that the algorithms are weak. On a frozen instance they are often the quality ceiling. The
objection is that the ceiling is computed for a shop that holds still. A school-bag week does
not hold still. Health moves with every minute of cutting. A rush trolley order is released
at mid-morning. A thread lot runs out. Re-evolving a population at every one of these
events is possible and usually too slow for the dispatch decision, which is “what should
the free operator start now.” The learning policies of the next subsection are attractive
because, after training, that decision is a forward pass.
1.1.4 Reinforcement learning for dispatching
Reinforcement learning treats scheduling as a sequence of decisions. The state is the shop.
The action is the next dispatch. The reward is progress toward the objective. Sutton and
Barto’s framework is the one used throughout [27]. Mnih and co-authors showed that a
deep network can learn an action value from raw high-dimensional states [28]. Schulman
and co-authors’ proximal policy optimisation then became the default stable policy-gradient
method for continuous training runs: a clipped ratio keeps a new policy from moving so far
that the data just collected become useless [29]. Haarnoja and co-authors’ soft actor-critic
13

===== PAGE 17 =====

adds an entropy bonus so that the policy does not collapse early [30]. Scheduling papers
use these algorithms; they do not need to reinvent them. The design problem is the state
and the action.
Zhang, Song, Cao, Zhang, Tan, and Xu trained a graph network to dispatch a classical
job shop [31]. The disjunctive graph is the state. A graph neural network embeds every
operation. The policy picks the next operation at a machine. Because the network shares
weights across nodes, a policy trained on modest instances can be applied to larger ones.
That size-agnostic property is the reason later factory work preferred graphs to fixed-length
vectors. Park, Chun, Kim, Kim, and Park reported a related graph-and-reinforcement-
learning design for job shops [32]. Luo studied the flexible job shop when new jobs are
inserted, and showed that a deep policy can react without rebuilding a genetic population
[33]. Lei, Guo, Zhao, Wang, Qian, Meng, and Tang separated the flexible-job-shop action
into several heads, so that operation choice and machine choice are not forced through a
single flat list [34].
These papers moved the field from “learn a rule for a fixed shop” to “learn a rule on
a graph that can grow.” They still optimise, in the typical experiment, one time-based
objective, and the graph still contains operations and machines only.
1.1.5 Heterogeneous graphs and multi-target policies
Flexibility makes the graph heterogeneous. An operation is not the same kind of node as
a machine, and the relation “can be processed on” is not the same relation as “precedes.”
Song, Chen, Li, and Cao used that fact directly [35]. Their IEEE Transactions on Industrial
Informatics model extends the disjunctive graph with machine nodes, defines an action as
a composite choice of an operation and a machine, and encodes the state with a two-
stage heterogeneous graph network. Proximal policy optimisation trains the policy. The
published result is that the learned rule beats hand-written rules and still runs quickly on
instances larger than the training set. This is the closest ancestor of the first technology in
this thesis. It solves the representation problem that plain job-shop graphs do not solve: a
machine has its own embedding, and the action is a pair rather than an operation index.
Two limits of that ancestor matter here. The machine node carries scheduling status,
such as the time it becomes free, but it does not carry a degradation state that changes
the processing time. And there is still no node for the operator or for a shared token.

1.1.6 Textile shops and the second resource
General flexible-job-shop benchmarks are machining benchmarks. A school bag is closer to
a sewing shop. Chen, Xie, Ma, Chen, and Wang formulated a textile flexible job shop and
modified ant-colony optimisation so that the pheromone remembers the product as well as
the machine, with a heuristic that lifts machines which the search would otherwise ignore
[44]. The objective is balance of machine time across the operations of the textile route.
The paper is useful because it shows that a textile route is not an arbitrary chain: machines
and product types constrain each other, and a search that ignores the product will over-use
the convenient machine. It does not model operators as competing resources, and it does
not model health.
Sewing research has a name for the operator constraint. A dual-resource-constrained
shop is one in which a station needs both a machine and a worker, and workers are fewer or
less flexible than machines. Xu, Xu, and Xie reviewed that literature [45]. The consequence
for dispatching is immediate. A machine that is free in the data table may be unusable
because the only qualified operator is inside another operation. Sequence-dependent setups
apply to the operator as well as to the machine: a change of colour, of needle, or of bag style
costs minutes of the person, not only of the head. Gong and co-authors’ labour-aware model
[22] prices this effect. It still plans labour as a cost accumulated by a static chromosome,
not as a token the online policy must seize and release.
Rework belongs in the same practical family. Flapper, Fransoo, Broekmeulen, and In-
derfurth reviewed planning when a quality failure sends work back [46]. A school-bag
inspection can fail a zipper or a crooked strap. The estimation shop in Chapter 3 keeps in-
spection as a required operation rather than a random rework loop, so that the comparison
of schedulers is not mixed with a second random process. The formulation in Chapter 2
leaves a place for a rework arc; the numerical shop does not draw one.

1.1.7 The gap that the first technology is built to close
The scheduling literature, read against a school-bag route, supports five conclusions.
The problem is a flexible job shop, and it is reasonable to keep the disjunctive-graph view
of precedence and machine choice [4, 35]. The action, however, is larger than a machine
assignment. It includes an operator whose skill changes the processing time, and a token
whose capacity is one or two, not the number of machines. A graph that has no operator
node and no token node cannot attend to the contention that actually blocks the hardware
bench and the khaki lot.
The targets are plural. Makespan research [9, 11, 31, 35] is the right foundation and
the wrong single number. Energy [23, 24, 22, 36], due dates [17, 37], and workload balance
[10, 44, 22] have each been studied. A factory policy has to carry them together, and a
manager has to be able to move the emphasis without training an entirely new system.
Preference-conditioned decisions are the natural shape of that requirement. The recent
multi-agent critics [36, 38, 37] move in this direction and still do not condition a resource-
bundle action on a preference vector tied to bag-factory tokens.
Theusefulonlineobjectisalearneddispatchpolicy, notapopulationthatisre-evolvedat
every breakdown. Graph policies are the ones that transfer across order-book sizes [31, 35].
They are also the ones that can accept an extra node type without a new hand-written
feature hierarchy.
Setups are sequence dependent and, in this factory, mostly colour changes [26]. A policy
that does not see the previous colour on the machine will pay the setup in the simulation
and never in the decision.
No paper in this section writes the machine’s wear into the same graph that chooses the
operator and the thread lot. That omission is not a small missing feature. It is a second
technology, reviewed next, which this thesis couples to the first instead of leaving it as a
report beside the plan.

1.2 Research on machine status and digital twins
1.2.1 Status is a signal, not a label
A machine status that a scheduler can use is a number with a clock. “The cutter is fine” is
not such a number. The cutter’s vibration, the current of its feed motor, the temperature
at the knife, the count of thread breaks on a sewing head, and the hours since the last knife
change are such numbers. They arrive from a controller, they are noisy, and they are late
by whatever period the collector uses.
Two standards make this more than a vendor choice. IEC 62264, the standard built
on ISA-95, separates the enterprise plan from the control system and defines the objects
that pass between them: equipment, material lots, personnel, and process segments [47].
IEC 62541, OPC Unified Architecture, is the usual way those equipment objects expose
live values to a client outside the programmable controller [48]. A scheduler that claims to
react to machine status is, in factory terms, a client of this stack. It does not need to own
the sensors. It needs a defined place where a health value enters the decision.
Lee, Bagheri, and Kao organised the same idea as a five-level architecture for cyber-
physical manufacturing: a smart connection, conversion of data to information, a cyber
model, cognition, and configuration of the physical system [49]. Monostori and co-authors
reviewed cyber-physical production systems as the place where this loop has to be closed,
and listed scheduling among the decisions that still sit too far from the physical state [50].
Xu, Xu, and Li’s survey of Industry 4.0 makes the same point at the level of the whole plant:
connectivity is no longer the scarce piece, and decision models that use the connectivity are
[51]. KorenandShpitalni’sreconfigurablemanufacturingsystemsaddadesignconsequence.
Iftheplantcanchangeitsrouting, thescheduleispartoftheconfiguration, notafilewritten
once per shift [52].
The school-bag factory does not need a new network architecture. It needs the cognition
level of Lee’s stack to be a scheduler, and the cyber level to be a degradation model whose
output is a feature of that scheduler. The next subsections review the models that can sit
at that cyber level.

1.2.2 Digital twins of a production system
A digital twin is a living model paired with a particular physical system, updated by
that system’s data, and used to make a decision about that system. Grieves and Vickers
stated the idea as a way to see undesirable behaviour before the physical system has to
exhibit it [53]. Tao, Zhang, Liu, and Nee reviewed industrial practice and separated a
digital twin from a plain simulation by the requirement of ongoing synchronisation [54].
Qi and Tao compared the twin with big-data manufacturing and argued that the twin is
the structure that makes data actionable [55]. Negri, Fumagalli, and Macchi reviewed the
role of twins inside cyber-physical production and found a wide spread of meanings, from
geometric models to scheduling models [56]. Cimino, Negri, and Fumagalli followed the
manufacturing applications and found the same spread [57]. Fuller, Fan, Day, and Barlow
listed enabling technologies and the open problems, among them the difficulty of keeping
the model and the asset in step [58]. Lu, Liu, Wang, Huang, and Xu proposed a reference
model for a twin-driven manufacturing system and again placed production planning among
the applications that the reference model must be able to host [59].
For this thesis the word “twin” is used in a narrow way. The twin of a school-bag machine
is not a three-dimensional drawing of the cutter. It is a statehk(t)for each machinek, a
rule that updateshk from the latest signals, and a pair of maps fromhk to the quantities
the scheduler needs: a processing-time multiplier and a probability that the next cycle is
interrupted. If those maps are not in the scheduling decision, the factory has a dashboard,
not a twin of the plan.

1.2.4 Maintenance inside the production schedule
The closer line of work inserts maintenance into the job shop itself. A 2024 study of bi-
objective flexible job shop scheduling with condition-based maintenance updates the degra-
dation parameters by Bayesian inference after each maintenance, and uses an evolutionary
algorithm with an insertion step so that a maintenance window disturbs the production
plan less than a naive split would [70]. The objectives are production and maintenance
together. The algorithm is offline. Machine health changes the feasibility of a chromosome;
it is not a feature of a learned online policy, and operators are not part of the chromosome.
Pandhare, Negri, Ragazzini, Cattaneo, Macchi, and Lee described a prognostics-enabled
twin that schedules a flow shop while equipment is degrading [71]. Field data, reduced
by principal components, drive a discrete-event model. A genetic algorithm searches the
schedule. The reported comparison is that a twin which knows the degraded state estimates
makespan more realistically than a twin which pretends the equipment is nominal, and that
the resulting schedule is better for that reason. The paper is the clearest statement that
health belongs inside scheduling. It is a flow shop, so routing flexibility is absent. It has
no operator tokens and no thread lots. The search is a genetic algorithm, so a rush order
or a sudden jump in a health residual means another population, not a masked action in a
policy that is already trained.
An online joint scheme for predictive maintenance and production planning, of the kind
discussed by Zhu, Wang, and Yuan, uses a rolling horizon: at each period the latest
remaining-useful-life estimate and the latest demand forecast are written into a shorter
optimisation, and only the first period is implemented [72]. Rolling horizons are the right
operational pattern for a planner who re-solves. They are heavy if the inner model is a full
flexible job shop with resource tokens, and they are unnecessary if a trained policy can be

1.2.5 The gap that the second technology is built to close
Four observations close the review.
Health is a state variable with a stochastic increment, not a static penalty. Wiener and
Bayesian residual-life models already supply the increment [60, 61, 63]. A scheduler that
reads a health value once per shift is ignoring the model it paid to build.
The map from health to the shop has two outputs, and both belong in the decision. The
processing time grows as the machine leaves its nominal condition, and the probability of
an interruption grows with it. Energy grows as well, because a worn drive draws more for
the same piece. A makespan model with nominal times will prefer the fast worn cutter and
then spend the saved minutes on a repair.
The maintenance action has to be cheap enough, and visible enough, to be chosen by
the same policy that chooses the backpack. A separate maintenance optimiser that runs
overnight will insert windows that the morning rush has already invalidated. A chance
constraint of the form (1.4), evaluated at the dispatch epoch, is the lightest coupling that
still refuses a dangerous assignment [66].
The twin must be allowed to force a new decision. Synchronisation that only refreshes
a dashboard does not changeCj. Event-triggered rescheduling, called when the predicted
failure probability or a newly released order crosses a threshold, is the configuration step
in the cyber-physical stack of Lee, Bagheri, and Kao [49]. The trigger condition is part of
the technology, not an implementation detail.

1.3 Problem set
1.3.1 The school-bag route
The product family in this thesis is the ordinary school bag: a soft-sided pack carried by
a student, together with two cousins that share the same shop, a trolley bag and a sports
sack. The factory is imagined as a single-site cell, not a supply chain. Cloth, webbing,
zippers, and hardware arrive as lots. Finished bags leave in cartons. The planning horizon
of the estimation is one congested day, long enough for wear and colour changes to matter
and short enough that the order book is a list rather than a season.
A classic student backpack crosses eight steps. The main shell is cut. The lining is cut. A
logo is embroidered or printed. The body, including the compartment seams, is sewn. The
shoulder straps and handle are sewn, preferably on a bartack or heavy lockstitch because the
webbing is thick. The zipper, buckles, and adjusters are attached at the hardware bench.
The bag is inspected. It is packed. The two cutting steps both need a die. Embroidery and
the sewing steps need a thread lot of the order’s colour. Hardware, inspection, and packing
do not.
A trolley bag crosses seven steps: cutting the shell, reinforcing the base, sewing the shell,
fitting the frame, fitting the wheels, inspection, and packing. The frame and the wheels
share the hardware bench, so a trolley order holds that bench longer than a backpack
does. A sports sack is the short route: cut, print, sew, drawstring, inspect, and pack. It
is the order a shortest-processing-time rule will always prefer, and the order that fills the
embroidery head while a trolley shell waits.
The eligible machines are deliberately uneven. Two cutters share the cutting group.
The automatic cutter is fast, high-powered, and, in the case studied in Chapter 3, already
worn at the start of the day. The manual cutter is slow, cheap in power, and healthy.
One embroidery head serves every logo. Two lockstitch machines and one bartack machine
serve the sewing and reinforcement steps, with a strap allowed to fall back from the bartack
onto a lockstitch. One hardware bench, one inspection table, and one packing table finish
the route. This mix is a partial flexible job shop in the sense of Brandimarte [9]: some
operations have a real choice, and some have only one machine.
Colour is a setup, not only a bill-of-material attribute. If a sewing machine or the
embroidery head moves from navy to red, the operator changes thread and the machine

1.3.5 The two technologies, stated as contributions
The first technology is resource-coupled heterogeneous graph attention scheduling (RCH-
GAT). The shop graph contains four node types that the assignment actually touches: the
operation, the machine, the operator, and the shop-level contention for tokens. Relation-
specific attention pools those nodes into a score. The score is conditioned on the preference
vectorω. Infeasible bundles, including bundles that fail skill, precedence, machine capacity,
or token capacity, are masked before the score is used. The training objective in the
full model is proximal policy optimisation of this masked, preference-conditioned policy.
The estimation system of Chapter 3 implements the same score in a one-hop form whose
coefficients are identified by cross-entropy search, so that every reported number comes
from an executable policy rather than from an unreported training run. Section 2.1 writes
the full model. The identification procedure is part of the system build.
The second technology is the status-synchronized digital twin with chance-constrained
rescheduling (SSDT-CC). Each machine carries a health state. The state scales processing
time and energy, and it supplies the failure probability of the cycle about to be started.
If that probability exceeds a toleranceε, or if the predicted health at the end of the cycle
exceeds a wear ceiling, the decision inserts a short preventive maintenance, renews the
health, and recomputes the cycle. The policy is an online policy, so a new health value is
visible at the next dispatch without a separate rescheduling project. The chance constraint
is what turns the twin from a display into a veto. Section 2.2 writes the state, the constraint,
and the coupling back into the graph features of the first technology.
The two technologies are one system. RCH-GAT without the twin can still prefer a
healthy machine, but it cannot renew a worn one. The twin without RCH-GAT can still
maintain the cutter, but it will maintain whatever machine a shortest-processing-time rule
happens to overload. Chapter 3 estimates them separately and together for that reason.
1.3.6 Questions the estimation is required to answer
The estimation is not a collection of illustrations. It is required to answer four questions
about the problem just stated.
First, on a nominal school-bag book, does the coupled system reduce makespan, energy,
27

===== PAGE 31 =====

imbalance, and expected failures relative to first-in-first-out, shortest processing time, ear-
liest due date, and apparent tardiness cost, without giving those rules a large advantage
on tardiness? A system that saves energy by missing every due date has not solved a
multi-target problem.
Second, when the day is stressed, does the same system hold up? The stresses used in
Chapter 3 are a mid-day rush release, an accelerated wear regime, and a thread-lot shortage.
These are the three ways the problem set says the shop escapes a static flexible-job-shop
benchmark.
Third, which technology produces which part of the gain? Removing resource features
and removing status-and-maintenance are the two ablations. If both removals do nothing,
the technologies are not doing the work the model description claims.
Fourth, does the chance-constraint tolerance behave like a real knob? Tighteningε
should buy fewer expected failures at the price of more preventive maintenance. If the
estimation cannot show that exchange, the constraint is only a story.
A fifth, softer question is how stable the tardiness comparison is. Total tardiness is a
sum of a few late orders, so its standard deviation across random books is expected to be
large. The estimation reports that spread instead of treating a small difference in the mean
as a ranking.

2.1.10 What the mask guarantees
Proposition 2.1.Suppose every order has a finite route, every operation has at least one
eligible machine group, every machine group has at least one operator with positive skill,
and every token pool that an operation requires has capacity at least one. Then Algorithm 2
finishes every released order in finite time, and every started bundle satisfies precedence,
machine capacity, operator capacity, and token capacity.
Proof.Every started bundle is taken fromF, andFcontains only bundles whose prede-
cessor is finished, whose machine and operator are free, whose skill is positive, and whose
token pool has a free unit. Capacity and precedence therefore hold by construction of the
mask. A started bundle occupies its machine, operator, and token for a finite duration,
because base times, size factors, and health factors are finite and health is clipped. At the
occupation’s end the three resources are released. IfFis empty and work remains, either a
resource is occupied, in which case the clock advances to a release, or an order is unreleased,
in which case the clock advances to its release time. The state cannot cycle: each advance
of the clock to a completion finishes one operation, and there are finitely many operations.
Hence the loop ends with every operation finished.
The proposition is about feasibility, not about optimality. A first-in-first-out policy
satisfies it and still produces the poor tardiness reported in Chapter 3. The proposition
is what makes the comparison in that chapter a comparison of decisions rather than a
comparison of repair heuristics. Every method in the estimation, including the classical
rules, dispatches through the same mask. They differ only in the score that ranksF.

2.1.15 Scaling, and what would break it

2.2.10 How many decisions a day contains
A book of sixteen orders has, on the routes of Section 1.3, somewhere between six and eight
operations each, so the cell starts on the order of one hundred and twenty bundles. Each
bundle is one call to the scorer. At a typical call the feasible set is not one hundred and
twenty. Precedence keeps only the next operation of each released order, and capacity keeps
only the free machines and free operators. A call with eight ready operations, two legal
machines, and two legal operators is a few dozen scores, each a constant-time evaluation of
Equations (2.13)–(2.30) and, when the twin is on, Algorithm 3. The day is a few thousand
such scores. That is why identification, which repeats the day a few thousand times, finishes
as a batch job, and why dispatch, which does it once, is not the thing a planner waits for.
The multi-layer model changes the constant, not the number of decisions. Each layer
costs a pass over the edges of the candidate graph. Four nodes and a handful of typed
edges remain a small graph. The reason to defer that model is not computer time. It is the
absence, in this thesis, of a plant large enough for the hand-grouped keys to be the wrong
inductive bias. Twenty identified coefficients already expose which costs matter. A deeper
network would hide that exposure unless its attention weights were read back out, which is
possible and was not necessary here.
The evolutionary reference pays a different bill. A population of eighteen, decoded twelve
times and then doubled inside the generational pool, is a few hundred full days of simulation
for one book, and it returns a front for that book only. Repeating it at every rush release
is the cost Section 1.1 objected to. The online score pays the identification once and then
pays one argmin per free resource.
2.2.11 Where the disjunctive graph went
It is reasonable to ask whether the four-node candidate graph replaced the disjunctive
graph or merely decorated it. Precedence is still a chain of conjunctive arcs: an operation
entersR(t)only when its predecessor has finished. Machine capacity is still disjunctive:
two bundles share a machine only in sequence, because a busy machine is not inF. The
same sentence holds for an operator. The token inequality (2.11) has no classical disjunctive
counterpart, because a die is not a machine and two operations can hold two dies at the
same time. The candidate graph is the disjunctive graph at the moment a decision is taken,
restricted to the operations that could actually start, and extended by the operator and

The score can be wrong in ways the mask will not catch, because the mask only enforces

An implementation that follows this chapter, rather than the estimation program, has

cutter example. Fourth the weights are identified, and the log-weights are inspected. A

exists, the Kalman step is put in front of the wear recursion and the variance guard is set so
that a new indicator cannot veto on its first noisy hour. Skipping to a multi-layer network
at step two would make every later check harder and would not change the mask.
The two technologies, stripped of the surrounding discussion, are small enough to restate.
RCH-GAT builds, at each free resource, the feasible bundles of Equation (2.1), scores them
by the preference-weighted attention of Equation (2.30), and starts the minimiser. The
graph has four node types because the bundle names an operation, a machine, an operator,
and a token. The weights in Equation (2.43) say which of those nodes the identification
actually used: load, skill, failure, setup, and energy, with colour contention nearly silent.

System Build and Estimation
The formulas of Chapter 2 do not, by themselves, say whether the two technologies move the
five targets. This chapter builds the smallest system that can be run, and then estimates it.
Section 3.1 describes the cell, the data, the programs, and the way RCH-GAT and SSDT-

36-minute makespan gap is a shift of the mean, not a separation that a single book would
reliably show. It is, however, in the direction the shop node was built for, and it appears
under shortage and, with a similar size, under rush, while it does not appear under nominal
tokens or under pure wear. The estimation therefore supports a limited claim. Resource
features are a small, correctly signed correction when lots are tight, and they are not the
source of the large gains in makespan, energy, and failures. Those gains survive the ablation.
A factory that already has ample dies and thread, and that is trying to justify the shop
node alone, will not see them in a one-day book of this size. A factory whose khaki lot is
a single spool will see a modest tightening of the day, on top of the much larger tightening
that comes from not running the cutter into a repair.

3.2.6 A static evolutionary reference
On four nominal books the small NSGA-II, without maintenance, produced nondominated
sets of size14,16,7, and18. The best makespan in those four sets was1002,1274,1081,
and1219minutes. The best tardiness was649,131,571, and326minutes. The best energy
was204,263,237, and238 kWh. These are four separate single-book searches. A point
that holds the best makespan of a front is not the point that holds the best tardiness of
that front.
The reference is useful as a ceiling check and as a warning against a sloppy comparison.
On one book, a population that can see every order at time zero can reach a makespan near
1000minutes without any maintenance, which is in the neighbourhood of the full system’s
twenty-book mean of1052. The energy attached to the evolutionary fronts does not enter
the neighbourhood of168 kWh, and the search has no failure action to bring it there. Giving
the evolutionary method the maintenance map and a long enough population would be a
different experiment. It was not run. The online system does not need that population at
dispatch time, which is the operational distinction drawn in Section 1.1 and left intact by
these four fronts.

The four scenarios support a single decomposition.

The classical rules are not close to each other, and the estimation does not replace them
with a vague “heuristic baseline.” Earliest due date is the tardiness rule. Shortest processing
time is the energy rule among the four, because it happens to favour shorter occupations
on lighter machines more often than earliest due date does. First-in-first-out is the rule
that shows the cost of having no score at all. Apparent tardiness cost does not dominate
earliest due date once health-scaled processing times and colour setups are in the cell.
The graph score without maintenance, RCH, is responsible for a first cut of roughly250
to350minutes of makespan and roughly50to100 kWh, depending on the scenario, together
with a partial reduction in expected failures. It does this while remaining competitive with
earliest due date on nominal tardiness and clearly better than earliest due date on rush
and wear tardiness. That is the estimate of resource-coupled attention on this cell. The
resource half of the name is the smaller half. The attention is mostly using health, energy,
slack, and processing time. Contention features add a modest, correctly signed shift when
lots are short.
The twin is responsible for the second cut: failures from about ten, or about thirty in the
wear scenario, down to about one, and a further several hundred minutes of makespan. The
mechanism matches Table 2.2 extended over the day. Maintenance is a local cost. Avoided

3.2.9 What the weights say about the tables
Equation (2.43) and Table 2.1 were identified before any estimation book was generated.
They predict the tables more specifically than the architecture diagram does.
The largest weight is load deviation. Every scenario shows a lower imbalance for RCH
than for the classical rules, and the full system lowers it further because renewed machines
can take work that a worn machine would otherwise hoard and then lose to a repair. The
second-largest relevant weights, skill gap and failure probability, are why a machine-only
reading of the graph is the wrong reading. Skill never enters the classical rules except as a
longer processing time after a weak pairing has already been chosen. Failure never enters
them at all.
The smallest weight is colour contention. The shortage scenario was built to make that
weight visible, and the visibility is modest. This is consistent rather than contradictory.
A weight of0.133cannot move a day by the same amount as a weight of5.660or as a
sixteen-minute renewal that deletes a forty-eight-minute expected repair. Die contention,
at0.917, is large enough to matter when both cutters want a die, and the nominal cell
has two dies for two cutters, so the feature is often slack. A cell with one die would be a
different estimate.

3.2.10 What a factory would instrument first
The estimate implies an order of instrumentation, and the order is not the order in which
the technologies were named.
The first instrument is a health indicator on the automatic cutter, the embroidery head,
and the more worn lockstitch, mapped onto the unit interval that Equation (2.12) expects.
Those three machines dominate power or dominate the route. A health number that cannot
veto a cycle is a dashboard. The veto is Algorithm 3, and it needs a maintenance action the
operator is allowed to start without a separate work order. Sixteen minutes is the action
this cell used. A plant whose knife change takes forty minutes should rerun the tolerance
sweep before copyingε= 0.10.
The second instrument is the operator skill table and a live free-time for each person.
The skill weight is large, and the mask is what keeps Sew-3 off the bartack. A scheduler that
knows machines and not people will reproduce the hidden wait described in Section 2.1.
The third instrument is the die pool and the thread lots. They are cheap to count and
they are already in the graph. On a one-day book of this size they are not where the hours
are. They become first-priority only if a colour is routinely single-sourced and the due
dates on that colour are the ones the planner is missing. The shortage table is the test:
if a factory’s own version of that table shows a large gap between the full score and the
no-resource score, the lots are the constraint. If the gap looks like the thirty-minute shift
reported here, the lots are not the constraint.
The network of Equations (2.31) and (2.32) is not on this list. Nothing in the twenty-
book cell is large enough to need a multi-layer embedding. The list is health with authority
to maintain, then people, then tokens, and a deeper network only when the identification of
twenty coefficients stops being enough. That is a smaller system than a generic Industry 4.0

3.2.11 A note on the clock
All times in the tables are minutes from the release of the first order. A nominal makespan
of1052minutes is about17.5hours, longer than a single staffed shift. The cell as param-
eterised is a congested day-and-evening load: one embroidery head, one hardware bench,
and a worn fast cutter. The absolute clock is a property of those capacities and of the batch
sizes, not a claim that a school-bag plant cannot finish sixteen orders in a shift. A plant
with two embroidery heads would show a shorter clock and, if the cutter were healthy, a
smaller maintenance effect. The ratios are what the estimate is for. The full system is
about thirty percent shorter than first-in-first-out on the nominal book (1521to1052) and
about forty-five percent shorter on the wear book (2098to1122). Those ratios are the
quantities a different capacity would be expected to preserve in direction, not in minutes.
Energy should be read the same way. One hundred and sixty-eight kilowatt-hours for the
nominal full system is the cutter, the embroidery head, and the sewing machines over that
long clock, including setups and the few maintenances. It is not a utility bill for a named
factory. It is the integral of the nameplate powers in Table 3.1 under the wear surcharge.
Moving work off Cutter-A is the main reason the integral falls. A plant whose automatic
cutter is efficient when worn would see a smaller energy gap and would still see the failure
gap, because the hazard does not depend on the nameplate.

3.2.13 Limits of the estimate
The cell is a simulator with a known wear recursion. A physical embroidery head has a
health indicator that must be built from thread-break counts, temperature, and current,
with the measurement path reviewed by Lei and co-authors [62], and it has a Kalman
residual that this estimate sets to zero. If that residual is large, the veto will chatter, and
the sixteen-minute maintenance will be applied to noise. The formula for dealing with the
residual is Equation (2.54). It was not exercised.
The order books contain sixteen or twenty orders and nine machines. Graph policies are
attractive in the literature because they transfer to larger books [31, 35]. This estimate
does not test that transfer. A coefficient vector identified on sixteen-order books is not, on
the evidence here, a policy for a plant with two hundred machines. The multi-layer model
of Equations (2.31) and (2.32) is the form proposed for that plant, and it is untested in
these tables.
Expected failures are not random failures. Inserting the mean repair delay makes meth-
ods comparable and removes the variance of a single Bernoulli draw. It also makes the
timeline optimistic about clustering. Two failures that the expectation treats as indepen-
dent half-repairs can, in a real morning, land on the same operator and block the bartack
for a full repair plus the queue behind it. A stochastic replication would widen the spreads.
It would not, unless the intensity model is badly wrong, reverse a gap between one expected
failure and thirty.
Tardiness is a noisy sum. The nominal comparison with earliest due date should be
read as “no harm, within a large spread,” not as a precise percentage. The wear and rush
comparisons are the ones in which tardiness actually moves.
Resource coupling is real in the model and small in the estimate. That is the right
outcome to publish. Inflating it to match the size of the maintenance effect would mislead
a factory into instrumenting thread lots before instrumenting cutter wear. The instrumen-
tation order implied by these tables is the other way around: health and the authority to
act on health first, token contention second, and a deeper network only when the cell is
larger than the one these coefficients were fit to.
The evolutionary reference is too small to be a competitor. It is a sanity check. A
serious NSGA-III run of the kind Gong and co-authors used for five objectives [22], with
maintenance included in the chromosome, remains an open comparison and would be the
