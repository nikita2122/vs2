A Vision-Guided Feed-Speed Controller for a Wooden Seed Sowing
Machine

Abstract
Hardwood seeds such as acorns vary in diameter and lock against each other in a hopper throat.
A meter that only tracks shaft speed will keep turning at the kinematic command while the
throat is bridged, and the spacing error is then already committed. This short paper specifies
a vision-guided feed-speed controller for that failure. A camera over the throat publishes
occupancy, equivalent diameter, and a bridge index. The motor loop, on a separate computer,
multiplies the kinematic shaft command by a scale that falls when the bridge index crosses a
threshold, and a fuzzy proportional–integral–derivative law tracks the scaled command. The
scale cut is capped, so one bad frame cannot stall the wheel. A numerical estimate of the same
event model used in the parent thesis, not a field trial, shows the expected pattern: on red oak
the multiple index falls from 22.5% with speed tracking alone to 12.5% with the constraint,
while planting efficiency stays near 61% because some spacings stretch. On bur oak and under
a 16 Hz frame disturbance, slowing the feed without changing vibration does not repair the
miss index. The controller is a quality intervention on doubles, with a rate side effect that
must be reported separately.

Introduction

Direct seeding of oak can cost about a third of planting nursery seedlings, so the placement of
each wooden seed is worth controlling [1]. The seed is a poor match to a cereal meter. Pin oak,
red oak, and bur oak differ by more than the adjustment range of one cell, and two acorns readily
form an arch. A ground wheel copies slip into every spacing. An electronic drive removes that

is allowed to slow down. Slowing is a decision, not a tracking error.

Inner loop

The design model of the loaded wheel motor is 0.18ω̇ + ω = u. The sample is 10 ms. The discrete
law is a fuzzy proportional–integral–derivative controller with nominal gains Kp = 2.4, Ki = 6.0,
and Kd = 0.08, plus bounded increments from a five-label Mamdani table on the scaled error and
its difference [7, 8, 9]. Integral action freezes when the error exceeds 0.8 rad/s or the command
is on its rail, which is the integral separation used by He and colleagues to limit overshoot [2].

The derivative path is filtered at 40 Hz. Frozen gains stay positive inside the increment box, so
each frozen loop is an ordinary stable speed servo. That is not a Lyapunov certificate for the
time-varying fuzzy system. The schedule moves with the tracking error, and the error of a 0.18 s
plant does not cross the linguistic range in one sample unless the setpoint jumps. Jumps are
rate-limited.
Shaft speed is a windowed difference over eight samples, 80 ms, so a 4096-count encoder does
not dominate the fuzzy input. A cell at 22 r/min arrives every 0.228 s. Vision samples the throat
about four times per cell, which is the point of looking at the throat rather than at the tube: the
constraint can act on the cell that is about to be filled. Four frames at a 30 ms abort budget still
fit inside that cell period. They stop fitting near 2.1 m/s, which is outside the specified speed and
is the same class of limit Borja observed.

On mild red oak the multiple index falls by about ten points and the miss index rises by about
two. Efficiency does not move. The constraint removes doubles and pays with longer gaps. On
pin oak, where the cell is large for the seed, multiples fall from 34.3% to 24.3% and efficiency
rises only two points. On bur oak in slope and a 12 Hz disturbance, efficiency falls from 11.9%
to 9.8% and the miss index rises. Under a 16 Hz disturbance on red oak, doubles fall and misses
rise, and efficiency stays near 22%. Slowing a meter that is missing because of vibration creates
longer gaps. The feed controller is not a substitute for a vibrator or a depth loop.
These figures are solutions of a declared model anchored to published qualitative facts: slip
hurts a ground drive, vision can cut misses when the computer keeps up, and large hardwood
seeds defeat a fixed cell [11, 3, 5]. They are not belt measurements. A sticky-belt comparison of
the PID and vision rows is the acceptance test. The ranking to look for is a lower multiple index
on red oak and pin oak, and no requirement that vision alone raise efficiency when the frame is
shaking above about 10 Hz.

Spacing side effect and faults

A constant scale σ stretches the geometric gap from Xt to Xt /σ. At the cap, σ = 0.78, every gap
grows by a factor of about 1.28 if the cells that do fill are singles. Part of that stretch replaces a
double. Part of it becomes a miss if the next cell was going to be empty anyway. The efficiency
column in Table 1 is that second part. A score that looked only at doubles would drive σ to the
floor. The cap, and the separate efficiency number, are what stop that.
The feed computer has four local faults. A late vision message holds B. After 150 ms, B decays
and the constraint opens while tracking continues. Two missed shaft-encoder periods remove
motor current. A stale ground speed is held for one second and then ramped to zero, so a dead
radar does not leave the wheel planting forever. A radar stuck at a high value is not detected by
these rules. It looks like a healthy, fast tractor. That case needs a second speed source before it
can be tripped. None of the four faults asks the factor coordinator for help. The camera can fail
and the shaft loop remains a speed servo at σ = 1.

6

Conclusion

The feed-speed controller is a cascade with one extra scalar. Equation (1) sets the speed of a
clear throat. Equation (3) reduces that speed when Equation (2) says the throat is bridging, and
the reduction is capped. The motor loop does not decode images. The estimate says the scalar
does what it was built to do: it cuts doubles on seeds that nearly fit the cell, and it does not, by
itself, recover bur oak or a harshly vibrating frame. Planting efficiency must be reported beside
the quality-of-feed index, because a controller scored only on doubles will always prefer the lowest
scale.
