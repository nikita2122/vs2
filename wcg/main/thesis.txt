Three chapters on sensing, control, and numerical estimation

This manuscript proposes two control innovations for a planter that meters irregular
wooden seeds, principally hardwood seeds such as acorns. Chapter 3 reports a
numerical estimation of the models derived in Chapter 2. It is not a field trial and
it does not claim measured plantation results.

Abstract
Direct seeding of hardwoods is attractive because a machine can place wooden seeds,
especially acorns, at a fraction of the cost of planting nursery seedlings. The same seeds
defeat the assumptions built into cereal planters. An acorn is heavy, elongated, and
variable in diameter. A cell that suits red oak doubles on pin oak and misses on bur oak
or black walnut. A ground wheel slips on a slope, a seed box vibrates, and a vacuum level
copied from a maize meter either does nothing for a large nut or pulls two small seeds
into one cell. Previous electronic meters close a speed loop. Previous laboratory studies
optimize vacuum, hole size, or vibration one factor at a time, usually offline. Neither line
of work coordinates the input speed of the seeds with the factors of the machine while the
lot of seeds is changing.
This manuscript studies that gap as a computer-science problem in system design. Two
innovations are proposed and then estimated together.
The first innovation is a vision-guided feed-speed controller. A camera over the throat
of the hopper measures occupancy, equivalent diameter, and a bridge index. An outer
kinematic law converts ground speed and the target spacing into a seed rate. An inner fuzzy
proportional–integral–derivative loop drives the cell-wheel motor. A constraint layer slows
the wheel when the bridge index crosses a threshold, which is the action that fixed-gain
speed control does not take. The vision computer is separate from the motor computer,
because earlier vision meters lost the spacing target when image processing and motor
control shared one slow microcontroller.
The second innovation is a multi-factor model-predictive coordinator. At a slower period it chooses vibrator frequency, assist vacuum, and opener depth. Its prediction is a
logistic map from seed size, vibration, slip, and depth error onto miss probability, double
probability, and spacing scatter. The map is minimized on a small grid subject to actuator
limits and a penalty on shaker and vacuum effort. The factor loop is deliberately blind

ii

to the vision constraint, so it cannot hide a bad vibration setting behind a slower feed. A
passive isolator, enabled with this loop, attenuates terrain vibration in the model.
The two loops are stacked. The factor coordinator sets the mechanical regime. The
vision loop trims the input speed inside that regime. Chapter 3 integrates the discrete
models over 4,000 metering events per case, with a fixed random seed, for pin oak, red oak,
bur oak, and a mixed lot, at two ground speeds, with slope and with harsh vibration. On a
mild red-oak run the quality-of-feed index moves from 58.6% under ground drive to 88.2%
with both innovations, and the planting-efficiency index moves from 52.4% to 87.0%. On
bur oak with slope and a 12 Hz terrain disturbance, quality of feed moves from 20.7%
to 75.4%. On the disturbed mixed lot the efficiency index rises by about 51 percentage
points across repeated draws. The shaker uses more energy when it is actually helping.
Under a 16 Hz disturbance the optimizer drops the shaker command from 9 Hz to 3 Hz
and relies on isolation, which is a result of the cost, not a hand-written rule. These figures
are solutions of the stated models. They rank the designs. They are not a substitute for
a bench or field test.

Chapter 1
Research on Previous Work
A wooden seed, in the sense used throughout this manuscript, is a hardwood tree seed
whose mass and shape are those of a nut rather than those of a cereal grain. The working
examples are pin oak, red oak, and bur oak acorns, together with the larger class that
includes seeds for which a maize cell is simply the wrong tool. Black walnut sits in that
larger class. The seeds are wooden in the ordinary mechanical sense: the shell is hard, the
diameter varies inside a lot, the shape is elongated, and two seeds readily lock against each
other in a throat. A sowing machine for these seeds has to accept a stream of irregular
objects, release them one at a time, and put each one at a commanded spacing and a
commanded depth while the vehicle moves.
The silvicultural reason for caring about the machine is direct seeding. Planting a nursery seedling is reliable and expensive. Placing the seed itself can cost about a third as
much, which is why direct seeding of oak remains attractive for restoration even though
rodents, drought, and handling still make the biological result less certain than transplanting [1, 2, 3, 4]. The machine does not solve depredation. It does decide whether the seed
arrives at the right place, at the right depth, and without a double or a gap. Those are
placement questions. They are also control questions, because the input speed of the seeds
and the factors of the machine move together as soon as the vehicle speed, the slope, or
the seed lot changes.
Cereal planters already have a large technical literature. Electric drives replace the
ground wheel [5, 6, 7]. Vacuum plates are optimized for hole diameter, pressure, and
peripheral speed [8, 9, 10, 11]. Vision has been put inside a corn meter [12]. Vibration has
been measured on rapeseed drills and connected to the seed-filling angle [13, 14]. A review

writes the problem those designs are required to solve. Section 1.1 reviews feed-speed
control. Section 1.2 reviews factor optimization. Section 1.3 states the indices, the coupled
decision, and the requirements. The innovations themselves are designed in Chapter 2 and
estimated in Chapter 3.

1.1.2

Ground-wheel drive and slip

The classical transmission is a ground wheel, a chain, and a gearbox. The cell wheel is
geared so that, with no slip and a nominal fill, Equation (1.3) holds. The construction is
simple and it needs no electricity. It fails in a specific way. If the drive wheel turns at
(1−s)v rather than at v, every spacing stretches by 1/(1−s). On a slope or on soft ground
the slip s is not a small constant. It varies inside a bout. The operator cannot see it in
the spacing until plants emerge, and wooden seeds are often not assessed by emergence
for a year.
Slip is the reason electronic drives were introduced on row-crop planters. Hydraulic
drives first allowed the rate to be set from the cab and allowed a prescription map. Electric
drives then removed the shaft and the chain and put a motor on each meter, which adds
individual-row shutoff, curve compensation, and a faster change of population [5]. The
same reasoning applies to a single-row hardwood planter, with a harsher slip environment:
mine spoil, scarified forest soil, and trails are not prepared seedbeds. Wells and colleagues
already ran their hardwood meter at modest speeds, 2.4 to 4.0 km/h, and still had to
respect slope [16]. A ground wheel on that terrain is a poor speed sensor.
Zhao and colleagues made the same diagnosis for maize and replaced the wheel with an
electronic meter whose shaft speed is computed from the set spacing and the measured
forward speed [7]. Their model is the discrete cousin of Equation (1.3). They report that a
fixed-gain proportional–integral–derivative controller was not enough once forward speed
kept changing, which is why they scheduled the gains. The hardware lesson carries over.
The gain-scheduling lesson only carries over if the disturbance is speed. For wooden seeds
the disturbance is also the shape of the next seed.

1.1.3

Proportional–integral–derivative control of the meter shaft

He and colleagues built a proportional–integral–derivative controller for a maize meter and
added integral separation so that a large speed step would not wind the integrator up [6].
The plant is the motor and the plate. The setpoint is a plate speed in revolutions per
minute. With gains Kp = 16, Ki = 0.05, and Kd = 36 they recorded, for a step from rest
to 24 r/min, a rise of about 0.4 s, an overshoot of 1.56 percent, and a steady error of 0.75
percent. On the bench the singulation index reached 98.4 percent at a planting speed of
12 km/h, with multiples and misses not above 1 percent. Those numbers are for graded
maize and a plate whose cells match the seed. They show that a well-tuned inner loop can
make ω follow a command. They do not show that the command is the right one when η
jumps because two acorns have locked in the throat.
The classical tuning route is still the one in Ziegler and Nichols, or a relay experiment,
followed by detuning for overshoot [19, 20, 21]. For a first-order motor model

Dambroz and colleagues showed that a low-cost electric drive can also select which seed

is a mode switch and a rate command. It is relevant here because a wooden-seed hopper
may hold more than one species, as forestry spot sowers already do [18]. This manuscript
does not design multi-species switching. It does assume that the controller must survive
a change of diameter statistics without a new mechanical plate.

1.1.4

Machine vision on the meter

Speed feedback answers the question “is the shaft turning at ω ⋆ ?”. It does not answer “is
there a seed on the next cell?”. Optoelectronic sensors in the seed tube answer a later
question: a seed has already been released, or it has not [24, 25, 26]. By the time the
tube sensor fires, the empty cell has passed the release point. The spacing error is already
committed. That is why a camera looking at the plate, rather than at the tube, is the
more interesting sensor for feed-speed control.
Borja and colleagues mounted a camera on a pneumatic corn meter and classified each

1.1.5

Travel speed as a disturbance, not only as a setpoint

Equation (1.3) treats v as a known input. Liu and colleagues showed that travel speed
also changes the uniformity that a meter can physically deliver [32]. Finger-pickup, scoopwheel, air-pressure, and air-blowing meters for maize were all sensitive to speed, on the
bench and in the field. The mechanism is not the control law. At higher peripheral speed
the seed has less time to settle in the cell, the release trajectory changes, and bounce in the
tube grows. Onal, Degirmencioglu, and Yazgi found an upper seed-release frequency near
16 seeds per second for cotton and maize on their vacuum unit, and an upper peripheral
speed near 0.34 m/s, beyond which the qualified index fell [11]. A hardwood planter will
not run at maize field speeds. Wells operated near 0.7 to 1.1 m/s. Even in that band,
speed remains a disturbance of η, so a controller that raises ω in proportion to v can push
the cell past the speed at which a heavy seed will still seat.
This is the first place where a pure tracking loop is the wrong optimality condition.
Tracking ω ⋆ minimises (ω − ω ⋆ )2 . Planting efficiency depends on misses and doubles. If

Chapter 1. Research on Previous Work

8

raising ω toward ω ⋆ increases misses because seeds are bridging, the tracker is faithfully doing harm. The vision constraint in Chapter 2 exists to break that faithfulness on purpose,
for a short time, and then to recover the rate when the bridge index falls.

1.1.6

Where feed-speed research stops for wooden seeds

The reviewed controllers were built for seeds that are small relative to their variation, or
that have been graded and coated until they behave like the spherical pellets Yazgi and
Degirmencioglu used as a controlled material [33]. A wooden seed violates that preparation. Diameter coefficients of variation of 0.12 to 0.20 inside one species, and above 0.30
in a mixed lot, are normal rather than defective. The cell cannot be resized on the move.
What can be resized, in effect, is the time allowed for a seed to fall into the cell, and that
time is the feed speed.
Published vision adjusts the plate after an empty hole is seen, or grades the result offline.
Published fuzzy control adjusts gains so that shaft speed tracks a kinematic command
derived from forward speed. Neither computes a bridge index from the shape statistics
of the seeds in the throat, and neither treats a reduction of feed speed as a constrained
decision rather than as a tracking error. That is the opening for the first innovation. It is
an opening in control architecture and in real-time system structure: which computer sees
the image, which computer turns the motor, and which quantity is allowed to saturate.

1.2

Research on multi-factor optimization

1.2.1

The factors that actually move the fill ratio

Feed speed is one input. The machine has others, and the hardwood problem is that they
interact. The factors considered in this manuscript are the following.
1. Vibrator frequency at the seed box. Low-frequency agitation can unlock a bridge
of large seeds. High-frequency agitation fluidises a mass of small seeds and, past a
threshold, destroys the fill.
2. Assist vacuum at the cell. Vacuum is the main factor in cereal precision meters. For
a heavy nut it is a weak force compared with weight, and for a small seed in a large
cell it is a way to pick up two seeds.

Chapter 1. Research on Previous Work

9

3. Opener depth. Depth does not change the meter directly. It changes bounce and
the vertical error, and it is the factor a forester means by “sown properly” [1].
4. Ground speed, already discussed, which sets the time budget of every other factor.
5. Terrain vibration and slope, which are disturbances rather than commands, but
which enter the same equations as the commands.
A design that optimizes one of these while holding the others at a convenient constant
will look good in that slice and fail in the slice beside it. The literature is rich in such
slices. It is poor in online coordination.

1.2.2

Vacuum, hole diameter, and peripheral speed

Karayel, Barut, and Ozmerzi modelled the vacuum pressure required to hold a seed on a
plate as a function of seed mass, hole area, and the speed of the plate [9]. The force balance
is the one every pneumatic meter uses: pressure difference times hole area must exceed
weight, centrifugal force, and a margin for vibration. Singh, Singh, and Saraswat optimized
pressure, hole size, and speed for cotton and showed that the optimum is a point in a threedimensional factor space, not a universal pressure [10]. Yazgi and Degirmencioglu made
the experimental design explicit. A central composite response surface over vacuum, hole
diameter, and peripheral speed, fitted to the multiple index, the miss index, and the
quality-of-feed index, gave an optimum near 5.5 kPa and a 3 mm hole for cotton, and no
optimum inside the tested range for plate speed [8]. The statistical method is appropriate
for a fixed seed lot in a laboratory. It produces a setpoint, not a controller. When the next
lot has a different diameter, the surface is a different surface, and the machine is already
in the field.
The aerodynamic calculation of Onal and colleagues makes the same point from the
other direction. A vacuum band that is wide relative to the seed creates a region in which
two seeds can seal, so adding holes to a plate in order to lower peripheral speed can raise
the multiple index [11]. For maize they recommended the familiar 26-hole plate. For
cotton they recommended fewer holes than the 72-hole plate supplied by default. The
recommendation is a mechanical redesign. A wooden-seed machine that must accept pin
oak and bur oak in the same season cannot carry a family of plates and still claim to be
automatic. The online substitute is to change vacuum and feed speed, which are actuators,
and to refuse a vacuum that the current diameter statistics do not support. That refusal

Chapter 1. Research on Previous Work

10

is exactly what the factor coordinator in Chapter 2 does when it drives assist vacuum to
zero for the lots studied here.

1.2.3

Vibration and the seed-filling angle

Wu and colleagues measured the vibration of a centralized rapeseed meter and reconstructed the seed motion with a discrete-element model [13, 34]. Field frequencies occupied
0 to 180 Hz. In the simulation, vibration did not merely shake seeds in place. It raised the
seed-filling angle from a band of 56 to 59 degrees with no vibration to a band of 70 to 91
degrees with vibration. The angle is the geometric expression of how far around the filling
zone the seeds are still in contact with the meter. A larger angle means a disturbed free
surface. Bench results followed the geometry: with vibration at 10 to 40 Hz the targetseed rate lay between 88.7 and 99.4 percent, against 100 percent with the device at rest.
Longitudinal offset of the seed layer mattered more than lateral offset. Holding that offset
inside 0 to 20 mm kept the target rate above 91 percent. The practical conclusion they
draw is to manage seed-layer height so that vibration has less to act on.
Zhang and colleagues put a hole-wheel rapeseed meter on a rig whose eccentric mass
produced controlled vibration, and compared amplitude with frequency [14]. Amplitude,
inside a few millimetres, did little. Frequency did a great deal. From 0 to 10 Hz the
discharge stayed near 7.6 to 8.2 g/min and the coefficient of variation of uniformity stayed
in a stable band. From 10 to 24 Hz the discharge fell nearly linearly with frequency
(R2 = 0.92) and the coefficient of variation rose nearly linearly (R2 = 0.88). The 10 Hz
threshold is the empirical anchor used later in the miss model. It was measured on
rapeseed, not on acorns. Using it for wooden seeds is a modelling choice, stated as such
in Chapter 2, not a claim that an acorn mass has the same resonance. The qualitative
content that transfers is the existence of a frequency above which more agitation is not
more singulation.
Li and colleagues, working on irregular vegetable seeds rather than on nuts, found the
opposite sign at a much higher frequency [35]. An air-suction drum, with suction near
16 kPa, clearing air near 3.4 kPa, and a cassette vibrated at 50 Hz, reached a qualified
rate of 96.9 percent, with 1.4 percent reseeding and 1.7 percent empty holes. Suction
dominated, then clearing air, then vibration. The seeds were small and irregular, and the
vibration was a high-frequency assist to stop them sticking, not a low-frequency unlock
of a bridge of heavy objects. Copying 50 Hz and 16 kPa onto an acorn meter would be

Chapter 1. Research on Previous Work

11

a category error. The factor coordinator must be allowed to choose a low frequency for
a heavy seed and to choose almost no vacuum when the cell is larger than the seed. A
response surface fitted to pepper seeds will not make that choice.
Inclination is a related factor. Lei and colleagues showed that the angle of a centralized
rapeseed meter changes the seed motion enough to move the performance indices, using
the same discrete-element tools [36, 37, 38]. A planter on a 10 to 20 degree trail is an
inclined meter whether or not the designer intended one. Wells had already included slope
in the hardwood tests: pin oak still metered at 97 percent on a 50 percent grade in the
laboratory at 3.2 km/h, and red oak at 97 percent on that grade at 4.0 km/h, while the
field result on a mild grade was about 95 percent of the target density at 2.4 km/h [16].
The laboratory number and the field number are both high, and both are for the species
the cell could accept. The same paper is the source for the failure mode this manuscript
refuses to ignore. Bur oak and black walnut did not meter satisfactorily. A larger seed
does not become a smaller seed because the vacuum is raised.

1.2.4

Mechanical hardwood meters

The pneumatic hardwood meter of Wells is the closest field ancestor. A commercial no-till
unit was rebuilt with a pneumatic singulator and, after penetration failed, with a parabolic
ripper. The metering result is species-dependent in exactly the way a fixed cell predicts.
Seeds near the design size pass. Seeds far above it do not. There is no vision and no online
change of speed beyond the ground-driven ratio.
The Tourney nursery seeder is the closest mechanical ancestor for singulation by geometry rather than by air [17]. A drum with an internal pickup carries one acorn out of the
mass and releases it at a fixed clock position. Five rows, 26 holes per revolution, a drum
speed of 27 r/min at 1 mile per hour, and a target of eight acorns per foot, give a concrete rate calculation of the same family as Equation (1.3). The inventors abandoned an
adjustable cone gearbox for a more direct drive after the gearbox proved to be the wrong
place to put the adjustment. That history supports a direct electric drive. It also shows
the limit of a purely mechanical pickup: the hole and the clock angle are manufactured,
and a mixed lot returns the designer to the original bridging problem they had already
seen when several acorns arrived at once.
Maleki and colleagues evaluated a multi-flight auger as a mass-flow meter for a grain
drill [39]. An auger is the wrong singulator for a seed that must be placed at 0.25 m,

Chapter 1. Research on Previous Work

12

because it is designed to produce a continuous ribbon. It remains relevant as a warning.
Many “seeders” in forestry are mass-flow devices or spot broadcasters [18]. A precision
index is then the wrong score, and a precision controller is the wrong investment. This
manuscript is about the precision case: one wooden seed, one station, a measured spacing.

1.2.5

Why offline optimization is not a controller

Response-surface and discrete-element studies share a workflow. The factors are chosen,
the machine or the particle model is run, a polynomial or a simulation output is fitted,
and an optimum is published. The optimum is a point (P ⋆ , d⋆hole , ω ⋆ ) or a recommended
band of frequencies. Nothing in that workflow runs while the hopper is emptying and the
slope is changing.
Model-predictive control was built for exactly the online version of this workflow. At
each sample one solves a finite-horizon problem, applies the first move, and solves again
when the state has moved [40, 41, 42, 43, 44]. Industrial surveys describe the method
as standard on slow multivariable plants where constraints are the real specification [45].
The stability theory for the constrained case is mature: a terminal cost and a terminal
set, together with a feasible optimisation, give a decreasing cost and an invariant set [46].
Explicit solutions exist when the problem is a small quadratic program, which removes
the need to run an iterative solver on the machine [47, 48]. None of this has been the
controller of a hardwood meter. Cereal rate loops are single-input speed loops. Cereal
factor studies are offline. The second innovation is the transfer of a constrained, recedinghorizon coordinator onto the factor vector of a wooden-seed machine, with a prediction
model small enough to sit beside the speed loop rather than replace it.
Identification of that prediction model is a separate and older subject [49, 50, 51]. A
recursive least-squares update can track a slow change in the gain from frequency to fill as
the lot changes. This manuscript uses a fixed logistic map whose coefficients are published
in Chapter 2, and it says so. The map is structured so that a later project can replace the
coefficients with estimates from a bench without changing the coordinator. Pretending
that the coefficients are already hardwood measurements would make the estimation in
Chapter 3 look like a field result. It is not.

The requirements below are written so that a later bench test can accept or reject the

2. Slip. The speed used in Equation (1.3) shall not be taken from a driven ground

3. Bridge constraint. When the bridge index exceeds its threshold, the feed scale

stall the wheel. The limit used in the model is a scale reduction of at most 0.22.
4. Latency. Image features shall be computed on a processor that does not share its
deadline with the motor current loop. The motor period is 10 ms. The feature
period is 50 ms. A missed feature frame shall hold the last bridge index for at most
three periods and then decay the constraint.
5. Factors. Vibration frequency, assist vacuum, and opener depth shall be commands,
not factory constants. Frequency shall be selectable at least over 0 to 14 Hz. Vacuum
shall be selectable at least over 0 to 8 kPa and shall be allowed to sit at zero.
6. Large seed. On a lot whose mean diameter exceeds the cell, the design shall be
scored by the change in Imiss relative to ground drive, not by a maize acceptance
threshold.
7. Small seed. On a lot whose mean diameter is well below the cell, the design shall
be scored by the change in Imult .
8. Harsh vibration. When the terrain disturbance lies above the 10 Hz anchor, the
factor command shall not add a second high frequency on top of it if the predicted
miss probability increases.
9. Fallback. Loss of vision shall leave a stable speed loop. Loss of the factor solver
shall leave the last feasible factor command.
10. Effort. The cost shall include a positive price on vibrator frequency and on vacuum,
so that a flat region of the quality surface does not default to maximum actuation.
11. Indices. Reports shall include Qf , Imult , Imiss , Cv , and ηE . A mean spacing alone
shall not be used as a pass criterion [52].

1.3.4

Research questions

The problem set reduces to three questions, which the remaining chapters answer in design
and then in numbers.
1. Can a feed-speed loop that is allowed to slow down when a vision bridge index is
high reduce doubles on small wooden seeds and gaps on large ones, beyond what
shaft-speed tracking already achieves by rejecting slip?
2. Can a slow constrained optimiser, choosing vibration, vacuum, and depth from a
size-aware prediction, improve the same indices in regimes where speed tracking has
little to offer, in particular under terrain vibration and on seeds larger than the cell?
3. When the two loops run together, do they interfere? In particular, does the vision
loop’s willingness to stretch the spacing cancel the factor loop’s gain in planting
efficiency, or do the loops improve different terms of the cost?
The first question is about the first innovation. The second is about the second. The
third is about the architecture that stacks them. A positive answer to the first two and a
qualified answer to the third are both useful. If the stack interferes, the design should say
so, because a deployer who turns both loops on by default would otherwise misread the
mild-case tables.

Scope

The machine in view is a single-row unit pulled or carried at about 0.7 to 1.2 m/s, with a
12-cell wheel, a 24 mm cell, a vibratory hopper, a small assist vacuum that is allowed to be
off, a depth-controlled opener, and a tube. Multi-row coordination, curve compensation,
prescription maps, and species switching are outside the design [5, 23, 58, 59]. Rodent
protection, seed treatment, and germination are outside the indices [1, 2]. Communication
with a tractor bus is mentioned in the build only as a possible later carrier for the set-point
Xt ; the control design does not depend on ISO 11783 [60].
The computer-science contribution is the split real-time system, the vision constraint
on an otherwise standard motor loop, and the constrained coordinator that treats machine factors as a short-horizon decision with an explicit cost. The mechanical layout in
Chapter 3 is the minimum hardware on which those two programs have a meaning. It is
not offered as a new singulator geometry. The drum pickup of Dinkel and Kangas and
the pneumatic cell of Wells remain the mechanical precedents [17, 16]. The claim is that
those mechanisms are being asked to run open-loop with respect to the two variables that
dominate their errors: how fast the wooden seeds are fed in, and how the box, the air,
and the opener are set while that happens.
Predictive control enters as a constrained finite-horizon decision, not as a neural policy
and not as a claim of global optimality over soil and weather [46, 45]. Fuzzy control enters
as a bounded scheduling of three gains on a motor that is already stable at the frozen gains
[61, 62, 63, 64]. Vision enters as a scalar constraint, not as a detector of tree species. Each
of those reductions is deliberate. Each keeps a failure of one component from becoming a
failure of the whole machine, which is the system requirement that the cereal vision meter
did not meet when the processor fell behind [12].

1.3.6

How a spacing is measured, and why the index needs a
procedure

The indices of Section 1.3 are only as good as the ruler that produces zi . Three procedures
appear in the work reviewed above, and they do not measure the same object.
The sticky-belt procedure lays a belt under the tube, moves the belt at the simulated
ground speed, and reads gaps between seeds that have stuck where they fell [31, 11, 26].
It removes soil bounce and opener depth. It keeps tube bounce and meter errors. It is the

Chapter 1. Research on Previous Work

20

right procedure for judging the feed-speed loop, because that loop’s product is a release
time. ISO 7256 is written in this spirit: a single-seed drill is tested for the regularity
of the metered stream, not for the forest that may or may not grow [53]. The Chinese
single-seed method GB/T 6973 organises the same three percentages, multiples, qualified,
and misses, about the theoretical spacing [54]. JB/T 10293 then turns those percentages
into a specification a manufacturer can claim against [22]. A hardwood machine should
be willing to be scored by the same arithmetic even if it is not willing to adopt the maize
pass marks. The arithmetic is what makes Table 3.3 comparable with a future belt test.
The field procedure measures plants, not seeds. Emergence, rodents, and depth then
enter zi [1, 52]. A miss in the field is no longer evidence that the cell was empty. Kachman
and Smith were explicit that a gap can be a failure to drop or a failure to emerge, and
that the indices do not separate them. This manuscript therefore refuses field plant counts
as the acceptance test of the controllers. They are the acceptance test of a plantation.
Mixing the two is how a good meter is blamed for a dry week, and how a bad meter is
excused by a kind season.
The optoelectronic procedure sits between the belt and the field. A gate in the tube
times each seed and converts time to distance by multiplying by v [24, 25]. The conversion
is exact only if v is the speed used by the kinematic law and the seed’s exit velocity along
the row is negligible. A heavy acorn leaves the cell with a tangential speed rω. At
r = 0.09 m and ω = 2.30 rad/s that tangential speed is 0.21 m/s, which is not negligible
beside a ground speed of 0.7 m/s. The horizontal component depends on the release
angle. A tube gate that assumes the seed’s row-speed equals v will assign the wrong
spacing by a few centimetres, which is enough to move a seed across the 0.5Xt boundary
when Xt = 0.25 m. The estimation avoids that bias by placing seeds from the event model
directly. A bench that uses a tube gate will need the release-angle correction derived in
Section 2.2.11, or it will disagree with the belt for a reason that is geometry rather than
control.
Panning and colleagues also remind the reader that a laboratory ranking and a field
ranking of planters need not match, even for sugarbeet, which is a far more uniform seed
than an acorn [31]. The estimation in this manuscript is a third thing again: a ranking
inside a declared model. It is closer to a belt test than to a field test, and it is not a
belt test. The procedure that would make it a belt test is the one named at the end of
Chapter 3.

Particle forces underneath the factor map

The logistic map is a summary. The physics it summarises is a contact problem. A
discrete-element model represents each seed as a body with a position and a spin, and
integrates the contact forces with its neighbours and with the wall [34]. The normal force
is a spring and a damper in the overlap. The tangential force is a spring limited by friction,
which is what allows an arch to stand. For two acorns the overlap spring is a crude model
of a wooden shell, but the structure of the argument does not depend on the spring law.
An arch over the cell stands when the contact normals meet the wall at an angle steeper
than the friction cone. Vibration that reverses the contact force for part of each cycle
collapses the arch. Vibration that keeps the contacts sliding turns the hopper into a fluid
and the cell fills with whatever arrives, including two small seeds.
Wu and colleagues used that style of model to show a filling angle that opens under
vibration, and Hu and colleagues coupled it to an airflow solver for a pneumatic rapeseed
meter [13, 38, 37]. The angle is a geometric observable of the same arch. A larger angle
means contacts that have walked up the wall. The 10 Hz threshold of Zhang and colleagues
is, in this language, the frequency at which the arch no longer reforms between cycles for
a small seed [14]. A large wooden seed has a longer contact time and a higher mass, so the
frequency at which its arch collapses is lower. Equation (2.20) is that statement written as
a decreasing function of diameter. It is not a modal analysis of an acorn. It is the smallest
formula that gives the coordinator permission to treat pin oak and bur oak as different
vibratory problems, which the single-frequency vegetable-seed result does not [35].
Inclination enters the same contact balance. On a slope the gravity vector gains a
component along the throat, and the arch becomes asymmetric [36]. Wells could still
meter red oak on a steep laboratory grade because the pneumatic force dominated that
component for a seed that fitted the hole [16]. A seed that does not fit never receives
the pneumatic force, and the slope component only makes the miss more likely. The slip
term in Equation (2.25) is the kinematic half of slope. The contact half is left inside the
miss coefficient on δ+ , which is why the estimation does not add a third slope mechanism.
Adding one without a measurement would be a second unsupported constant.
This particle picture also explains a limit of vision. The camera sees the free surface and
the seeds in the window. It does not see the contact forces under that surface. The bridge
index is a proxy: high occupancy, low circularity, and oversize are the visible correlates of
an arch. They will be wrong when an arch stands below a surface that looks open, which

Chapter 1. Research on Previous Work

is a known hopper failure and a reason the constraint is capped. A capped constraint can
be late. An uncapped constraint can stop the machine on a misread surface. The cap at
22 percent is the design’s choice of which error is cheaper.

This chapter specifies the two innovations at the level of signals, equations, and timing.

2.1

Vision-guided feed-speed control

2.1.1

Role of the loop

The feed-speed controller is a cascade with a constraint. The outer input is ground speed
v and the spacing set-point Xt . The inner output is a motor voltage, or a speed-mode
torque command, that turns the cell wheel. Between them sits a scalar σ ∈ [0.72, 1.08]
that multiplies the kinematic speed. When the throat looks clear, σ stays near 1 and the
loop is an ordinary electronic meter [6, 7]. When the throat looks bridged, σ falls and the
wheel waits. The waiting is the innovation. A tracker without the constraint would treat
the same situation as a brief shortfall of fill and would not change its behaviour at all,
because the shaft would still be at the commanded speed.
The loop does not detect species and does not draw boxes around seeds for an operator.
It compresses each accepted frame into the features of Section 2.1.3. Those features are
the only vision data the motor computer is allowed to see.

Fault states inside the feed loop

Four fault states are defined so that the real-time program has somewhere to go.
1. Vision hold. A late datagram holds B. Tracking continues. A counter is incremented.
2. Vision decay. After 150 ms, B is halved each 50 ms until it lies below 0.42. The
constraint opens. The operator lamp for vision is lit. The motor loop is unchanged.
3. Encoder loss. If the shaft encoder misses two periods, the motor current is removed
and the wheel stops. Coasting a cell wheel with an unknown speed is how doubles
become a pile. This state is a stop, not a fallback to open voltage.
4. Speed-sensor loss. If v is stale for 200 ms, ω ⋆ is held at its last value for 1 s
and then ramped to zero. Holding forever would keep planting after the tractor has
stopped only if the failure mode is a stuck high reading, which is unsafe. Ramping
down is the chosen failure direction.
None of these states requires the factor coordinator. They are local to the feed computer.
The factor computer is informed by a single health bit so that it can freeze u when the
wheel is stopped, rather than integrating a model that assumes events are still occurring.

2.2

Multi-factor model-predictive optimization

2.2.1

Role of the loop

The factor coordinator owns vibration frequency f , assist vacuum P , and opener depth
h. It does not own the wheel speed. It runs at 5 Hz, which is once every 200 ms, and
on this machine that is once every few cell events at the speeds of interest. Its job is to
keep the triple (f, P, h) at a point where the fast loop’s constraint is rarely needed. A
coordinator that needs the constraint to look good has chosen a bad regime. For that
reason the prediction inside the coordinator is evaluated at σ = 1 and with the vision flag
clear. The vision loop may still act, and the estimation credits that action separately. The
coordinator is not allowed to assume that the action will happen.
This separation is also a software separation. The factor task can overrun a deadline
without stretching a motor period. On an overrun the previous feasible triple is held.

Relation to a quadratic program

A reader who expects the classical quadratic program can recover it as an approximation.
Let x stack the three probabilities and the normalised scatter at the mean diameter, and
let the grid be replaced by a box u ∈ U. Linearising Π about the previous command u−

quadratic if one ignores the logistic curvature, or if one keeps the curvature and accepts
a sequential quadratic program [65, 48]. Constraints are the box and the slew. That
program is the form surveyed by Qin and Badgwell and analysed by Mayne and colleagues
[45, 46]. It was not the form implemented for the estimation, because the logistic is cheap
to evaluate exactly and the input dimension is two once depth is removed. The grid avoids
a linearisation error near δ+ = 0, where the max(·, 0) terms are not differentiable and a
gradient method needs a subgradient convention.
An explicit multi-parametric solution in the sense of Bemporad and colleagues would
be attractive if the linear model were trusted, because the control law would become a
lookup of critical regions [47]. With a non-smooth logistic and an input grid of 55 points,
the lookup is the grid itself. Memory on the factor computer is not the binding constraint.
Deadline margin is. Fifty-five evaluations leave the processor idle for most of the 200 ms
period, which is the desired state of a safety-related task: busy for a short, bounded time,
then waiting.
Stability of a one-step constrained search on a static map is simpler than stability of
a dynamic horizon. The applied u is always one of the 55 feasible points, the slew limit
makes the applied sequence Lipschitz in time, and the plant-side actuators are themselves
stable servos. There is no hidden unstable mode in the coordinator. The quality of the
closed loop is entirely a question of whether J ranks the true probabilities in the right
order. That is a model-quality question, and Chapter 3 does not answer it experimentally.
It answers the narrower question of how the indices move when the plant is the same map
the coordinator optimises, with Monte Carlo variation of diameter, slip, and depth noise
that the coordinator does not see seed by seed. Even in that favourable situation the
coordinator is not given the per-seed diameter, only the lot statistics, so the comparison
is not a tautology of perfect preview.

Information the coordinator is denied

The following quantities are measured somewhere on the machine and are intentionally
not inputs to J.

vision alive, encoder alive, wheel stopped. Health bits change modes. They do not enter
J as if they were diameters.

2.2.12

Response times of the two computers

The periods in Table 2.2 are not a schedule until each task’s worst-case execution is set
against them. The feed computer has one control task. At a 2 ms budget inside 10 ms
its utilization is 0.20. A single task at that utilization cannot miss a deadline unless the
budget is a lie. The stall rule, two missed periods, is then a watchdog on the lie: if the
timer interrupt is late twice, the motor current is removed. The watchdog is a hardware
timer that the control task must kick. A software counter inside the late task will not
count.
The vision and factor computer has two periodic tasks on one processor. Vision needs
up to 30 ms every 50 ms. The search needs up to 40 ms every 200 ms. If vision is released
first and runs to its budget, the search can start 30 ms into a vision period. In the worst
phasing, vision is released again before the search finishes. Preemptive priority is given
to vision, so the search is suspended. The search’s worst-case response is its own 40 ms
plus the vision executions that can preempt it. In 40 ms of search time at most one vision
job is released, adding 30 ms, so the response is at most 70 ms. The deadline is 200 ms.
The margin is 130 ms, which is the quantity that absorbs a slow frame, a checksum retry,
and the logger. This is a response-time bound for two periodic tasks with one preemption
edge, not a general schedulability theorem. It is enough for this task set because there are
only two tasks and the higher one does not preempt itself.

The bound fails if the vision budget is wrong. A frame that takes 80 ms blows the 50 ms
period and steals the margin from the search. The fault rule already treats a late feature
datagram as a hold on B. The additional requirement is that a frame which overruns is
aborted at 30 ms and published as a miss, rather than allowed to complete late and then
count as a success. Aborting a late frame is what keeps the 70 ms bound true. Completing
it “just this once” is how the corn vision meter lost the 6 km/h case [12].

The grid writes a step. The shaker and the regulator do not. Each is modelled, for the

shows that the requirement is a time-constant requirement of about 40 ms, not a vague
“fast enough”. A shaker amplifier that is fast in current and slow in mechanical stroke

Equation (2.23).

follow. The limit is therefore not hiding an actuator that cannot keep up. It is preventing
the search from chattering between the 3 Hz and 9 Hz near-tie when the sample mean

the shaker between two equally good commands. With the slew, a change from 9 Hz to
3 Hz takes three periods, 0.6 s, which is about two or three cell events at 4.4 seeds/s. The
seeds in between see an intermediate frequency. That intermediate is one of the middle
frequencies the grid dislikes. The estimation does not simulate the slew. It applies the
chosen command for the whole run, because each reported case has constant lot statistics
and a constant disturbance. A bout in which the disturbance steps from 6 Hz to 16 Hz
would pay a short transient the tables do not contain. The transient is bounded by 0.6 s of
travel, about 0.7 m at 1.1 m/s, which is three target spacings. Three spacings are visible
on a belt and invisible in a 4,000-event mean.

The shaft encoder sets a floor under the fuzzy loop that the continuous model hides. A
4096-count encoder on the cell shaft has an angular quantum of 2π/4096 = 1.53×10−3 rad.
At the 10 ms sample the corresponding speed quantum, if speed is a first difference, is

the command if the difference is taken raw. That is coarser than the 0.012 tracking residual

is the feedback ωn , and the derivative filter runs on the tracking error of that alreadywindowed speed. A second difference of the raw counts is not computed.
The same 80 ms window is the right size relative to a cell. At ω = 2.30 rad/s a 12-cell
wheel presents a new cell every 2π/(12ω) = 0.228 s. The vision period, 50 ms, samples
the throat about four times per cell. The motor period samples it about 23 times. The
factor period, 200 ms, is almost one cell. A constraint that changes σ therefore takes
effect on the cell that is about to be filled, not on a cell that has already released. This
timing is the reason the camera looks at the throat rather than at the tube. A tube gate
0.3 s later, from the flight calculation, reports a cell that the wheel has already left. Four
vision samples per cell is also the budget inside which a single aborted frame, the 30 ms
abort of the response-time argument, still leaves three frames. The bridge index is a vote
across those frames through the one-pole filter of Equation (2.4), whose 0.92 coefficient
remembers roughly twelve frames. A one-frame lie decays. A real bridge, present for a
third of a second, accumulates.
A two-step horizon is the natural objection to the one-step grid. Suppose the disturbance is known to step from 6 Hz to 16 Hz one period from now, and the command is 9 Hz.
A two-step search could move toward 3 Hz on this period, arriving as the step arrives,
whereas the one-step search waits to see 16 Hz and then slews. The value of that preview
is the cost of three spacings of intermediate frequency, which the slew analysis already
bounded at about 0.7 m of travel. Preview would save that 0.7 m. It would require a
forecast of terrain frequency. The inertial unit’s one-second Fourier window is a report
of the past second, not a forecast of the next 200 ms. Using it as a forecast would be a
model of the ground that this machine does not have. The one-step search is the controller
that matches the sensor. A terrain map from a previous pass would justify a two-step,
or a longer, horizon, and would be a different study, closer to the prescription maps of
variable-rate cereal planting than to a single-row hardwood meter [58, 59, 15].
The small-gain view of the constraint is similar in spirit. The map from σ to the next
bridge index is not known as a transfer function. What is known is a bound. The scale
cannot fall by more than 0.22, and the occupancy filter cannot move by more than 0.08 of
its input per vision sample. A scale cut therefore cannot chase its own tail inside one cell
period: the wheel slows, the filter moves a small step toward the clear-throat target, and
the next σ is computed from a B that has not yet fully forgotten the bridge. The loop can

Chapter 2. Design of the Two Controllers

oscillate only if B itself oscillates, which a real arch does not do at 20 Hz. The cap and
the filter are the small-gain ingredients. They are not a computed gain margin. They are
the structural reason a margin is plausible, and they are the knobs a bench would turn if
the wheel hunted.

2.2.17

Rates and power a builder can check with a stopwatch

The kinematic numbers are simple enough to audit without the simulator, and they should
be audited, because every later index is a fraction of them. At v = 1.10 m/s and Xt =
0.25 m the target is λ⋆ = 4.40 seeds/s, which is 264 seeds per minute and 15,840 seeds
per hour. A 15 litre hopper holding acorns at a bulk density near 0.6 kg/L holds about
9 kg. Red-oak acorns near 4.6 g give roughly 2,000 seeds in a full hopper, which is
2000/4.40 ≈ 455 s, or seven and a half minutes, of continuous metering. The hopper is a
short-bout vessel on purpose. A refill warning at 15 percent mass fires with a little more
than a minute left, which is enough to stop at the end of a pass and not enough to forget.
Pin-oak seeds are lighter, so the same mass is more seeds and a longer bout. Bur oak is
the opposite. The warning is on mass, not on a seed count, because the load cell does not
know the species and the camera’s count of the throat is not a count of the hopper.
The shaft speed that serves this rate, with twelve cells and a unit fill, is 22.0 r/min. A
builder with a tachometer and an empty throat can command that speed and watch the
encoder. The check does not need seeds. If the tachometer reads 22 r/min while the radar
reads 1.10 m/s and the set spacing is 0.25 m, Equation (2.5) is implemented. Seeds are
what test the fill, not the arithmetic.

this spacing. The machine is not specified to run there. The inequality says why. It is
the same lesson as the 6 km/h reversal on the corn meter, written as a speed limit that
belongs to the vision budget rather than to the agronomy [12].

2.2.18

What an empty cell does to the count

The rate audit above assumes every cell releases one seed. The indices exist because that
assumption fails, and the failure has a counting form that is easy to misread on a display.
Suppose that over N cell passages the machine travels N Xt /σ and releases Ns singles
and Nd doubles, with the rest empty. The number of seeds is Ns + 2Nd . The number of
positive spacings is one less than the number of seeds, which for a long run is essentially
Ns + 2Nd . A double contributes a short spacing and also splits what would have been one
interval into two, so it increases the spacing count. A miss contributes no spacing of its
own. It lengthens a neighbour. The miss index is therefore not 100(N − Ns − Nd )/N .
That ratio is the empty-cell rate. The miss index is the fraction of observed spacings that
exceed 1.5Xt . If empties arrive in isolation, each empty lengthens one spacing from about
Xt to about 2Xt and the miss index is close to the empty-cell rate. If two empties arrive
together, one spacing of about 3Xt replaces two empty cells in the count, and the miss
index, which divides by the number of spacings rather than by the number of cells, moves

Chapter 2. Design of the Two Controllers

51

differently from the empty-cell rate. The plantation looks worse. The percentage may
rise or fall. The estimation’s event model places isolated empties more often than bursts,
because each event is drawn independently. A real arch lasts for several cells and would
burst. The bridge constraint is the mechanism aimed at that burst: it slows the wheel
while B stays high, which is a run of cells rather than one draw. The independent-draw
estimation therefore understates the value of the constraint on true arches and overstates
the chance that a single slow cell is enough. That bias is a reason the both-mode gain
on bur oak in Chapter 3 should be read as a lower bound on what a persistent bridge
would give the constraint to do, and as an upper bound on how tidy the resulting spacings
would look. Independent draws make tidy tables. Arches make clumps. The controller is
designed for the clumps. The table is computed from the draws. Both facts belong in the
design record so that a belt test full of clumps is not scored as a refutation of a table that
assumed none.
A twenty-cell strip makes the distinction numerical. Write E for an empty cell and S
for a single, and ignore doubles. The release pattern
SSESSSEESSSSESSSSSES
has five empties in twenty cells, an empty-cell rate of 25 percent. The spacings, in units
of Xt and dropping the final open end, are approximately

Fourteen spacings follow. Four of them exceed 1.5, so the miss index is 4/14 ≈ 29 percent,

counts empties, and a display that shows 29 percent miss index from a belt, which counts
spacings, are both right. They are not the same statistic. The estimation reports the
belt statistic, Equations (1.7)–(1.9). The tube gate in the build reports the empty-cell
rate through Equation (2.16). An operator who treats them as one number will “tune”
a controller that is already consistent. The design keeps them on different computers for
that reason, not only for the deadline.

What the extra analysis changes

None of these calculations change the five modes of the estimation. They change what
a disagreement with the estimation would mean. A two-centimetre bias is flight, not a
bad gain. A tie between 3 Hz and 9 Hz is a flat cost, not a bug in the arg-minimum. A
vision overrun that is allowed to finish is a broken response-time bound, not a mysterious
rise in misses. The controllers remain the constraint of Equation (2.7) and the grid of
Equation (2.37). The surrounding arithmetic is the part that makes those two equations
implementable on the two computers of Section 3.1 without confusing their faults with

System Build and Estimation
The controllers of Chapter 2 need a machine that can produce their signals and a numerical
procedure that can score them without pretending to be a field season. Section 3.1 specifies
that machine as a system build: mechanics only so far as the signals require, then sensors,
computers, timing, and calibration. Section 3.2 integrates the same models the controllers
use and reads the indices defined in Section 1.3.

3.1

System build

3.1.1

What the build is for

The build is a single-row wooden-seed planter whose information architecture matches
Figure 2.3. It is specified here as an engineering description, not as a fabricated prototype.
No mass, no weld, and no photographed assembly is claimed. The description is detailed
because a control design that does not name its sensors cannot be implemented, and
because several modelling assumptions in Chapter 2 are assumptions about hardware.
The isolator attenuation αiso = 0.55, the depth-noise reduction from 6.5 mm to 1.2 mm,
and the refusal to take speed from a driven wheel are all build decisions. If a later prototype
changes them, the estimation has to be rerun. That dependency is intentional.
The agronomic envelope is the one already used in the hardwood trials and in the
direct-seeding review. Forward speed is 0.6 to 1.2 m/s. Target spacing is 0.25 m, which is
a restoration spacing rather than a nursery-bed spacing of several seeds per foot [17, 1].
Target depth is 30 mm, inside the band in which acorns are covered without being buried

53

Chapter 3. System Build and Estimation

past their reserves. The cell mouth is 24 mm, chosen as a compromise around red oak and
knowingly wrong for pin oak and bur oak. The control system is the means of living with
that compromise. A family of interchangeable cells would also work, and would not need
this thesis. The build keeps one cell in order to force the controllers to do the work.

3.1.2

Mechanical path of a seed

Seeds sit in a hopper of about 15 litres, enough for a short bout at 4.4 seeds/s without a
refill and small enough that the changing head of seeds is a real load disturbance on the
wheel. The hopper floor is a short vibratory tray driven by an electromagnetic shaker.
The tray discharges into a throat that is the camera’s window. Below the window, a
12-cell wheel of 180 mm pitch diameter picks seeds from the throat and carries them to
a release at the bottom of the housing. Elastomer inserts in the cells accept a diameter
band around 24 mm without machining a new wheel for each species. They do not, and
cannot, accept a 32 mm acorn and a 15 mm acorn with equal fill. That is the residual the
controllers are scored on.
A low-pressure port opens onto the cell during the rising half of the rotation and is
vented before release. The port is the assist vacuum. Its regulator must be able to hold
0 kPa, meaning the port is open to atmosphere, because the cost will select that point
for small seeds. A regulator that cannot go to zero is the wrong part, even if its span is
otherwise impressive. The maximum used by the grid is 5 kPa, and the hardware stop is
8 kPa, above the grid so that a faulted command still has a relief valve under the cereal
pressures that this machine deliberately does not use [9, 35].
From the release, a smooth tube of 40 mm inside diameter drops the seed to the opener.
The tube is straight enough that a photoelectric gate sees one chord of the seed, and short
enough that time of flight at a drop of 0.4 m is under 0.3 s. That delay is why the gate
is a logger and not the bridge sensor. The opener is a narrow shoe behind a ripper point,
the arrangement Wells adopted after a plain no-till opener failed to penetrate spoil [16].
Depth is set by a linear actuator against a gauge wheel, with a potentiometer on the pivot.
A press wheel closes the slot. The press wheel is not controlled. Its load is a spring.
The vibratory tray, the cell wheel, the vacuum port, and the opener are the four actuated
elements. Everything else is structure. The structure that matters to the model is the
mount between the seed box and the frame. Rubber isolators are specified at the box feet,
and the coordinator-on case takes their attenuation of terrain frequency as αiso = 0.55

shaker frequency on top of an already harsh frame is expensive in misses. A measured
transmissibility would replace the constant 0.55 without a change to the grid search.

Computers and the bus between them

Two processors are specified, matching the deadline split.
The feed computer is a small microcontroller with a hardware encoder interface and
a 10 ms timer interrupt that cannot be disabled by the communication routine. It runs
Equation (2.12) and Equation (2.7). It receives feature datagrams and factor-health bits.
It does not decode images. Its worst-case execution for the control step is required to be
under 2 ms, leaving 8 ms of idle time inside the period. That idle time is the margin
Borja’s combined processor did not have [12].
The vision and factor computer is a single-board processor with a camera interface.
Vision and the grid search share it because both are slow relative to the motor and both
tolerate a missed deadline. They do not share a thread. Vision is a high-priority periodic
thread at 50 ms. The grid search is a lower-priority thread released every 200 ms. If the
search is still running when the next vision frame is due, the frame runs and the search
continues. If the search is still running at the next 200 ms boundary, its result is discarded
and the previous (f, P ) is held. Discarding a late result is safer than applying a result
computed from a stale d¯ that has since been replaced. The search was sized at 55 costs so

Chapter 3. System Build and Estimation

that a discard is a fault, not the normal path.

The datagram between the boards is a fixed 32-byte record: time stamp, d,
vision-health, factor-health, f , P , and a checksum. A failed checksum is treated as a
missed vision frame and follows the hold-and-decay rule. There is no dynamic allocation

3.1.6

Calibration procedure

Calibration is a procedure, not a learning algorithm. It is specified because the features and
the depth servo are meaningless without it. The order matters. A scale factor computed
before the camera is clamped will be repeated after the next service and will silently shift
B.
1. Clamp the camera and the lamp. Place the 20 mm disk in the throat on the centreline. Record the equivalent diameter reported by Equation (1.6) and set the
millimetre scale so that the report is 20 mm. Repeat at 10 mm left and right of
centre. Reject the mount if the three reports differ by more than 0.6 mm.
2. Empty the throat. Record the residual image used by the background subtraction.
Confirm that occupancy reads below 0.02.
3. Command the wheel at 15 r/min with an empty throat. The shaft encoder and a
hand tachometer shall agree within 0.3 r/min. This checks Equation (2.5)’s sensor,
not the seed path.
4. Command f from 2 to 12 Hz in 2 Hz steps against a strobe or a frequency output
of the amplifier. The inertial unit, mounted for this step on the tray rather than on
the frame, shall report the same peak within 0.5 Hz. Then return the unit to the
frame.
5. Command P at 0, 2, and 5 kPa. The transducer shall read within 0.3 kPa. The zero
command shall read below 0.2 kPa. A zero that actually holds 1 kPa will bias the
cost toward doubles on small seeds.
6. Set the opener on a flat rail and command 20, 30, and 40 mm. The potentiometer
reading shall be within 1.5 mm. This is the sensor error. The 1.2 mm standard
deviation used in the estimation is the closed-loop residual about the set-point, which

Chapter 3. System Build and Estimation

59

includes rail roughness in the model and is not the same number as the calibration
tolerance.
7. Drive the unit on a hard surface at a constant 1.0 m/s and compare radar speed
with distance over time. The mean error shall be below 2 percent. Slip of a driven
wheel is not a failure of this check. A difference between radar and the driven wheel
is the slip the control law is there to ignore.
No seed is required for these steps. A seed check is a bench experiment and sits outside
this manuscript. The estimation does not use the tolerances as random errors except for
the depth residual and the slip model already stated. Adding the calibration tolerances as
extra noise would be a straightforward sensitivity run. It was not the run reported below.

3.1.7

Safety interlocks

The wheel motor is disabled when the opener is raised, so that a headland turn does not
drill seeds onto the surface. The shaker is disabled when the hopper mass is below the
refill threshold, so that an empty tray is not driven at 10 Hz for a bout. The vacuum
pump or regulator is disabled at the same time. These interlocks are combinational logic
on the feed computer, independent of the factor search. A software fault in J cannot leave
the shaker running on an empty machine once the load cell has crossed the threshold. The
load cell itself can fail. A stuck high reading leaves the shaker enabled, which is the worse
direction for noise and the safer direction for not silencing a full hopper. The failure is
detected by the operator from the mass display, not by a second estimator.

3.2

Estimation

3.2.1

What is being estimated

The estimation integrates the event model of Section 2.2.2 over long sequences of seeds.
It asks how the indices move when the feed law and the factor law are switched on and
off. The plant and the coordinator’s predictor are the same logistic map. The Monte
Carlo still has private randomness: each event draws a diameter from the lot, a slip, and
a depth error. The coordinator sees only the lot mean and coefficient of variation. The
feed constraint sees a filtered occupancy state, not the next diameter. The comparison

Chapter 3. System Build and Estimation

60

is therefore a comparison of information and of actions, not a replay of one open-loop
trajectory.
Five modes are defined.
1. Ground. The wheel is geared to the driven wheel. The speed scale equals 1−s. Slip
has mean 0.07 + 0.012 times slope in degrees. Factors are fixed at f = 0, P = 3 kPa,
and a passive depth error of standard deviation 6.5 mm. Terrain enters with αiso = 1.
2. PID. Electronic tracking of ω ⋆ from radar speed. The scale is 1 plus a small tracking
residual of standard deviation 0.012. No vision constraint. Factors remain at the
ground-drive constants. Slip is rejected except for a quarter of the ground-drive slip
passed into the probability map, representing residual speed error.
3. Vision. The PID mode plus the bridge constraint of Equation (2.7). Factors remain
fixed. This mode is the first innovation alone.
4. MPC. The PID mode plus the grid minimiser of Equation (2.37), the isolated mount
αiso = 0.55, and the tight depth residual of standard deviation 1.2 mm. Vision is
off. This mode is the second innovation alone.
5. Both. Vision and MPC together.
Each case runs 4,000 cell events. The random generator is the NumPy generator with
seed 20261029 for the main table, and with seeds 20261029 + 17r for twelve repeats of
the mixed-lot gain. The script that implements the modes is simulate_estimation.py,
stored with this manuscript. A reimplementation that follows Section 2.2.2 and the mode
list above should match the table to sampling error. The script is the definition of the
sampling error that was actually used.
Lots are log-normal in diameter. Pin oak has mean 15 mm and coefficient of variation
0.12. Red oak has 20.5 mm and 0.15. Bur oak has 32 mm and 0.20. The mixed lot has
22 mm and 0.32, which is a stand-in for a hopper that was not cleaned between species.
Scenarios cross these lots with speed, slope, and a terrain frequency. The eight scenarios
are the rows of the tables in this section. They were chosen to probe the requirements: a
mild red-oak baseline, a slope, a harsh vibration, a small seed, a large seed, and a mixture.
Indices follow Equations (1.7)–(1.11). Positions are accumulated by advancing Xt /σ at
each event, placing one seed on a single, placing two seeds 0.22Xt apart on a double, and
placing nothing on a miss. Scatter from Equation (2.28) is added to the placed positions.

3.2.7

Energy and the price terms

Energy in the tables is not joules on a named motor. It is the mean per event of 0.08ω 2 +
0.012f 2 + 0.02P 2 , evaluated in the script so that a faster wheel, a faster shaker, and a
higher vacuum all cost something. Because the optimum vacuum is zero in every MPC
row, the vacuum term does not drive the comparison. The shaker term does. Mild red
oak at 9 Hz costs about 1.39 per event in the both-mode, against 0.60 for PID. Harsh red
oak at 3 Hz costs 0.53, which is less than PID, because the wheel speed is similar and the
shaker is quiet. The prices inside J are what allow that quiet point to win when agitation
would push feff over 10 Hz. If the prices were removed, the grid could still avoid 10 Hz
through the harshness term in zm . The prices matter for ties. They are not the sole reason
the harsh-case command falls.
A deployer who is short of electrical power learns a specific lesson. The camera and the
fuzzy loop are cheap in this energy proxy. The shaker is the cost of the second innovation,
and it is a cost the optimiser already turns down when the frame is doing the shaking.
Running the shaker at 9 Hz out of habit during a 16 Hz bout would pay the energy and,
inside the model, would also pay in misses.

3.2.8

Where the estimation can mislead

Several readings would be stronger than the evidence.
The plant is the predictor. A coordinator that minimises J on Π will look good when
the world is Π. The private randomness prevents a perfect score, and the tables show

Chapter 3. System Build and Estimation

67

residual misses above 6 percent even in the best red-oak row, but a systematic error in
the sign of a coefficient would move the real machine in the wrong direction while these
tables stayed flattering. The coefficients most exposed to that risk are the 10 Hz anchor,
which was observed on rapeseed [14, 13], and the isolator constant 0.55, which was not
observed at all. A bench measurement of acorn fill against shaker frequency on this cell
would replace both the anchor and the Gaussian width in Equation (2.21). Until that
measurement exists, the ranking is a design calculation.
The camera is not simulated as an image. Occupancy is a filtered state driven by the
rule in the script, not by rendered acorns. Occlusion, dust, and strobe failure are absent.
The fault logic specifies what the software should do when features stop. It does not
estimate how often they stop. Borja’s accuracy on a clean plate, a fraction of a percent of
frames wrong, is the optimistic reference [12]. A throat of wooden seeds is a harder image
than a painted plate hole.
Depth noise is an independent Gaussian. Real depth error is correlated along the row,
because the soil is correlated. Correlated error would widen the distribution of local
spacing less than the independent model if the opener rides over a long wave, and it would
create bursts of misses if the opener dives. The index means might move less than the
appearance of a planted row. The estimation reports means.
Germination, rodent take, and the difference between a spacing and a surviving seedling
are outside the indices by the scope statement [1, 2]. A high ηE can still be a poor
plantation. The machine-level claim stops at placement.
The ground-drive baseline uses P = 3 kPa because that is a plausible leftover cereal
setting, not because a hardwood manufacturer recommends it. Part of the MPC gain is
the removal of a harmful default. If the baseline is redefined as P = 0 and f = 0 with
electronic speed already on, the remaining gain is agitation, isolation, and depth, plus
vision. That comparison is the PID-versus-MPC gap in the tables, not the ground-versusboth gap. On mild red oak it is 87.0 − 61.0 = 26.0 efficiency points, still large, and it
does not depend on accusing the ground drive of a bad vacuum. On that reading the PID
row already has the bad vacuum. The MPC row removes it. The gain is still the factor
decision.

Answers to the research questions

The questions in Section 1.3 can now be answered in the only sense this manuscript
supports: as statements about the model.
The feed-speed loop with a vision constraint reduces doubles beyond shaft-speed tracking. On mild red oak the multiple index falls from 22.5 to 12.5 percent. On pin oak it falls
from 34.3 to 24.3 percent. The same loop does not, by itself, reduce misses on bur oak
or under a 16 Hz disturbance, and it can lower planting efficiency by stretching spacings.
The first innovation is a quality intervention with a rate side effect. It should not be sold
as a productivity controller.
The factor coordinator improves the indices in the regimes where tracking has little to
offer. Under 16 Hz terrain the efficiency index moves from 21.8 percent with PID to 64.5
percent with the coordinator, at a lower shaker command. On harsh bur oak it moves
from 11.9 percent to 44.0 percent before vision is added. The coordinator selects zero
assist vacuum for every lot in the grid. Within the model, the right vacuum for this cell
is none.
The loops interfere when the factor regime is bad, and they reinforce when the regime
is good. Interference shows up as a quality gain without an efficiency gain: vision versus
PID on mild red oak, and both versus MPC under 16 Hz. Reinforcement shows up as a
gain in both indices: both versus MPC on pin oak, on bur oak, and on the mixed lot. The
architecture did not make interference impossible. It made interference visible, because the
two indices are published separately and because the coordinator is not allowed to assume
that vision will hide its mistakes. That visibility is the system result. An implementer
can turn the constraint off under a measured high terrain frequency and leave it on for a
mixed or small-seed lot, using the same programs.

3.2.10

What a bench would have to show

A minimal bench, if one is built from Section 3.1, would need only enough instrumentation
to recompute Table 3.3 and Table 3.4. A sticky belt or a grease belt under the tube, a
variable-speed belt under the wheels, and a shaker under the frame would reproduce the
five modes [31, 26]. The acceptance test is not a single threshold. It is the ranking.
Vision should cut the multiple index relative to PID on red oak and on pin oak. Vision
alone should not be required to raise efficiency under a frame vibration above 10 Hz. The

Chapter 3. System Build and Estimation

69

coordinator should select a low frequency in that case and a higher frequency when the
frame is quiet. Both together should beat PID on bur oak by a margin large enough to see
in a few hundred seeds, not only in 4,000. If the bench reverses the sign of the frequency
effect, the Gaussian in Equation (2.21) is the part to refit. The cascade structure, the
deadline split, and the separation of Qf from ηE would remain the parts worth keeping.
Until that belt is run, the numbers in this chapter are an estimate of a design, computed
from a map that encodes the published failure modes of hardwood metering, of groundwheel slip, of high-frequency vibration, and of a vision loop that slows the input of seeds.
They are large enough, and consistent enough across lots, to justify the bench. They are
not themselves the bench.

3.2.12

Reading the near-tie under harsh vibration

Section 3.2 reported a 3 Hz command for red oak at 16 Hz terrain. The grid slice in
Chapter 2 shows why that command should not be over-interpreted. At the nominal
diameter the predicted costs at 3 Hz and at 9 Hz differ by about 0.0001. The drawn
sample mean in the recorded run selected 3 Hz. Plant-side, with αiso = 0.55, a 3 Hz
command and a 16 Hz terrain give feff ≈ 9.3 Hz, and a 9 Hz command would give about
12.6 Hz. The plant therefore experiences a real difference between the two commands
2
even though the predictor, using the heavier weight 0.35 on fdist
, scores them almost alike.

The run that was printed used 3 Hz. A second run whose sample mean broke the tie the
other way would print 9 Hz and would show a higher plant-side harshness. The efficiency
gain over PID would remain, because both corners beat a command of 0 Hz, which is the
PID and ground setting. The identity of the winning hertz would not remain. That is the
correct confidence statement. The coordinator rejects the middle of the frequency range
and rejects a non-zero vacuum. It does not, on this cost, identify a unique frequency once
the terrain is already harsh.

3.2.13

A note on repeatability of the other scenarios

The twelve-repeat standard deviation of 0.89 points was computed only for the mixed-lot
efficiency gain. The other scenarios were single runs of 4,000 events. A binomial picture of
a proportion near 0.6 with 4,000 trials has a standard error near

q

0.6 · 0.4/4000 ≈ 0.008,

or 0.8 percentage points, before the dependence among successive spacings is considered.
Dependence widens the error. Doubling the binomial picture is a cautious reading: differences of two points in the tables are inside noise, and differences of ten points are

Chapter 3. System Build and Estimation

71

not. The vision-versus-PID multiple-index drop on mild red oak is about ten points. The
both-versus-ground efficiency gaps are tens of points. The vision-versus-PID efficiency
differences of one point are not a finding. They were described as “does not follow” and
“stays”, which is the language that one point deserves.
The script seed is part of the result. A reader who changes the seed will move the fourth
digit of a long run and can move the harsh-case frequency across the tie. Publishing the
seed, 20261029, and the repeat rule 20261029 + 17r, is what makes that movement a check
rather than a contradiction. The file estimation_results.json stores the run that the
tables were typed from.
