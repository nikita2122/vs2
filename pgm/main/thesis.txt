                                         Abstract

This thesis studies the last visual check of a brewery packaging line: beer crates stacked on
a pallet, carried by a forklift into the warehouse. A camera looking at the side of the load
must name each crate by body color and must count how many crates pass. The stack is
regular, the colors are few, and most of the geometry is known in advance. The image is
not. Forks shadow the bottom row, the top row is often cut by the frame, adjacent crates
of the same color share a weak seam, and the aisle mixes daylight with lamps whose color
changes through the day.
    The starting point is YOLO-vf-seg, an instance segmenter in the one-stage YOLO
family whose neck is biased toward the horizontal lips of a side-view stack. Two updates
are developed on top of that segmenter. The first, the illumination-invariant chromatic
prototype head (ICPH), does not ask a global white-balance algorithm to repaint the
frame. It builds, inside each predicted mask, a feature that keeps reflectance chromaticity,
opponent color, and an estimate of the local illuminant, and it scores that feature against
a learned prototype of each crate color. The second, perspective lattice completion (PLC),
rectifies the front face, snaps mask centers to the known crate pitch, fills a single-cell gap
only when orthogonal neighbors support it, and multiplies the occupied front cells by a
pallet-depth reading.
    Both updates are estimated by a Monte Carlo observation model, not by a recording
from a brewery aisle. Under that model, ICPH holds color accuracy better than raw
prototype matching once the illuminant becomes strong, and it avoids the collapse that
gray-world and shades-of-gray corrections produce on a nearly uniform plastic face. PLC,
applied to one frame, is the most accurate of the six counters that were compared. Fusing
every frame of a passage by a simple union is not: false cells accumulate and the gap
rule then invents crates. The thesis therefore specifies the system around a single sharp
frame, states the geometry and the timing budget, and treats the numerical chapter as an
estimate of the two modules under an explicit generative model.

Chapter 1

Related Work and the Problem This
Thesis Addresses

The counting problem in this thesis sits at the junction of three older lines of work. The
first is instance segmentation: the assignment of a separate mask to every object, rather
than a single label per pixel. The second is visual counting, which began with crowds and
cells and has only recently been asked to count goods that hide one another. The third is
computational color constancy, the attempt to separate the color of a surface from the
color of the light that falls on it. A brewery aisle forces all three to be solved together, on
a moving forklift, from a camera that sees only one face of the stack. This chapter sets out
the principles, then the systems that have already been built for warehouses and cartons,
and then the precise gap that the rest of the thesis fills.


1.1      Principles of segmentation, counting, and color
1.1.1     What the camera records
A digital camera does not record the paint on a crate. It records a number in each of three
channels, and that number mixes the spectrum of the lamp, the reflectance of the plastic,
the spectral sensitivity of the sensor, and the exposure chosen by the shutter. Under the
standard simplification used throughout color constancy, a Lambertian surface under a
single illuminant collapses to a diagonal model [1]. If R(x) ∈ R3 is the reflectance of the
surface at pixel x and L ∈ R3 is a positive illuminant vector, the observed intensity is the
element-wise product
                                      I(x) = L ⊙ R(x).                                 (1.1)

The same crate, moved from a north-facing door to a sodium lamp deeper in the aisle,
changes L and therefore changes I, while R stays fixed. Any classifier that memorizes I is
memorizing the lamp as well as the plastic.


                                              1
    Three further effects sit on top of (1.1) in the scene of this thesis, and none of them
is a small correction. Shading from the forks and from the crate above multiplies I by a
scalar that varies smoothly across the face. A neighboring crate of another color leaks into
the mask wherever the predicted boundary is a few pixels too wide. Motion of the forklift,
if the shutter is open for more than a few milliseconds, smears the horizontal lip of each
crate along the direction of travel. The diagonal model remains the right first equation
because the two updates in Chapter 2 attack illuminant and geometry separately, but the
estimate in Chapter 3 puts shading, edge mixing, and a detection loss that grows with
blur back into the observation model so that the diagonal story is not asked to carry the
whole scene.

point X on the front face of the stack to a pixel

When the front face is approximately a plane, the map from that plane to the image is a

1.1.2     Detection, semantic segmentation, and instance segmenta-
          tion
Object detection returns a box and a class. The modern two-stage line begins with region
proposals and a convolutional classifier [4], speeds the classifier by sharing the feature
map [5], and then learns the proposals themselves [6]. A cascade of detectors trained at
increasing intersection-over-union tightens the boxes that survive [7]. One-stage detectors
skip the proposal stage. SSD places default boxes on several feature maps [8]. RetinaNet
shows that the extreme imbalance between background anchors and object anchors can be
handled by a focal loss that down-weights the easy negatives [9]. FCOS removes anchors
and regresses a box from each foreground pixel [10]. CornerNet detects a pair of corners
and groups them [11]. CenterNet detects the center point and reads the box size from that
point [12]. YOLO, from its first version, frames detection as one regression on a grid [13].
    Semantic segmentation assigns a class to every pixel and does not say which crate is
which. Fully convolutional networks showed that a classifier can be turned into a dense
predictor [14]. U-Net added a skip-connected decoder that restores spatial detail [15].

                                             2
DeepLabv3+ combined atrous convolution in the encoder with a light decoder [16]. None
of these outputs can be counted by connected components when two crates of the same
color touch, because the two crates are one component.
    Instance segmentation returns a separate mask per object. Mask R-CNN adds a small
fully convolutional mask head on each region of interest of Faster R-CNN, and aligns the
pooled features with the box by bilinear sampling [17]. The mask head is class-specific in
the original design: the network predicts a mask for every class and the box class selects
which mask is kept. That separation matters for the brewery. Color type and spatial
extent are different questions. A mask can be right while the color name is wrong, and
the counting stage should be able to use the mask either way.
    The optimization and generalization background used throughout is the standard one
for deep networks [18, 19]. Training these models depends on large labeled sets. Pascal
VOC established the detection challenge that the first deep detectors were measured on [20].
ImageNet supplied the pretraining that made those detectors converge [21]. COCO raised
the instance count per image and became the standard mask benchmark [22]. A warehouse
of identical crates is a different distribution from COCO. The features transfer; the decision
threshold and the color head do not, which is why a prototype head trained on crate colors
is part of the update rather than a reuse of the COCO classifier.


1.1.3     The one-stage mask and the YOLO line
Two-stage mask heads are accurate and, for a forklift that may clear the camera in under
a second, often too sequential. YOLACT removed the region-wise mask head. It predicts
a dictionary of prototype masks on the whole image and a vector of coefficients on each
detection, then forms the instance mask as a linear combination of the prototypes, cropped
to the box [23]. YOLACT++ keeps that assembly and improves the prototypes and the
backbone [24]. The assembly is
                                      M = σ(P c) ,                                    (1.3)

where P is the stack of prototype masks, c is the coefficient vector of one detection, and σ is
a sigmoid. The prototypes are shared. Individuality lives in c. FastSAM later showed that
a YOLOv8 segmenter, which uses this same assembly, can be trained as a fast proposal
generator for a segment-anything style task [25].
    Other one-stage designs are worth keeping in view because they expose different failure
modes. SOLO predicts a mask on a spatial grid, so that location itself is the identity of the
instance [26]. SOLOv2 makes the mask weights dynamic and adds a matrix non-maximum
suppression that is cheaper than a pixel-wise suppression [27]. CondInst generates the
weights of a tiny mask head from the instance features, conditioned on the instance [28].
BlendMask combines a top-down attention on a bottom-up position-sensitive map [29].
PolarMask represents the contour as rays from the center, which is attractive for compact


                                              3
blobs and brittle for a crate whose visible region is a rectangle with a bite taken out
by occlusion [30]. SparseInst activates a sparse set of instance queries and avoids non-
maximum suppression [31]. BoxInst shows that a projection loss and a pairwise color
affinity can train a CondInst-style mask from boxes alone [32]. The pairwise term is
relevant here: plastic crates are nearly constant in color inside the face, so a color-affinity
prior agrees with the object, and it also agrees with the failure mode in which two adjacent
crates of the same color are glued into one mask.
    The YOLO family itself is the practical backbone of the baseline used in this thesis.
After the original grid regression [13], YOLO9000 introduced joint training and anchor
boxes [33], and YOLOv3 moved to a feature pyramid and a stronger backbone [34].
YOLOv4 organized a large set of training and architecture choices that had been scattered
across papers [35]. YOLOv5 made that line reproducible as a maintained implementa-
tion [36]. YOLOv6 reworked the neck and the loss for industrial deployment [37]. YOLOX
replaced the coupled head and the anchors with a decoupled head and an anchor-free as-
signment [38]. YOLOv7 studied which of the so-called freebies survive re-parameterization
and set a high bar for real-time detection [39]. YOLOv8, released as software rather than
as a single paper, is the version whose segmentation head follows (1.3) and whose detection
head is decoupled [40]. YOLOv9 introduced programmable gradient information so that
the loss sees a more complete view of the input, and showed a gain on the mask task as
well as on boxes [41]. YOLOv10 removed the need for non-maximum suppression by a
consistent dual assignment [42]. YOLO11 continues the same implementation line [43].
Real-time transformer detectors, from DETR [44] through deformable attention [45] and
denoising queries [46] to RT-DETR [47], have closed the speed gap. They are not the
baseline here, because the crate system needs a mask, a stable box center, and a small
extra head, and the YOLACT-style segmenter already provides the first two.
    The feature pyramid that these detectors share is the structure of Lin and colleagues [48]:
a top-down path that carries semantics down to high-resolution maps, and lateral con-
nections that restore detail. EfficientDet replaced the plain sum of those laterals with a
weighted bidirectional pyramid [49]. CSPNet reduced duplicate gradient flow by splitting
the feature map, and that split is the ancestor of the cross-stage modules in the later
YOLO backbones [50]. Channel attention of the squeeze-and-excitation kind [51], the
convolutional block attention module [52], and coordinate attention [53] are the usual
tools when a designer wants the network to prefer one spatial axis. The vertical-feature
neck of YOLO-vf-seg, defined properly in Section 1.3.5, is a deliberate use of that last
idea: on a side view the informative edges are horizontal, so the neck should be allowed to
treat the vertical coordinate differently from the horizontal one.
    Backbones have their own literature, and the warehouse papers cited in the next section
borrow from it. Residual networks made the depth of the backbone a free parameter
rather than an optimization accident [54]. MobileNet and MobileNetV2 showed that


                                              4
depthwise separable convolution and inverted residuals keep accuracy when the computer
is small [55, 56]. EfficientNet scaled depth, width, and resolution together [57]. ConvNeXt
modernized a plain convolutional network with the training recipe of transformers [58]. The
vision transformer and the Swin transformer replaced the sliding window with attention over
patches [59, 60], using the attention mechanism introduced for sequence transduction [61].
RepVGG trains a multi-branch block and folds it into a single convolution at inference [62].
That fold is why several recent warehouse models can quote a high frame rate after having
trained a heavier block. The present design does not replace the backbone. It adds a color
head whose parameter count is a fraction of one percent of a medium segmenter, and a
counting stage that is not a network at all.
    Box regression losses affect whether two stacked crates receive two centers or one
merged box. Generalized IoU adds the empty area of the smallest enclosing box to the
ordinary overlap penalty [63]. Distance-IoU and the complete-IoU variant add the distance
between centers and the aspect ratio, so that a non-overlapping prediction still receives a
gradient [64]. Wise-IoU modulates that geometry by a focusing coefficient that depends
on how the rest of the batch is behaving [65]. IoU-Net argued that the classification score
is a poor proxy for localization quality and predicted the overlap directly [66]. Generalized
focal loss and VarifocalNet fold a localization quality into the classification target so that
non-maximum suppression ranks boxes by a score that means what the suppression thinks
it means [67, 68]. For crates, a high classification score on a box that straddles two stacked
faces is exactly the error that produces a missed count. The lattice stage can repair some
of those misses. It cannot repair them if the center it receives lies between two true cells.
    Mask losses have a similar tension between region overlap and boundary fidelity. The
Dice coefficient [69] and its soft version in V-Net [70] emphasize overlap and are stable
when the crate occupies a modest part of the window. A generalized Dice reweights classes
by the inverse of their volume [71]. Overlap losses under-penalize a boundary that is off
by a few pixels, and a few pixels on a crate lip are the difference between one instance
and two. PointRend treats the uncertain boundary pixels as a rendering problem and
classifies them at higher resolution [72]. Boundary patch refinement crops small windows
along the predicted contour and re-segments those windows [73]. Both are compatible
with YOLO-vf-seg as a later refinement. They are not the updates proposed here, because
the dominant counting errors in a regular stack are missed cells and wrong colors, not a
contour that is cosmetically rough.


1.1.4     Counting what is visible and what is hidden
The oldest learned counter regresses a density map whose integral is the count. Lempit-
sky and Zisserman introduced that idea so that a model could be trained from dotted
annotations [74]. A multi-column network estimates the density at several receptive fields


                                              5
when the objects vary in scale [75]. CSRNet keeps a single deeper column and uses dilated
convolution to enlarge the field without losing resolution [76]. Later losses match the
predicted and target distributions rather than only their pixel values, which reduces the
tendency to place a blurry mass where a sharp peak should be [77]. A composition loss
combines counting, density, and localization [78]. Counting in the wild showed that the
same idea extends past crowds when the annotations are still points [79].
    A density map is the wrong intermediate for typed crates. The integral of a density
does not remember which color produced which mass of the integral, and a side view of a
stack violates the assumption that every object contributes a visible dot. The bottom row
is partly behind the forks. Crates in the second and third rank of the pallet are not in the
image at all. Integrating a density over the visible face estimates the visible count, and
only under the further assumption that occlusion eats a similar fraction of every crate.
That assumption is false at the bottom and at the top of the frame.
    Detection counting is the other extreme. Each surviving mask contributes one to a
tally. It is simple, it preserves class, and it is biased low by every miss. Area-ratio counting,
discussed with the warehouse systems in the next section, tries to recover partial crates by
dividing a visible area by a nominal area. It recovers a fraction of a crate and then has to
round. Geometry-aware counting uses the fact that the objects are the same size. That is
the opening this thesis uses. The front face of a beer-crate stack is a lattice with known
pitch. A missed detection is often a hole in an otherwise regular grid, and a hole with
supported neighbors is a different event from an empty region of the aisle.
    Hidden objects can also be counted from volume. Dumery and colleagues decompose
the count of identical stacked objects into a reconstructed volume times an occupancy ratio
estimated from a depth map, and they show that people are poor at the same task [80].
Their method assumes a container seen from several views, objects that are approximately
identical, and a depth estimator. A brewery forklift presents a different bargain. The
objects are identical only within a color, the colors matter, the useful view is the side
rather than the top, and the hidden dimension is a small integer: how many crates deep
the pallet is. Multiplying an occupied front lattice by that integer is a coarser, and for this
scene a more direct, use of the same idea that hidden extent should be measured rather
than hallucinated pixel by pixel.
    Associative embedding groups detections that belong to the same structured object
by a learned tag [81]. A Hungarian assignment matches two sets at minimum cost [82].
PLC does not need either construction for the front face, because the lattice supplies the
grouping. Both remain relevant if a later version of the system has to associate the same
physical crate across a long passage with a moving camera. The estimate in this thesis
uses a simpler rule, and Chapter 3 reports that the simpler temporal rule over-counts.

global estimate on the whole frame is not a repair: the frame contains a green crate, an
amber crate, a gray floor, and a yellow safety line, and the average of those surfaces is not
the illuminant on any one crate. Chapter 2 therefore keeps the Minkowski norm only as a
feature channel, computed inside the mask, and trains a classifier that is allowed to use
the residual chromaticity rather than being forced to trust the corrected color as if it were

1.1.6     Occlusion boundaries
When one crate overlaps another, the contour in the image is a mixture of the true object
boundary and the occlusion boundary. Standard mask heads do not distinguish the two,
so the occluded crate is trained to look like its visible fragment. BCNet models the local
region as two overlapping layers and regresses the occluder and the occludee with separate
graph-convolution branches, which decouples the two boundaries [91]. MaskFormer and
Mask2Former recast segmentation as mask classification with a transformer decoder, and
the masked attention of Mask2Former restricts each query to the region it already believes
is its own object [92, 93]. Mask DINO carries the same decoder into a detector that
shares queries between boxes and masks [94]. The segment-anything model and its video
successor show that a large promptable segmenter can produce masks for objects it was not
trained to name [95, 96]. Naming remains our problem. A foundation mask is a plausible
front end. It does not know that amber and yellow are different stock-keeping units, and
it does not know that a hole in a regular stack is probably a missed crate rather than a
hole in the load.
    The side-view stack adds a regularity that generic occlusion models do not use. Crates
of one product are the same size, they are placed on a pallet in a grid, and the hidden
ones continue that grid in depth. A bilayer head can paint the visible fragment of an
occluded crate more honestly. It cannot see the crate behind the front face. The count of
those crates is a geometric statement, not a segmentation statement. That split, between
a mask model for what is visible and a lattice model for the regular structure the mask
only samples, is the design choice defended in Section 1.3.5.

1.2      Previous systems for cartons, warehouses, and
         stacked goods
1.2.1     A dataset of stacked cartons
Public detection sets barely contain a loaded pallet. Yang and colleagues built the Stacked
Carton Dataset to close that gap: 16,136 images and about 250,000 instance masks, split
between warehouse photographs and images gathered online, with labels that mark a
carton as inner or outer and as occluded or fully visible [97]. The accompanying detector
starts from RetinaNet and adds two modules. Offset prediction between classification and
localization corrects the score used by non-maximum suppression so that a well-localized
box is not discarded in favor of a worse box with a higher class score. Boundary-guided
supervision uses the mask contour as an extra training signal so that repeated printed
textures do not glue neighboring cartons together. Both ideas transfer in spirit to beer
crates. The class score of a YOLO head is not a count, and the seam between two amber
crates is exactly the repeated-texture problem, only worse, because the texture is a flat
color rather than a printed logo.
    The benchmark on that dataset also records how standard detectors behave on stacks.
Two-stage and one-stage detectors, DETR, YOLOX, and CenterNet are all evaluated, and
so are several mask models. The spread of those results is a warning against quoting a
COCO mask average precision as if it were a warehouse count accuracy. A model can gain
several points of box average precision and still merge the seam that the count depends on.
The carton dataset does not, however, label body color as the quantity to be inventoried,
and it does not place the camera on the path of a forklift. It is the right evidence that
stacked boxes are a segmentation problem. It is not yet the problem in Section 1.3.


1.2.2     Instance segmentation of a warehouse scene
Zhang and Tan modify YOLOv8n for instance segmentation of an indoor warehouse [98].
They replace the feature block with a RepVGG-style block, insert a multi-scale convo-
lutional attention between the backbone and the decoder, and train with Wise-IoU. On
their CargoV10 set the mask mean average precision at an overlap of one half is reported
as 70.5 percent, 5.1 points above the unmodified baseline, at 84.8 frames per second. The
classes include goods, labels, forklifts, pallets, and people. That class list is the neighboring
problem, not the same problem. A forklift in their images is an object to be segmented.
In this thesis the forklift is the carrier of the object, and the camera is aimed at the load.
Their result still matters for two engineering reasons. First, a small YOLO segmenter
sustains a warehouse frame rate after the backbone has been re-parameterized, so a color
head of a few thousand parameters will not be what breaks the timing budget. Second,


                                               9
Wise-IoU and an attention block aimed at adjacent similar objects are evidence that the
community already treats “neighboring instances that look alike” as the warehouse failure
mode. Beer crates of one color are that failure mode in its pure form.
    CheapConv pushes the same industrial constraint from the other side: parcel segmen-
tation that has to run in real time on a logistics line, with an architecture pared down
for that budget [99]. The design lesson is identical. Accuracy that arrives only in a large
transformer decoder does not automatically survive contact with a line that cannot wait.
The updates in this thesis are shaped by that constraint. ICPH is a linear map on a pooled
feature. PLC is arithmetic on a few dozen centers.


1.2.3     Area ratios and three-dimensional occupancy
Hu, Wang, and Yang count stacked goods by an area ratio [100]. An instance segmenter, in
their experiments Mask2Former, produces a mask for each visible piece of the stack. The
area of that mask is divided by the area the object would have presented had it been fully
visible, and the ratios are accumulated into an inventory. On a tobacco-cargo stacking set
they report an inventory accuracy of 93.90 percent. The method is aimed at occlusion
and at dark warehouses, which are both present in a brewery aisle.
    The area ratio is the natural baseline for PLC, and Chapter 3 estimates it under the
same observation model. Its weakness on a regular crate stack is structural. A mask that
covers 0.86 of a face contributes 0.86 to the count only if the missing 0.14 is occlusion of a
present crate. The same 0.14 is missing when the segmenter simply stopped at a shadow.
The ratio then needs a rounding rule, and rounding errors add across a stack of twenty
crates. A lattice does not round a fraction of a cell. It decides that a cell is occupied or
not, using the pitch that the area ratio never consults. The area ratio remains the right
tool when the goods are irregular and no pitch exists. Tobacco bales and mixed cartons
can look like that. A single family of beer crates does not.
    The three-dimensional counter of Dumery and colleagues is the other published answer
to hidden objects [80]. Volume times occupancy is powerful when the stack is a jumble
inside a bin and several camera views can be taken. It is a poor fit when the hidden axis is
an integer depth of identical crates, the camera sees one side during a passage of a second
or two, and the operator already knows, or can read from the pallet pattern, whether the
load is one, two, or three crates deep. PLC takes the integer and refuses the volumetric
reconstruction. The comparison is not that one paper is wrong. The comparison is that
the regularity of the brewery load is information, and a method that ignores it has to
relearn the pitch from pixels on every frame.

1.2.4     Rotated, lightweight detection of logistics boxes
REW-YOLO detects logistics boxes with a lightweight rotated detector [101]. A RepViT-
style block replaces part of the YOLOv8 convolution, an efficient local attention is built
into the feature pyramid, a rotation angle is regressed, and Wise-IoU weights the box loss.
On their BOX-data the model reaches 90.2 percent mean average precision at one half
overlap, with 2.18 million parameters and about 131 frames per second. On the occluded
and rotated subset they report 91.3 percent precision and 86.6 percent recall. The paper
is explicit that YOLOv8 misses boxes whose occlusion exceeds roughly two thirds, because
the edge that the detector needed has disappeared.
    Rotation matters less for the front face of a pallet that was stacked square to the forks,
and it matters immediately if the forklift yaws while it passes the camera. A homography
estimated once per passage absorbs a modest yaw if the face stays planar. It does not
absorb a crate that was loaded askew inside an otherwise square stack. The present design
treats that as a residual error of the lattice snap, visible in Chapter 3 as a center noise of
a few centimetres, rather than as an angle to be regressed on every box. If a deployment
sees large in-plane rotation, the REW-YOLO head is a compatible replacement for the
box branch. It still would not name the color under a changing lamp, and it still would
not multiply by depth. Those are the two holes the present updates are cut to fill.
    REW-YOLO also documents the data augmentation that logistics papers now treat
as mandatory: random rectangular occluders covering 5 to 25 percent of a box, and
perturbations of brightness, contrast, hue, and saturation. The color estimate in this thesis
is not trained on those augmentations of real photographs. It is trained inside a generative
model that applies a random illuminant gain, a shadow, and a strip of a neighboring color.
The augmentation list is evidence that illumination and occlusion are already recognized
as the nuisance variables. The estimate asks how large the illuminant nuisance can grow
before a chromatic prototype loses the type.

1.3      The problem this thesis addresses
1.3.1     The passage into the warehouse
At the end of the packaging line the product is no longer a bottle. It is a crate, and the
crates are no longer on a conveyor. They are stacked on a pallet, the pallet is on the forks
of a truck, and the truck is driving into the warehouse. That passage is the last moment
at which the load is presented, face-on and in motion, to a camera that the building can
own. A count taken later, from the aisle, sees the long side of a row of pallets and a great
deal of occlusion between pallets. A count taken earlier, on the conveyor, sees single crates
and does not see the stacking errors introduced when the pallet is built. The passage is
the right place to close the tally.

drawn on the title page and, with the quantities used later, in Figure 1.2. The front face
is a grid. In the nominal geometry each cell is 0.40 m wide and 0.28 m tall, and each crate
extends 0.30 m in the depth direction, away from the camera. A typical load is two to six

regular spacing of the lips above and below. Mixed colors make the seam easier and the
color decision harder, because each mask is contaminated by a strip of the neighbor when
the boundary is off by a few pixels. The lamp color is not under the designer’s control.
Dock doors contribute daylight. The interior contributes whatever lamp the warehouse
installed. The truck may stop, or it may pass at a walking pace. The system has to
produce a count for that one passage, not a count averaged over a day of well-lit stills.


1.3.2     Formal task
Definition 1.1 (Passage count). A passage supplies a short image sequence I1 , . . . , IF ,
the intrinsics K, a homography H from the image plane onto the front face of the stack,
the nominal face size (w, h), and a depth reading dˆ ∈ {1, 2, 3}. The load contains crates of
classes K. The required output is a count N̂k for each k ∈ K and a total N̂ = k N̂k . A
                                                                                   P




                                                  15
count is exact when N̂k = Nk for every k. A total is exact when N̂ = N .

    The definition separates two accuracies on purpose. A swap of amber and yellow
preserves the total and destroys the inventory. A missed cell destroys both. The estimate
in Chapter 3 measures color accuracy and total-count accuracy in separate trials, because
the generative model draws type labels and geometric misses independently. A deployment
would run them on the same masks. The definition is still the right target: both numbers
are part of the product, and a system that reports only N̂ has not finished the job.
    The depth reading is part of the input, not part of the image. In the building it may
come from the known pallet pattern, from a second, coarse sensor, or from the width of
the load as seen by a top camera that this thesis does not design. The observation model
used later is honest about that reading: it is correct with probability 0.90 and otherwise
off by one. The image system is not allowed to pretend that depth is free.

Assumption 1.1 (Regular front face). Within one passage the front-face pitch is the
nominal (w, h), the face is close enough to a plane that one homography rectifies it, and
a crate center that is detected falls nearer to its own cell than to a neighboring cell after
rectification.

Assumption 1.2 (Stable body color). Each class k has a reflectance prototype. Illumina-
tion, shading, and a limited amount of boundary mixing change the observed color. They
do not change the prototype that the class is supposed to match.

    Both assumptions are design assumptions. They are true of a single family of returnable
crates, stacked by a palletizer, photographed from a fixed gate. They are false of mixed
cardboard of unknown size, and this thesis does not claim that case. Assumption 1.1 is
what allows PLC to snap centers to integers. Assumption 1.2 is what allows ICPH to keep
six prototypes rather than a continuous color regression.


1.3.3     Requirements
The requirements below are the filter applied to every design choice in Chapter 2 and to
the system sketch in Chapter 3.

   1. Type and total. The output is a count per body color and the sum of those counts,
      as in Definition 1.1.

   2. One passage, one answer. The truck does not return. The system may use a
      handful of frames while the face is in view. It may not average over the next truck.

   3. Side view only. No requirement is stated for a top camera or a second side. Depth
      enters as a reading with a stated error rate.



                                             16
  4. Illumination is not controlled. A method that needs a gray card on every pallet,
     or a lamp of known spectrum, fails the requirement. A method that uses a calibration
     of the camera once per shift is acceptable.

  5. The seam is part of the task. Two adjacent crates of the same color must be
     able to become two cells. A single mask over both is a counting error even if the
     pixels of plastic are correctly labeled as crate.

  6. The budget is a line, not a benchmark. The segmenter must leave room for
     a color head and a counting stage inside the time the face is in view. A head that
     adds a few thousand parameters meets that budget. A second large network does
     not, unless a later measurement says there is spare time.

  7. Errors must have a sign that an operator can understand. Under-counting
     hides product. Over-counting creates product. The estimator reports bias, not only
     an absolute error, so that a positive and a negative failure stay distinct.

   Requirement 7 is why Chapter 3 reports signed bias next to every mean absolute error.
A method that misses three crates and invents three others has zero bias and a disastrous
inventory. Absolute error catches it. Bias catches a method that is systematically short,
which is what raw mask counting does on a stack that is three crates deep.


1.3.4    Where an unmodified segmenter fails
An unmodified YOLO-style segmenter, trained to emit masks and a class, fails this passage
in four repeatable ways.
    The first failure is merged seams. The prototype assembly (1.3) is excellent at compact
objects on COCO and weak at a long, low-contrast horizontal edge between two identical
textures. If the coefficients of two stacked crates are similar and non-maximum suppression
keeps one box, the tally is short by one for every such merge. Boundary refinement [72, 73]
and occlusion-aware bilayer heads [91] reduce this failure and do not use the fact that
the lips are periodic. The vertical-feature neck defined below is the baseline’s attempt to
prefer those lips. It is not, by itself, a counter.
    The second failure is color under the wrong lamp. A classifier trained on RGB crops
of crates in one aisle learns L ⊙ R for that aisle’s L. Equation (1.1) says the decision
boundary moves when the truck uses a different door. Global constancy does not put
the boundary back, for the reason in Section 1.1.5. The color error is not uniform across
classes. Amber and yellow share a high red channel and a low blue channel. White sits
near the neutral axis, so a small residual gain pushes it toward whichever chromatic class
the noise favors. Any head that claims to solve type has to be estimated on those pairs,
not only on a mean accuracy.

against the failure it is supposed to repair.


1.3.5     YOLO-vf-seg, and the two updates
YOLO-vf-seg is the baseline this thesis updates. It is a one-stage instance segmenter of the
YOLOv8-seg type: a cross-stage backbone, a feature pyramid, a decoupled detection head,
and a prototype mask assembly of the form (1.3). The letters “vf” name a vertical-feature
gate in the neck. On a side view the signal that separates stacked crates is a set of nearly
horizontal lips, periodic in the vertical image coordinate. The gate pools each feature
map along the horizontal axis, passes the resulting column through a small convolution,
and multiplies the map by a sigmoid of that column, expanded back across the width. In
equations, for a feature map U ∈ RC×H×W ,

    The updated system adds two modules, drawn in Figure 1.1.
    The illumination-invariant chromatic prototype head, ICPH, reads the pixels inside
each mask and the mask-pooled features of the neck. It builds the eight-dimensional
chromatic feature of Chapter 2, standardizes it, and scores six color prototypes with a
linear classifier. The head exists because of the second failure mode above. It is small
enough that requirement 6 is not in doubt: the parameter count estimated in Chapter 3 is
under ten thousand weights on top of a medium segmenter.
    Perspective lattice completion, PLC, reads the mask centers after they have been
mapped by H into metres on the front face. It estimates the origin of the lattice, snaps
each center to an integer cell, fills a hole that has at least two orthogonal neighbors inside
the bounding rectangle of the detections, and multiplies the number of occupied cells by
the depth reading. The module exists because of the third and fourth failure modes. It
is not a network, and it has no training iteration. Its only geometric parameters are the
nominal pitch (w, h), which Assumption 1.1 fixes.
    A third idea was considered and set aside. Training the segmenter with an auxiliary
density loss, so that the integral of a density agrees with the number of masks, repairs
inconsistency between two visible-count estimators. It does not see behind the front face,
and it does not use the pitch. A fourth idea, a second camera above the pallet, would see
the depth directly and would violate requirement 3. Both ideas remain available if the
assumptions fail in a particular building. They are not the contributions.
    The contributions are deliberately unequal in kind. ICPH is a learned head, and
the estimate of it is an estimate of a classifier under a color model. PLC is a geometric
algorithm, and the estimate of it is an estimate of a counter under a detection model.
Putting them in one network and quoting a single average precision would hide the separate
ways they fail. Chapter 2 derives each one. Chapter 3 builds the passage system around
both, and then estimates them separately under models that are written down in full.


1.3.6     What is in scope, and how the thesis is organized
The thesis is a system design and an estimate of that design. It specifies the camera’s
relation to the load, the segmenter, the color head, the lattice, the depth reading, and the
way a passage is reduced to one answer. It does not report a field trial. The numbers in
Chapter 3 are produced by the Monte Carlo instrument simulate_estimation.py under
the observation model written there, with the seed fixed. They are estimates of the two
modules. They are not accuracies measured on a brewery floor, and the text does not call
them that.


                                             19
    Several related problems are named so that they are not silently included. Bottle-level
inspection inside the crate, reading of printed lot codes, detection of a broken crate, and
identity of the forklift are out of scope. So is any claim about a cardboard carton of
unknown size. The lattice pitch is an input. If a second family of crates with a different
pitch shares the aisle, the system needs a pitch hypothesis per family; that extension is
described and not estimated.
    The organization follows the three-chapter limit. This chapter has set out the principles,
the prior systems, and the problem. Chapter 2 derives ICPH and then PLC, with the
losses, the algorithms, and the complexity. Chapter 3 specifies the passage system that
holds the two modules, and then reports the estimate: protocol, tables, the worked passage,
the parameter cost, and the limits of what the estimate can support.
    The standard that the later chapters are written to is the standard of this problem
statement. A module earns its place if it attacks one of the four failures in Figure 1.3 or
the invisible rank behind them, if it respects the side-view and timing requirements, and if
its errors can be shown with a sign. ICPH and PLC are the two modules that meet that
standard. The next chapter writes them down.

at removing the reflectance. Gray-world (p = 1) and shades-of-gray (p = 6) fail in the same
direction. The failure is slightly worse for larger p only because a larger p concentrates
on the brightest pixels, which on a uniform face are the same color as the mean. The
estimate in Chapter 3 is where this proposition becomes a table: both classical corrections
fall to roughly one fifth accuracy, which is little above the one-in-six rate of a guess among
six colors, as soon as the face is close to uniform.

of M belongs to a neighbor, or if a logo has a different reflectance, then ℓ̂ is no longer
parallel to a single L ⊙ R and I′ is no longer neutral. Those violations are exactly the
nuisances of the passage. They are weak signals. A method that needs them in order to
retain any color at all is a method that has thrown away R. ICPH therefore keeps three
views of the same pixels at once: the corrected chromaticity, which is informative when
the mask is contaminated; the opponent coordinates of that chromaticity, which align with
the amber–yellow and red–green confusions; and the chromaticity of ℓ̂ itself, which still
carries L ⊙ R and is the term the classical correction divides out.

2.1.7    How the training distribution is built
Each training face is drawn as follows. A class k is uniform on the six prototypes. An
illuminant gain is drawn as a log-normal vector,

                  g = exp(ξ),       ξc ∼ N (0, s2 ),   g ← g/ mean(g),              (2.18)

with severity s uniform between 0.05 and 0.55 during training. The normalization by the
mean removes a pure exposure change, which shading already models, and keeps the draw
on the illuminant direction. A shadow scalar ρ is uniform on [0.40, 1], and a smooth shade
multiplies the rows of the patch by a line from 0.72 to 1.18. With probability 0.25 the
first 18 percent of the rows are replaced by a different prototype under the same g and ρ,
which is the neighboring-crate strip. Pixel noise of standard deviation 0.012 is added and
negative values are clipped. The feature (2.8) is computed on that patch, and the label is
k.
    Six thousand patches and five hundred full-batch gradient steps with step size 0.35
are the training schedule of the estimate. The step size is large because the features are
standardized and the model is linear; it is not a step size that would be copied onto a
YOLO backbone. The ℓ2 penalty is applied to W on every step. Nothing in this schedule

                                             29
is claimed to be optimal. It is fixed, it is seeded, and the test patches are drawn after
training from the same generative steps at fixed severities that training never isolated.
    The strip of a neighbor is the important augmentation. Without it the classifier can
ignore χ and read the class from η alone, because on a pure face η is the chromaticity of
L ⊙ R. With the strip, η is pulled toward the neighbor and χ carries the disagreement
between the two reflectances. That disagreement is what a real mask boundary looks like.
Training without the strip would overstate accuracy on clean faces and understate the
amber–yellow collision when the boundary is wrong.


2.1.8     Calibration of the margin
The margin γ in (2.12) is not a probability. Softmax outputs of a linear model trained
under a single generative severity are not calibrated to a different severity, and a warehouse
shift is exactly a change of severity. The system therefore treats γ as a rank, not as a
chance. The operating rule, justified by the harsh-severity confusion in Chapter 3, is:

   • if γ is among the lowest tenth of margins seen on a calibration shift, the type is
     marked uncertain;

   • uncertain types are excluded from the automatic per-color tally and listed for the
     operator;

   • the total count from PLC is still reported, because geometry does not depend on γ.

   A calibration shift is a few dozen pallets of known color at the start of a week, not
a gray card. This is the one place the design asks a person to confirm a label. It is also
the place where amber and yellow will concentrate if the lamps in that building are worse
than the harsh setting of the estimate. The rule is a consequence of Proposition 2.1 being
only approximately false: when the residual that ICPH reads becomes small, the honest
output is an abstention, not a forced name.


2.1.9     Cost, interfaces, and the failures that remain
The runtime interface is a mask and a patch of pixels in, and a class plus a margin out.
The training interface is a set of patches drawn as in Section 2.1.7, or, when real images
exist, the same feature computed on masks whose color a person has confirmed. The head
does not modify the mask and does not modify the lattice. Those two statements are
what keep the estimate separable. A color error cannot create a cell, and a gap-fill cannot
change a prototype.
    The failures that the derivation predicts, and that the estimate should be checked
against, are the following.



                                             30
   1. At very small severity the raw cosine (2.15) can beat ICPH, because the correction
      inside χ removes a reflectance signal that the raw matcher was using lawfully. A
      crossover at low severity is not a defect of the table. It is Proposition 2.1 in mild
      form.

   2. Amber and yellow remain the nearest pair. No linear feature built from a three-
      channel mean separates them once the gain noise exceeds their chromatic distance.
      More pixels do not help if the pixels are the same color. A logo-aware neck path

   3. White is near neutral. Any error in ℓ̂ moves η toward a chromatic class, and white

2.1.11      What each block of the feature is worth
Proposition 2.1 says that χ and o go to zero on a pure face, and that η keeps L ⊙ R. The
linear layer might still use the small residuals that shading and the neighbor strip leave
in χ. Whether it does is an empirical question about this feature, and it can be asked
inside the same generator without a new theory. A second run, restarted from the same
seed and the same schedule of six thousand patches and five hundred steps, trains six
classifiers. Each classifier sees a subset of the eight coordinates. The subsets are the three
chromaticity coordinates, the two opponent coordinates, the three illuminant coordinates,
chromaticity with opponent, chromaticity with illuminant, and the full vector. Accuracy
is measured at severities 0.05, 0.30, and 0.60 on 2500 fresh patches each. This run is not
the run behind Table 3.2. Restarting the seed consumes the random stream in a different
order, so the full-feature column here is a replicate, not a transcription.
    Table 2.1 is the practical form of the proposition. Chromaticity alone, the quantity a
corrected matcher would trust, sits near 0.54 at a mild lamp and falls toward 0.49 at a
harsh one. It is well above chance, because the shade ramp and the neighbor strip keep
χ from being exactly neutral, and it is far below the raw matcher, which at low severity
still sees R directly. Opponent coordinates alone do no better. They are a rotation of
χ, and a rotation cannot recover a signal the correction destroyed. Adding opponent to
chromaticity moves the mild-lamp accuracy from 0.542 to 0.550. The opponent block is
not where the accuracy lives.
    The illuminant block alone is a different estimator. At s = 0.05 it reaches 0.864.
On a nearly pure face η is the chromaticity of L ⊙ R, and when L is near white that


                                               32
chromaticity is nearly the chromaticity of R. At s = 0.60 the same block falls to 0.504,
close to chromaticity-only. A strong gain rotates L ⊙ R off the prototype, and η rotates
with it. The illuminant feature is a good feature exactly while the lamp is the nuisance
the raw matcher can also tolerate, and it ceases to be sufficient at the severity ICPH was
introduced to handle.
    The combination of χ and η is the head. At s = 0.05 it reaches 0.976, at s = 0.30
it reaches 0.824, and at s = 0.60 it reaches 0.592. The full eight-dimensional vector
changes those three numbers to 0.974, 0.827, and 0.596. Opponent coordinates are worth
a few thousandths at the harsh end and a slight loss at the mild end, which is what an
almost-redundant linear feature does under an ℓ2 penalty. They are kept in the specified
head because they cost two coordinates and because the amber–yellow axis is an opponent
axis. They are not kept because the ablation shows a large effect. A deployment that
needs to shrink the feature can drop o and expect to lose the margin visible in the last
column, not the gap between ICPH and raw matching.
    The ablation also warns against a story in which “illumination invariance” means
the corrected chromaticity. The invariant-looking coordinates are the weak ones. The
coordinates that carry the class are the ones the classical algorithm divides out, read by a
classifier that has seen many lamps, together with the residual that contamination leaves
in χ. Invariance here is a property of the decision, not of a single normalized image. That
is a weaker slogan and a more accurate description of Table 2.1.

2.2.1     An error budget in crates, before the tables
The tables measure a net error. The net is easier to read if it is split, even approximately,
into the pieces the design can and cannot move. Consider a front face whose true occupancy
is a solid m⋆ cells, depth d, truth N = d m⋆ . Let the detector return m⋆ − q + f centers
that snap into distinct true or false cells, where q is the number of missed true cells and
f is the number of false cells that land outside the true set. The gap rule then adds r
restorations of true misses and p phantom cells. The declared front count is

                                 m = (m⋆ − q + f ) + r + p,                              (2.24)

and the booked total, when the depth reading is correct, is N̂ = d m. The signed front
error is
                   m − m⋆ = −q + f + r + p = −(q − r) + (f + p).                (2.25)

The first parenthesis is unrestored misses. The second is false cells plus phantoms. PLC
exists to make r close to q without making p large. The span, by comparison, sets the
declared count to the area of the bounding rectangle, which sets r = q on a solid rectangle
whose corners were detected, and sets p equal to every truly empty cell inside that rectangle
plus any cell the false positives pushed into the percentile span. On the stepped stack of
Figure 2.6, q may be zero and p is already one for PLC and two for the span, before any
detection noise. That is a structural p, not a random p.
    When the depth reading is off by one, an additional ±m appears in N̂ −N . Section 3.2.13
estimates that term at about one crate of absolute error under the ten percent error rate,
for fronts near ten cells. It does not enter (2.25). A table that shows PLC-single at mean
absolute error 3.01 is therefore a sum of at least three positive contributions: unrestored
misses scaled by depth, phantoms and false cells scaled by depth, and depth-reading
mistakes. Removing any one of them leaves the other two. This is why a three-tenth-of-a-
crate improvement over the span can coexist with a three-crate absolute error, and why
the improvement is still the right comparison. The span pays the same depth-reading
term. The difference between the rows is the difference in (q − r) and (f + p), not the
difference in the sensor.
    Equation (2.25) also fixes the sign of the bias. A rule with r near q and p > 0 on
stepped outlines has positive front bias. A rule with r = 0, which is the raw mask count,
has negative front bias equal to −q + f , and q exceeds f because the detection probabilities
in (3.1) are all below one and the false-center mean is only 0.35. Multiplying by d ≥ 1
preserves the sign. The sign flip between the mask rows and the lattice rows of the
counting table is (2.25) with two different (r, p), written before the table so that the flip is


                                              35
a prediction. Chapter 3 checks it.

20 cm horizontally, so a miss of the snap from noise alone is rare. The errors that move
the count are missed detections and false detections, not the rounding.

               n(r, c) = 1{(r−1,c)∈F } + 1{(r+1,c)∈F } + 1{(r,c−1)∈F } + 1{(r,c+1)∈F } ,

     The threshold of two orthogonal neighbors is the smallest number that distinguishes a
hole in a wall from the end of a wall. One neighbor is an edge: every occupied cell on the
boundary of a solid stack has empty cells beside it with exactly one occupied neighbor, and
filling those would grow the stack by a rind. Two neighbors is a corner of a hole. Three or
four would refuse to fill a hole that sits against the top of the load, which is where clipping
happens. Two is the compromise, and it has a known over-fill: a stack whose true outline
already has a step, such as a top row with only the right-hand crate present, presents the
empty cell beside that crate with an upper or lower neighbor and a lateral neighbor. The
rule fills the step. Section 2.2.11 computes that case on the grid used in the estimate.

2.2.8     Why a union across the passage over-fills
A passage supplies F frames, five in the estimate. The natural attempt to be robust is
to snap every frame, count a vote v(r, c) of how many frames produced cell (r, c), and
complete gaps on the cells whose vote is at least one. Call this PLC-passage. It is the
temporal method that was estimated, and it is not the method the system should run.
    The reason is the interaction of the vote with (2.32). A false center is rare in one frame.
Across a union it is enough that each frame contribute a different false cell, or the same
false cell just outside the true rectangle, for the bounding rectangle to grow and for the
neighbor test to acquire support it did not have. Once a phantom cell is added, immediate
update lets it support a second phantom. The bias has a sign: passage fusion adds cells
and almost never removes them, because a vote threshold of one cannot delete a cell that a
single noisy frame invented. The estimate measures that positive bias. The recommended
counter is PLC on the single sharpest frame of the passage, PLC-single, where sharpness
is the frame with the highest median margin on the color head and, if color was not run,
the frame whose mask areas vary least across the stack.
    A vote threshold of ⌈F/2⌉ would block the union of one-frame false cells. It would also
block a cell that was visible in only the sharpest frame and missed in the others, which is
the usual signature of the bottom row. That trade is not estimated here. It is named so
that the negative passage result is read as a result about the threshold of one, not as a
result about the uselessness of a second frame. The second frame remains the right place
to look when the first frame’s residual of (2.30) is large.

2.2.10     Uncertainty
PLC returns an integer. The uncertainty that should travel with it is not a softmax. Three
quantities are available without a second model.
    The origin residual, the median absolute residual inside (2.30), measures whether the
centers agree on a lattice. A residual above a few centimetres means the snap is not in the
regime of Proposition 2.2.
    The completion count |F \ D| measures how much of the answer was inferred rather
than seen. A passage that completes more than a small fraction of its cells is a passage
whose bottom or top row was not really detected. The total can still be reported. It
should be marked as completed, not as observed.
    The depth bit, a Boolean that is false when the reading is known to be a default rather
than a measurement, distinguishes a count that will be off by a rank if the default is wrong.
The system displays all three next to N̂ . None of them is turned into a confidence interval
in this thesis, because the observation model can compute an interval and a deployment
cannot compute the observation model. The honest displayed uncertainty is the triple

2.2.12     Complexity
Let n be the number of centers in the frame and let R × C be the bounding rectangle, at
most a few dozen cells for the loads in the design range. Origin estimation is a median of
n scalars, snapping is O(n), and completion is O(RC). There is no iteration in the sense
of an optimizer and no parameter in the sense of a weight. The homography is a single


                                               43
matrix-vector product per center. Relative to the segmenter, PLC does not appear in a
FLOPs budget. Relative to the area ratio it is the same order, a pass over the detections,
and it uses a different summary of those detections.
    The implementation consequence is that PLC can be re-run, inside the passage, on
whichever frame the sharpness rule selects, without buffering features or resizing an image.
The system in the next chapter uses that freedom. It keeps five masks only long enough
to choose a frame, then runs Algorithm 2 once.


2.2.13     What PLC does not claim
PLC does not claim to see the rear rank. The factor dˆ is an input. PLC does not claim to
separate two crates that the segmenter merged into one center: a merged seam arrives as
one point, snaps to one cell, and the neighboring cell is filled only if some other detection
supports it. A systematic merge of every seam is not a hole pattern the rule recognizes.
The vertical-feature gate (1.7) remains the baseline’s defense against that systematic merge.
PLC does not claim invariance to the wrong pitch. A face of width 0.30 m snapped on a
pitch of 0.40 m will alias, and Proposition 2.2 will not apply. A second family of crates
requires a second pitch hypothesis and a selection between the two origins by the median
residual. That selection is a small extension of (2.30) and it is not in the estimate.
    The claim that is made is narrower. On a load that satisfies Assumption 1.1, with
centers that mostly fall in the correct cells and a depth reading of the quality in (2.33),
declaring occupancy by a snapped set plus a two-neighbor completion, and multiplying by
the reading, estimates N with a smaller absolute error than counting masks, summing
area ratios, or filling the bounding span. Chapter 3 is the test of that claim under one
observation model. The claim is not a field accuracy. The derivation in this section is
what would have to be revisited if the test had come out the other way.

2.2.17      A bound on a single false center
One false center can do only a limited amount of damage on a single frame, which is the
other half of the argument against the passage union. Suppose the true occupied set is a
solid rectangle and the origin is exact. A false center falls either in an occupied cell, in
which case the snap ignores the duplicate, or in an empty cell. An empty cell adjacent to
the rectangle has at most one orthogonal neighbor inside the rectangle if it shares a side,
and it has none if it only touches a corner. The test n ≥ 2 does not add a side-adjacent
cell on the strength of one false center. The false center occupies a cell by itself, and the
count grows by d,ˆ once. It does not start a rind.
    The passage union removes that limit. Frame 1 may place a false center on the left of
the rectangle and frame 2 a false center above that false cell, or two frames may place
false centers in the same empty side cell and in a second empty cell that becomes its

                                                         47
neighbor through the immediate update. Vote threshold one treats each of those as
support. The single-frame bound, “one false center adds at most one cell,” is false of the
union. The estimated bias of +5.33 crates for PLC-passage against +0.83 for PLC-single
is the bound failing in the direction the mechanism predicts. It is also the reason the
software specification has no vote accumulator. An accumulator with threshold one is not
a harmless extra. It is the counter the estimate rejected.


2.2.18     Why the span is the serious baseline
The mask tally and the area ratio are easy to beat once depth is multiplied in, and a
comparison that stopped there would overstate the lattice. The span is the serious baseline
because it already knows (w, h) and it already refuses to count raw detections. The
three tenths of a crate that separate PLC-single from the span are the entire geometric
contribution of the neighbor test, and they should be described at that size.
    On a solid rectangle whose corner crates were detected, the span’s percentile width
and height recover the true number of rows and columns, and the span books d times
that product. PLC books the same product if every interior hole was restored and no
phantom was added. The two counters differ when the outline is not a solid rectangle,
when a false center stretches a percentile, or when an interior miss is common and the
span, which does not look inside the rectangle, cannot tell a hole from a filled cell. The
stepped top row is the first case. A false center just outside the left column is the second:
the second percentile of x moves, n̂c in (2.36) can increase by one, and the span multiplies
a whole extra column by the number of rows and by d.     ˆ PLC snaps that false center into
one cell. If the cell has fewer than two neighbors it stays a single phantom, not a column.
The neighbor test is a limit on how much one false center can inflate the count, which
is the same limit Section 2.2.17 stated from the other direction, and it is the reason a
method with a slightly higher exact-match rate can still be the one to deploy when the
alternative’s errors come in whole columns.
    None of this makes the span a straw man. On the loads the generator actually draws,
most faces are nearly solid, the span’s mean absolute error is 3.31 against PLC-single’s
3.01, and a deployer who implemented only the span would have captured most of the
gain over mask-times-depth. The thesis still specifies the neighbor test, because the extra
code is a double loop over a few dozen cells, because the bias drops from +1.60 to +0.83,
and because the failure mode of an extra column is worse than the failure mode of a single
phantom cell on a step. The specification would be different if the estimate had gone the
other way. It did not.

System Construction and Estimation

The modules of Chapter 2 are not yet a system. A passage has a camera, a trigger, a
computer, a depth reading, and a person who has to act on a wrong tally. Section 3.1
specifies that system around YOLO-vf-seg, ICPH, and PLC, at the level of geometry, data
flow, calibration, timing, and the operator’s record. Section 3.2 then estimates the two
modules under a Monte Carlo model whose parameters are the same numbers used in the
specification. The estimate is a property of the model. It is the evidence this thesis can
offer without a filmed passage, and the text keeps that limit visible next to every table.


3.1     System construction
3.1.1    The gate
The system is installed at a warehouse door that every finished pallet passes, rather than
on the forklift. A camera on the truck would move with the load and would see the
load from a fixed relative pose, which is attractive, and it would require power, vibration
isolation, and a radio on every truck, which this design refuses. A gate camera sees many
trucks. The price is that the face is in view for a short interval, and that the interval
depends on the driver.
    The nominal installation is a camera whose optical axis is horizontal, at a height of
1.4 m above the floor, aimed perpendicular to the path of the truck at a standoff of about
2.5 m from the plane in which the front face is expected. At that standoff a face of width
1.6 m, four nominal crates, subtends a moderate field of view, and a stack of six crates at
0.28 m reaches 1.68 m above the pallet. A pallet height of roughly 0.15 m puts the top of a
six-high stack near 1.8 m. The camera height of 1.4 m looks slightly down on the lower
rows and slightly up at the top row, which is the perspective the homography is asked
to remove. A camera placed at the top of the stack would see the top faces and would
lose the bottom row to the pallet. A camera placed at fork height would see the forks and
would clip the top. The middle of the design range is the compromise.

                                            51
    The shutter is specified from the truck speed rather than from a frame-rate fashion. At
1.5 m s−1 , a walking pace, a 2 ms exposure moves the load by 3 mm during the exposure.
That smear is small beside the 3.5 cm center noise already admitted in the lattice. At 5 m s−1
the same exposure moves the load by 10 mm, still inside the half-cell of Proposition 2.2,
and a 10 ms exposure would move it by 50 mm, which is a quarter of a cell and no longer
negligible. The specification is therefore an exposure at or below 2 ms, with the gain raised
to compensate, and a frame rate of 15 Hz so that a face which is fully in view for half a
second contributes about seven frames, of which the estimate’s five-frame passage is a
conservative subset. The blur variable in the observation model is the normalized version
of whatever this exposure fails to prevent. It is not a second shutter design.
    Illumination is not redesigned. The gate inherits the dock. What the system specifies
instead is a prohibition and a calibration. The prohibition is against an automatic white
balance in the camera firmware. Firmware white balance is a global gain of the kind
Section 1.1.5 rejected, and it moves from frame to frame as the truck’s painted mast enters
the field. The camera is locked to a fixed gain and a fixed color balance for the shift.
ICPH then sees a stable L inside the passage and a varying L between shifts, which is the
variation the head was built for. The calibration is the weekly set of known-color pallets
used to rank the margin, as in Section 2.1.8.


3.1.2     What is computed, and where
One industrial PC at the gate is sufficient. The segmenter is the heavy step. ICPH and
PLC are not. A medium YOLO-seg model on a current embedded GPU sustains a frame
rate above the 15 Hz of the camera if the input is kept near the model’s training resolution;
the warehouse result cited in Section 1.2.2, 84.8 frames per second for a small variant, is
evidence that the family has margin, not a measurement of this PC. The design therefore
budgets the segmenter at one frame period, the color head at well under a millisecond
for a few dozen masks, and the lattice at well under a millisecond. The budget that can
actually break is the segmenter. If a deployment finds that the medium model misses the
frame period, the specified downgrade is a smaller YOLO-vf-seg variant with the same
two heads, not the removal of PLC. Removing PLC reintroduces the factor-of-depth error,
which is larger than the accuracy difference between a small and a medium backbone.
    The software is a single process with the stages of Figure 3.1. The trigger is a motion

kept frame the segmenter writes masks, boxes, and centers. ICPH writes a class and a
margin per mask. When the trigger closes, the frame with the highest median margin is

3.1.3     The segmenter as a component
YOLO-vf-seg is used as specified in Section 1.3.5. The input is a three-channel image at the
network’s native resolution. The neck applies the vertical-feature gate (1.7) on the three
pyramid levels that feed the head. The detection head emits boxes and objectness. The
mask head emits thirty-two prototypes and a coefficient vector per detection, combined
by (1.3) and cropped to the box. Non-maximum suppression keeps one detection per
crate when the boxes overlap heavily. The suppression threshold is part of the deployment
calibration, not part of the Monte Carlo model. The model represents a miss by a detection
probability that already folds suppression, shadow, and blur into one number. A future
measurement on real frames would have to unfold that number before anyone tuned the
suppression threshold against this thesis’s tables.
    The segmenter is not trained in this thesis. A deployment train would start from a
public YOLO-seg checkpoint trained on COCO [22, 40] and would continue on gate images
labeled for masks and for the six colors. The color labels are the same labels ICPH needs,
so one labeling pass serves both the mask head’s class branch and the chromatic head.
The vertical-feature gate can be trained from the start or folded in after the baseline
converges. Neither schedule is estimated here. What the system specification fixes is the
interface the training must preserve: masks whose first moment is a usable center, and


                                               53
pixels inside the mask that are still the crate’s pixels. A training run that inflates masks
to win a region overlap will hand ICPH the neighbor’s color and will hand PLC a center
that has drifted toward the neighbor. The overlap loss is therefore not the only training
criterion a deployment should watch. The center error in centimetres on the rectified face,
the quantity Proposition 2.2 bounds, is the criterion that matches the lattice.


3.1.4     Calibration
Three calibrations are specified, and they are not optional in the way a software default is
optional.
    The geometric calibration estimates K and H. A planar target of known spacing is held
in the plane of a typical front face, the camera is locked, and H is computed by the direct
linear transform with RANSAC rejection of corners that the detector mismatched [2, 3, 102].
The scale of H is fixed by the target, in metres. After calibration, a person checks that
the residual of a crate-sized rectangle on the target is small beside w/2. This calibration
is repeated when the camera moves. A temperature drift of a fixed camera is ignored until
that residual, checked at the start of a week, grows.
    The pitch calibration is a confirmation, not an estimation. The nominal (w, h) =
(0.40, 0.28) m is checked with a tape on the crate family that the gate will see. If a second
family is present, its pitch is entered as a second hypothesis. The origin estimator (2.30)
then runs twice and the hypothesis with the smaller median absolute residual is kept. The
estimate in Section 3.2 uses one pitch. The second hypothesis is specified so that the
software has a place to put it, and so that nobody “solves” a second family by stretching
H.
    The color calibration is the weekly set of known pallets. It does not refit W . It records
the distribution of margins and sets the abstention threshold at the tenth percentile of
that distribution, as Section 2.1.8 states. Refitting W is a maintenance action, taken when
the abstention rate rises for a week, not a per-passage action. Refitting on the passage
itself would let a single badly lit truck move the prototypes.

3.1.6     The record the operator sees
The record of a passage contains one line per color and one total, plus the diagnostics that
change the meaning of those numbers. The fields are:

   1. time, gate identity, and the frame index that PLC used;

   2. N̂k for each color, computed by (2.42), with uncertain masks listed separately and
      not folded into a color;

   3. the total N̂ = dˆ|F|;

   4. the depth reading and whether it was a default;

   5. the number of completed cells |F \ D|;

   6. the median origin residual, in centimetres;

   7. the minimum color margin on the chosen frame.

    A total without the completed-cell count would hide the difference between a stack
that was seen and a stack that was inferred. A per-color tally without the minimum
margin would hide an amber–yellow swap. The seven fields are the smallest set that
matches the failure modes of Figure 1.3. The display does not show a live mask overlay
as a requirement. An overlay is useful during calibration and is not part of the tally the
warehouse books.
    Fault handling is a list of refusals. The system refuses to book a total when the buffer
is empty, when the origin residual exceeds half a cell, or when dˆ is missing and no shift
default exists. In each refusal the images are kept and the passage is marked for a person.
The system does not refuse merely because a margin is low. It books the total, withholds
the uncertain color, and says so. That split follows from the independence of the two
modules: a bad color is not a bad count.

3.1.7     What the construction deliberately leaves out
No second camera is specified. No truck-mounted computer is specified. No automatic
white balance is permitted. No density branch is added to the segmenter. No multi-frame
union is used in the tally. Each omission corresponds to a result or a requirement already
stated. Listing them here is part of the construction, because a later implementer who
adds a union “for robustness” would be reversing Section 2.2.8 without a new measurement.
The place to revisit any of these omissions is a field trial that measures the corresponding
error, not a default setting in the process.
    The construction is complete enough to implement and incomplete in the way any
unfilmed design is incomplete. It does not name a lens, a cable, or a model of PC. Those are
procurement choices inside the geometric and timing constraints above. The constraints
are the design.


3.2      Estimation
3.2.1     Status of the numbers
Every number in this section comes from one instrument, simulate_estimation.py, run
at a fixed seed, writing estimation_results.json. The instrument implements the
observation models of Sections 3.2.2 and 3.2.3. It does not read an image. It does not
fine-tune a detector. A table below is therefore an estimate of a module under a stated
generative model. Where the model is harsh, the estimate is harsh. Where the model
omits a nuisance, the estimate is silent on that nuisance. The discussion in Section 3.2.9
says which silences matter.
    The instrument has two independent blocks. The color block trains the linear ICPH
of (2.11) and tests it against raw cosine matching, gray-world, and shades-of-gray. The
counting block does not look at color. It drops centers with a position-dependent probability,
snaps the survivors, and scores six counters. Independence is a modeling choice already
used in (2.43). It means the tables must not be multiplied together into a single invented
accuracy.


3.2.2     Observation model for color
A test patch is drawn by the same steps as training, with the severity held fixed so that
a curve against illuminant strength exists. The severity values are 0.05, 0.15, 0.30, 0.45,
and 0.60. At each value, 2500 patches are drawn, the class uniform on the six prototypes,
the gain as in (2.18) with that fixed s, the shadow, the shade ramp, the neighbor strip
with probability 0.25, and the pixel noise. Training used 6000 patches with s random in
[0.05, 0.55] and 500 full-batch steps at learning rate 0.35 and λ = 10−3 . Test patches are


                                             56
not in the training set; they are fresh draws, which for this generator is the relevant notion
of “unseen.”
    The four decision rules are exactly those of Section 2.1.5. Raw matching uses (2.15) on
the uncorrected mean. Gray-world corrects with p = 1 and then matches. Shades-of-gray
corrects with p = 6 and then matches. ICPH computes (2.8), standardizes with the
training moments, and applies the trained W and b. Accuracy is the fraction of patches
whose predicted class equals the class that generated the reflectance, before the illuminant
was applied. A neighbor strip does not change the label. The label is the class of the face
that owns the patch, not the class of the contaminating strip. That choice makes boundary
error count against the classifier, which is the deployment meaning of a messy mask.


3.2.3     Observation model for counting

{1, 2, 3}. Each front cell is occupied with probability 0.93, and the stack is forced to
contain at least one crate. A blur variable β is uniform on [0, 1]. The probability that an

3.2.8     Parameter cost of the head
The cost model of (2.17) is a count of weights, not a profiled runtime. The reference
segmenter in that model is a YOLOv8m-seg scale network, 27.3 million parameters and
on the order of 102 GFLOPs, whose published COCO box and mask figures sit near 50
and 41 mean average precision. Those COCO figures are a reference for the size of the
network. They are not a prediction of gate accuracy. On top of that network the specified
ICPH adds 9760 parameters, a fraction of 3.6 × 10−4 . The counting stage adds none. The
estimate of accuracy and the estimate of cost answer different questions. The cost answer
is that the color head is not the part of the system a deployer would shrink first. The
accuracy answer is that the head is worth having when the illuminant is not mild, and
that it does not resolve amber against yellow at severity 0.60.


3.2.9     How to read the estimate
The estimate supports five statements, and it does not support a sixth.
    It supports the statement that classical per-mask color constancy is the wrong correction
on a nearly uniform crate face. That statement is Proposition 2.1 plus the flat curves near
chance in Figure 3.2. It does not depend on the linear head being optimal.
    It supports the statement that a linear head on (χ, o, η) degrades more slowly than raw
cosine matching as the illuminant severity grows, and that the residual errors concentrate
on amber, yellow, and white. The crossover at low severity is part of the statement, not a
footnote.
    It supports the statement that multiplying a front count by a depth reading is the
largest single reduction in counting error available inside this model. The reduction from
13.55 to 4.87 mean absolute error dominates the later geometric refinements.

                                             62
    It supports the statement that, given depth, a lattice snap with two-neighbor completion
is a small improvement on a span fill and a clear improvement on an area ratio whose
visible fraction averages below one. The improvement is a few tenths of a crate on the
mean, and a reduction of positive bias.
    It supports the statement that unioning five frames at a vote of one increases positive
bias. The recommended system runs PLC on one frame because of this statement, not in
spite of it.
    It does not support a field accuracy, a claim about cardboard of unknown pitch, a claim
about a different depth-sensor error rate, or a claim that the neck path of Section 2.1.6
has been trained. Those would require images, a second pitch, a measured sensor, and a
trained backbone. The instrument does not contain them, and the tables do not imply
them.
    A reader who needs a single comparison should take it from Table 3.4 and Table 3.2
together and keep the conditions attached. At severity 0.30, ICPH names the design
palette correctly on about five patches in six, against about four in five for raw matching
and about one in five for a classical correction. On the counting model, PLC-single is off
by about three crates on a load of about twenty-three, against about five for masks times
depth and about fourteen for masks alone. Both sentences are about the generators in
Sections 3.2.2 and 3.2.3.


3.2.10     What a field trial would have to add
A trial that could replace this estimate would record, at one gate, the images, the depth
reading, a person-confirmed count per color, and the lamp condition in enough variety
that severity has an empirical meaning. It would report the same metrics: signed bias,
exact total, exact per-color count, and abstention rate. It would keep PLC-single and
PLC-passage as separate rows, because the passage result is easy to “improve” by a code
change that the model has already rejected. It would publish the confusion matrix, not
only the mean, because amber and yellow can move the mean by a point while dominating
the inventory complaints.
    Until that trial exists, the system of Section 3.1 is a specification and the tables of
this section are an estimate of its two numerical modules. The specification is already
constrained by the estimate: one frame, not five; a chromatic feature, not a corrected
color; depth as an input, not as a fraction of mask area. That is the sense in which the
estimation chapter closes the design. It does not close the measurement.

3.2.12     From pixels to a booked line
The pieces are scattered through the derivations. One passage, with the numbers of the
worked example, shows the order in which the computer applies them. The trigger opens.
Five frames are buffered. On each frame YOLO-vf-seg emits masks. ICPH scores each
mask. Suppose the first frame has the lowest blur and the highest median margin, and
that its ten true front crates produced nine detections, as in the instrument’s draw. The
nine centers are multiplied by H and snapped. The origin residual is a few centimetres,
inside the half-cell, so the passage is not refused. The gap rule adds the restored miss
and the one phantom cell of Figure 2.6. The depth reading returns 2, which matches the
truth in this particular draw. The total booked is 11 × 2 = 22 against a truth of 20. The
completed-cell count is 2 if both the restored cell and the phantom were absent from the
raw snap, and the record shows that 2. The color head names the nine detected faces.
The two cells that were not detected inherit by (2.42). If one of the nine was an amber
face scored as yellow with a margin below the weekly tenth percentile, that face is listed
as uncertain and is not forced into either color’s tally, while the total stays 22.
    The operator who sees this record can do three different things, and the record is built
so that they stay different. A total of 22 with two completed cells and a small residual is
a geometric inference, to be trusted to the extent the building trusts PLC-single’s bias of
under one crate on average. An uncertain amber is a color question, to be confirmed from
the image or from the pallet ticket, without reopening the total. A depth field that says
“default” rather than “from the pallet record” is a rank question. Collapsing all three into
a red flag would send every stepped stack to a person. Separating them sends the person
the question the model cannot answer.
    The same passage under the counters that were rejected reads as follows. The mask
tally books 9 and is short by 11, because it ignores depth and one miss. Mask times depth
books 18 and is short by 2. The span books 24, the full rectangle times depth, and is long
by 4. PLC-passage, given the instrument’s five detection counts of 9, 10, 10, 10, 10, books
28 and is long by 8. The booked line of the specified system is the least wrong of those
integers on this draw, and it is still wrong by two crates. The worked passage is not a
demonstration that the error has been removed. It is a demonstration that the record
contains the integer and the reason the integer might be high.


3.2.13     Sensitivity to the depth reading
The depth model fixes a ten percent chance of being off by one. The tables do not vary
that chance, and a reader will want the direction of the dependence. Condition on the
front estimate |F| being some integer m, and on the truth N = d m⋆ . When dˆ = d, the
counting error is d(m − m⋆ ), the front error scaled by depth. When dˆ = d + 1, the error
gains an extra m. When dˆ = d − 1, it loses m. At the stated probability 0.10 of a wrong


                                            65
reading, split equally between the two directions and ignoring the clip at the ends of
{1, 2, 3}, the expected contribution of depth noise alone is of order 0.1 m in absolute error
and near zero in bias, because the two directions cancel in the signed mean and add in the
absolute mean. On a front of about ten cells, depth noise contributes on the order of one
crate of mean absolute error. That is a substantial part of PLC-single’s 3.01, and it is a
part no lattice rule can remove.
    Two consequences follow for the building, both outside the Monte Carlo grid and both
readable from the scaling. Halving the depth error rate, from 0.10 to 0.05, is worth on the
order of half a crate of mean absolute error if the front estimate stays as it is. That is
comparable to the entire gap between the span and PLC-single. A better depth source
can therefore matter as much as the geometric refinement this thesis spent a section on.
Improving the front estimate without improving dˆ still matters, because front errors are
multiplied by d, which is why PLC-single’s advantage over the span grows from depth 1
to depth 3 in Table 3.5 even though the rule itself does not see d. The design priority that
follows is: obtain dˆ from the pallet record whenever the record exists, and spend the vision
effort on the front face. Spending the vision effort on inventing dˆ from the side image
would put a high-variance estimate into the factor that multiplies every other error.


3.2.14     What the estimate constrains in the build
The specification in Section 3.1 was written to be consistent with the tables, and the
consistencies are checkable.
    The camera’s white balance is locked because gray-world and shades-of-gray, including
any global gain that behaves like them on a uniform face, destroy the type. The lock is
not a preference for manual color. It is Figure 3.2.
    PLC runs on one frame because the five-frame union at vote one has bias +5.33. A
software default that fused frames would contradict Table 3.4.
    The total is multiplied by a depth reading because the mask tally without that
multiplication has bias −13.53. A vision-only total is the front count.
    The record shows completed cells and the minimum margin because PLC-single’s
remaining error is a small positive bias from cells the rule inferred, and ICPH’s remaining
error is a swap of amber and yellow rather than a uniform sprinkling of mistakes. A record
that showed only N̂ would hide both.
    The color head is not the place the implementation looks for speed, because 9760
parameters do not move the budget in Table 3.1. The segmenter is that place.
    None of these constraints is a field result. Each one is a decision that would be wasteful
to relitigate inside the same observation model. A field trial can overturn any of them.
Until it does, they are the decisions the estimate is for.

3.2.15       Trace from requirement to row of the table
The requirements of Section 1.3.3 were a filter on the design. The estimate is now in hand,
and the filter can be read in the other direction: each requirement is either discharged
by a row of a table, or it is a property of the specification that no table can discharge.
Table 3.6 is that reading. It is the checklist a later implementer can use without re-deriving
the thesis. A requirement marked “specification” is discharged by the absence of a code
path, not by a number. A requirement marked by a table is discharged only inside the
observation model of that table.
    Two rows of Table 3.6 are the ones most likely to be overturned by a real dock, and
they should be named rather than buried. The seam is not estimated. The Monte Carlo
center noise is an average of many ways a center can move, including a merged seam that
slides the moment (2.2) halfway between two crates. If real frames merge seams often
enough that the center falls outside the snap radius, PLC will book one cell where two
crates stand, the completed-cell count will not rise, and the bias formula (2.25) will acquire
a new negative term the present q does not represent, because the miss will be invisible to
a counter that never received two centers. The vertical gate is the specified mitigation. It
is untested. A field trial that labels seams is the trial that can retire or confirm that part


                                              67
of the architecture.
    The illumination row can be overturned in the other direction. If the dock’s lamps, after
the camera gain is locked, stay inside the mild end of Table 3.2, raw matching is the better
head and ICPH’s correction is a small loss. The weekly margin calibration will show this as
a high abstention rate that buys nothing, or as a confusion matrix that is already diagonal
under the raw rule. The specified response is to keep the feature and the linear layer,
because they are cheap, and to lower the influence of the correction only if a refit of W on
that building’s patches puts near-zero weight on the residual coordinates. The ablation
says the weights that matter are the illuminant block and the residual chromaticity. A
refit is allowed to discover that, on this dock, the illuminant block alone is enough. The
refit is not allowed to replace the head by a gray-world division. That alternative is not
close.


3.2.16     What a passage stores
The record of Section 3.1.6 is what a person reads. The computer should store a little
more, because the estimate cannot be replaced by a field trial that did not keep the inputs
of the estimate. For each booked passage the store keeps the chosen frame, the masks
of that frame, the centers in pixels and in metres, the origin residual, the set D before
completion, the set F after completion, the depth reading and its source bit, the per-mask
class and margin, and the seven displayed fields. It does not keep every frame of the buffer
once the sharp frame has been chosen, except when the passage was refused, in which case
the whole buffer is kept. The distinction is deliberate. A refused passage is the data from
which a better trigger or a better shutter will be judged. A booked passage is the data
from which PLC and ICPH will be judged, and the unused frames are the temptation to
reintroduce the union that Table 3.4 rejected.
    Retention is a shift, not a year. The geometric calibration target images are kept until
the next calibration. The weekly color-calibration patches are kept until the next refit
of W , together with the vector W itself, so that a change in amber–yellow recall can be
attributed to the weights or to the lamps. None of this store is a contribution of the thesis.
It is the minimum on which the claim “the tables were replaced by a measurement” can
later be true. A deployment that books N̂ and discards the centers cannot compute the
origin residual after the fact, cannot tell a phantom cell from a detection, and cannot
rebuild Table 3.3 when a customer complains about yellow crates booked as amber.
    The size of the store is small next to the video the camera could have written. One
chosen frame at the working resolution, a few dozen masks as compressed bitmaps, and a
page of numbers, times the number of trucks in a shift, is an ordinary disk. The constraint
is not capacity. The constraint is that the booked integer and the evidence for the integer
stay in the same record, so that the signed error the thesis treats as meaningful can be


                                             68
computed on a Friday from the passages of the week.


3.2.17     What the mean error is, in a shift
A mean absolute error of three crates is an abstract number until it is placed against a
shift. The counting model drew loads whose mean truth was 22.6 crates, so three crates is
about thirteen percent of a typical load and, on the depth-three slice, four crates against a
larger load. Suppose a shift sends forty pallets through the gate, independent in the sense
of the model, and each is booked by PLC-single. The expected total absolute discrepancy
across the shift is about 40 × 3.01 ≈ 120 crates, while the expected signed discrepancy
is about 40 × 0.83 ≈ 33 crates long. The signed number is the one that survives in a
daily reconciliation against the packaging line’s own tally: misses and phantoms that
cancel inside one pallet do not cancel in the sum of absolute errors, and the bias adds. A
warehouse that “checks the system” by comparing the day’s sum to the day’s production
will see a discrepancy near the bias, and will conclude that the system is long by a fraction
of a crate per pallet. That conclusion is the bias column. It is not the mean absolute error,
and it is not a reason to turn the gap rule off. Turning the rule off returns the system to
the mask-times-depth row, whose bias is −4.33 per pallet, about 170 crates short over the
same forty pallets, a reconciliation error several times larger and of the dangerous sign.
    The color errors do not add in the same way. A swap of amber and yellow preserves
the total crates and moves one pallet’s line from one stock-keeping unit to another. Over
forty pallets, at the harsh severity where amber recall is 0.47 and yellow recall is 0.41,
a large minority of those two colors would be booked under the other name if the head
were forced to answer. The abstention rule exists so that this minority is a list of faces
for a person rather than a silent transfer between two bins in the inventory. The shift
arithmetic for color is therefore a count of abstentions, not a count of crates. The store of
Section 3.2.16 keeps the margins so that the tenth-percentile threshold can be recomputed
from the week’s own pallets rather than from the generator’s severity dial. Until that week
exists, the dial and the shift arithmetic above are the scale on which the tables should be
read: a few crates of absolute error per pallet, a bias under one crate and positive, and a
color head that is asked to abstain on the pair whose prototypes sit 9.3◦ apart.
    The same shift makes the abstention rule quantitative. Suppose a pallet shows eight
amber or yellow faces on the front, the harsh recall of those two classes is near one half,
and the other colors are held well enough that the person is not asked about them. Four
faces a shift, times forty pallets, is on the order of a hundred faces a day if every lamp is
as harsh as s = 0.60 and if every face is forced into the confusion rates of Table 3.3. That
is not an operating point the design accepts. It is the upper end of the generator, and it
is the reason the camera gain is locked and the reason the weekly calibration is allowed
to move the abstention threshold. At the mild end of Table 3.2 the same eight faces are


                                             69
named correctly almost all of the time by either ICPH or the raw matcher, and the list for
the person is short. A dock that measures its own severity, by the margin distribution on
the known-color pallets, will know which of those two days it is having. The thesis cannot
know that for a building it has not seen. It can refuse to hide the range.


3.2.18     Summary of the thesis
The passage of a loaded forklift is a counting problem with a known pitch, a small integer
depth, and a color inventory that has to survive the lamp. Published segmenters solve the
visible mask and leave the color and the depth. Published constancy algorithms estimate
a lamp for a mixed scene and erase a uniform face. Published area-ratio and volumetric
counters recover hidden goods without using a crate lattice that the palletizer already
imposed.
    This thesis updates YOLO-vf-seg with two modules sized to those facts. ICPH keeps
reflectance and illuminant as separate coordinates of one per-mask feature and scores
six prototypes, at a cost of 9760 parameters on the specified head. PLC snaps rectified
centers to the crate pitch, fills a hole that has two orthogonal neighbors, and multiplies by
a depth reading that the side image is not allowed to invent. The Monte Carlo estimate
is consistent with the derivations. Classical correction falls to chance on a uniform face.
ICPH holds a modest lead over raw matching once the illuminant leaves the white point,
and it still confuses amber with yellow when the illuminant is harsh. Depth multiplication
removes most of the counting error. The lattice removes a further, smaller share and will
over-fill if it is allowed to union every frame of the passage.
    The system that follows from those results is a gate camera with locked white balance,
a segmenter that produces masks and centers, a color head that is allowed to abstain, and
a lattice that runs once on the sharpest frame. The tally records the total, the colors,
the depth reading, the number of cells that were inferred, and the weakest color margin.
That record is the output the problem statement asked for. The numbers attached to it in
this chapter are estimates under a model that the thesis writes down, and they remain
estimates until a gate is filmed.
